Warm-up: where are you now?

Every other guide in this course is about the product. This one is about you, the person making it. That makes it the guide most people skim, and the one that decides whether the rest of the course sticks. Knowledge you do not practise fades in weeks. A routine you keep for six months turns into skill.

Study it in five sittings, one per Part plus the wrap-up. Do the exercises on your own product; if you do not have one yet, use the capstone in chapter 17 as your product from day one. Three questions first, to see what you already believe about practice.

Diagnostic 1

You have read nine design books this year but your app still looks "off" to you. What is the most likely reason?

Knowing that spacing should be consistent and being able to see a 4px inconsistency are different abilities. The second is perceptual and comes from deliberate, repeated practice with feedback (chapter 11). "Talent" and "tools" are the two stories people tell to avoid that work.
Diagnostic 2

You are adding a "share reading list" feature to your app. How much process do you need?

Process should be proportional to risk and size. A medium feature deserves a written problem and a quick look at alternatives (sketches), but a solo maker gets more from building early than from polished mockups nobody else will read. Skipping all thinking (c) is how features get built three times. See chapter 1.
Diagnostic 3

Which habit grows taste fastest?

Taste is trained perception plus reasons. Saving and scrolling give exposure without analysis. Annotating and copying force you to notice the decisions (chapter 8, chapter 9, chapter 12).

Reflection before you start

Write three lines in your notes: the product you are working on now, the one design skill you feel weakest in, and how many hours a week you can honestly give to practice (not to building). You will use all three in the curriculum chapter.

Part one

The workflow of one

A team's process exists partly to coordinate people. You have nobody to coordinate, so most of that ceremony is waste for you. But some of it exists to coordinate you with your future self: the you who forgets why a decision was made, who falls in love with the first idea, who builds before thinking. These seven chapters keep the parts of process that protect you from yourself, and drop the rest.

1

The solo loop, end to end

Taught by Ryan Singer, with Bill Buxton and Kent Beck

The whole design process for one person is a loop of twelve small steps in four phases (understand, shape, make, learn), and the skill is choosing how much of each step a change deserves.

Textbook design processes are drawn for teams of ten: a researcher hands insights to a designer, who hands mockups to engineers, who hand a build to QA. Each hand-off needs a document, so the process fills with documents. When you are alone, there are no hand-offs between people. There is only one hand-off left, and it is the dangerous one: from you-today to you-in-three-weeks, who has forgotten the reasoning.

So the solo loop keeps every thinking step and cuts most communication artifacts. You still frame the problem before solving it, still look at more than one solution, still test with real people. You just do each step in the smallest form that does its job: a paragraph instead of a deck, a sketch instead of a mockup, a coded prototype instead of a clickable one.

What each step is for, in one line

  1. Idea journal. Capture every problem you notice (yours and users') with the date and the situation. Ideas are cheap; the journal is how you notice which ones keep coming back.
  2. Problem brief. One page: who has the problem, when, what they do today, what "solved" looks like, what you will not do. Forces you to separate the problem from your favourite solution. (Deep dive in Problems Worth Solving.)
  3. Research. Talk to three to five people, read support emails, watch someone do the task today, study how three other products solve it. Hours, not weeks.
  4. Sketches. Many rough options, fast. Their job is to make you compare, so you never ship your first idea by default.
  5. Flows. The sequence of places and actions, drawn as a breadboard or a simple flow diagram, before any screen is drawn properly.
  6. Wireframes. Layout and hierarchy in grey boxes, only for screens whose structure is genuinely uncertain.
  7. Visual. Type, colour, spacing, icons, applied through your tokens and components (see Systems & Platforms).
  8. Prototype. Something that moves, so you can feel timing, gestures and transitions that static pictures hide.
  9. Build. The real thing. For you, often the prototype grows into it.
  10. Test. Watch real people use it, before and after shipping (see Testing & Iteration).
  11. Ship. Release it with a changelog entry that says what changed and why.
  12. Learn. Compare what happened with what you expected in the brief. Write the gap down. It becomes the next entry in the journal.

Process proportional to risk

Not every change runs every step at full size. A typo fix runs the loop in five minutes: notice, fix, ship. A new product runs it over months. The question to ask is: how expensive is it to be wrong here? If a mistake is cheap to undo (a label, a colour), skip ahead to build and learn from real use. If it is expensive (data model, navigation structure, pricing, anything that changes users' habits), spend more time in understand and shape, where changing your mind costs an eraser.

Kent Beck's old programming advice, "make it work, make it right, make it fast", has a design equivalent: make it right (the problem), then make it work (the flow), then make it good (the craft). Solo makers usually invert it. They start with the craft they enjoy (a beautiful header, a custom font) on a flow that has not been questioned, for a problem nobody confirmed.

For the solo creator

Your biggest advantage over a team is that thinking and making happen in one head, so the loop can be very tight: sketch at 10:00, build at 11:00, show a friend at 16:00. Your biggest risk is the same thing. Nobody asks "why are we building this?", so you must ask it on paper, in the brief, before you open the code editor. Ten minutes of writing saves days.

Do

  • Ask "how expensive is it to be wrong?" before choosing how much process to run.
  • Write the problem down before any solution, even as two sentences.
  • Look at three or more options before committing to one.
  • Close every loop with a written "what I learned".

Don't

  • Copy a big-team process (personas, journey maps, hand-off specs) nobody will read.
  • Polish visuals on a flow you have not tested.
  • Skip "learn" because the feature shipped; shipping is the middle, not the end.
  • Run the full process for a label change.

Common mistakes

Big-team theatre

You learned design from courses written for agencies, so you produce personas, journey maps and specs for an audience of one.

Keep an artifact only if future-you will read it. Most solo makers need five documents (next chapter), not twenty.

Opening the editor first

Building feels like progress and writing feels like delay, especially when you are a strong engineer.

Rule: no new feature branch until the brief has a "what solved looks like" line. It takes ten minutes.

Never closing the loop

Once it ships, the next feature is more exciting than checking whether this one worked.

Put a "learn" date in your calendar two weeks after each release, and compare results with the brief.

Check yourself

You want to change your app's navigation from a sidebar to a tab bar. How should you size the process?

Navigation changes are expensive to be wrong about: they break muscle memory, and reverting them breaks it twice. That pushes time into understand and shape. A poll (d) measures stated preference for something people have not used, which is weak evidence.

The five artifacts a solo maker needs

Taught by Michael Nygard (decision records), with Olivier Lacan (Keep a Changelog)

Keep five small living documents (a one-page brief per project, a decision log, a design principles page, a screen inventory and a public changelog) and nothing else until you feel a real need.

Documents in a team are messages between people. Your documents are messages between versions of you. That changes the test for whether a document is worth keeping: will I read this again, and will it change what I do? A persona you wrote once and never opened fails. A decision log you check every time you are tempted to reopen a settled question passes.

The five below each answer a question you will ask yourself repeatedly. They are plain text on purpose. Markdown files in your repo (a /design folder) beat a sprawling workspace in a separate tool, because they live next to the code, are versioned with it, and are searchable from your editor.

1. The one-page brief

One per project or significant feature. Its job is to stop you from solving the wrong problem and to let you measure success later. The "No-gos" line is the most valuable: it is where scope creep is prevented in advance. The "Appetite" line comes from Shape Up: instead of estimating how long something will take, decide how much time it is worth, then design a solution that fits.

# Brief: [feature or product name]          Date: 2026-11-01

Problem       Who struggles, in what situation, doing what?
              (One paragraph. No solution words.)
Evidence      What makes me believe this is real?
              (3 user quotes, support emails, my own diary entries)
Today         What do they do now instead? What does it cost them?
Solved looks  What will be observably different when this works?
like          (A behaviour or number, not "users will love it")
Appetite      How much time is this worth? (e.g. 2 weeks, then stop)
No-gos        What I will deliberately NOT do in this version.
Risks         The 1 to 3 unknowns most likely to sink it.
Check on      Date I will compare results with this page.

2. The decision log

Software architects use Architecture Decision Records, a format Michael Nygard proposed in 2011: short notes recording context, decision and consequences. The design version is even shorter. Its value appears in month four, when you are about to change the reading font back to the one you rejected in month one, and the log tells you why you rejected it. It turns re-litigation into a two-minute read.

3. Design principles

  
Three to five sentences that describe what your product prefers when two good things conflict. "Simple" is not a principle, because nobody argues for complicated. A real principle has a cost: "Reading beats managing: we would rather lose a power feature than add a screen that pulls you out of the text." You use them as tie-breakers in chapter 14.


# Principles for [product]
1. [X] over [Y]: [one sentence on what we give up and why]
2. ...
(Test each one: would a reasonable competitor choose the opposite?
 If not, it is a platitude, not a principle.)


  
4. Screen inventory

  
A list of every screen, sheet, dialog and important state, with its status. Its job is to make the whole product visible at once, so you notice the empty state nobody designed and the settings screen still using last year's styles. A simple table works; a single canvas of thumbnails works better once you have more than twenty screens.

5. The changelog

  
Public, dated, human. Olivier Lacan's "Keep a Changelog" convention groups entries as Added, Changed, Fixed and Removed, newest first. For a product (not a library), write for users: what they can now do, not which files changed. It doubles as your progress record, your marketing and your memory. Linear's public changelog is a well-known example of a changelog that also communicates craft and pace.


## 1.4 · 2026-11-01
Added    Reading streaks you can turn off (Settings › Reading).
Changed  Book covers load 2x faster on slow connections.
Fixed    Long titles no longer overlap the author name.
Removed  The "Popular" tab. Few people used it and it
         pulled attention away from your own library.


  

    
    
  


  
For the solo creator
Budget it: the brief takes 15 minutes, a decision entry 3 minutes, a changelog entry 5 minutes per release. If any document takes longer than that, it has turned into a performance. Write the decision entry at the moment you decide, while the alternatives are still in your head; reconstructing it a week later is twice the work and half as honest.


  

    
Do
  • Keep documents in the repo as plain text.
  • Write decisions at the moment you make them.
  • Record the cost and the "revisit if" for each decision.
  • Write the changelog for users, not for yourself.

    
Don't
  • Create a document because a course for teams said so.
  • Write principles nobody would disagree with.
  • Let the screen inventory drift; update it each release.
  • Hide removals in the changelog; explaining them builds trust.

Sketching for people who "can't draw"

  
Taught by Bill Buxton, with Jake Knapp (Crazy 8s) and Dan Roam

  
A UI sketch is a thinking tool, not a drawing: it needs only eight shapes, it should take under a minute, and its value comes from making many so you can compare them.

  
Bill Buxton's Sketching User Experiences (2007) draws a sharp line between sketches and prototypes. Sketches are quick, cheap, plentiful, ambiguous and disposable; they suggest and explore. Prototypes are more refined and specific; they describe and test. The mistake solo makers make is skipping straight to the second kind, so the first idea becomes the only idea.

  
The mechanism is simple. Your first idea is usually the most obvious one, the one every competitor also had. Ideas two to five are where you start recombining. Ideas six to eight are often strange, and strange ideas contain the fresh pieces. But you only reach idea eight if each idea costs less than a minute. That is why sketches must be ugly: a sketch that takes ten minutes to make is a sketch you will defend instead of throw away.


  

  
Thumbnails before frames

  
Start smaller than you think. A thumbnail the size of a postage stamp (about 4 × 7 cm for a phone screen) can only hold the big decisions: what is at the top, what is the main thing, where is the action. That is exactly the level you should be deciding at first. Use a thick pen (a marker or a 0.8 mm felt-tip), never a fine pencil. The thick line physically prevents detail, which keeps you on structure. Ryan Singer's "fat marker sketches" (next chapter) use the same trick.


  

    
    
  


  
Crazy 8s and 6-8-5

  
Crazy 8s, popularised by Jake Knapp's Google Ventures design sprint (described in his book Sprint, 2016), is the most useful timed sketching drill: fold a sheet of A4 into eight panels and sketch eight variations of one idea, one minute each. A close cousin, often called 6-8-5, asks for six to eight ideas in five minutes. The time pressure is the active ingredient. It stops you polishing and forces you past the obvious.

  
Do them on one specific question, not "the app". Good prompts: "the first screen after install", "how a finished book is celebrated", "how to show reading progress without a number". Use the timer below with a real sheet of paper.

After the eight: pick, then combine

  
Sketching produces options; choosing is a separate step. Put the sheet on the wall, step away for ten minutes, then put a dot on the two or three panels that answer the question best and circle any single good piece in the others (a placement, a label, an interaction). Your next sketch combines the dotted panels with the circled pieces. That combination is usually better than any of the eight.


  
For the solo creator
Keep a stack of A5 paper and one thick pen on your desk, out of the laptop's reach. The switch from screen to paper is itself useful: it moves you from "how do I build it" mode into "what should it be" mode. Photograph each sheet with your phone and drop it into the project folder, named with the date and question. That is your sketch archive, and it costs ten seconds.


  

    
Do
  • Sketch one specific question at a time.
  • Use a thick pen so you cannot add detail.
  • Write real words for headings and buttons.
  • Make at least six options before choosing.

    
Don't
  • Open Figma to "sketch".
  • Erase; cross out and draw again.
  • Judge while generating; sketch first, choose later.
  • Throw sketches away: photograph them.

Breadboards and fat markers

  
Taught by Ryan Singer (Shape Up, Basecamp, 2019)

  
Before you draw screens, draw the flow as words (places, the things you can do there, and where they lead), then sketch only the layout ideas that matter with a marker too fat for detail.

  
In Shape Up, Ryan Singer borrows the word "breadboard" from electronics, where engineers wire a circuit on a board to prove it works before designing the case. A design breadboard has three elements, all written as plain text: places (screens, dialogs, menus you can navigate to), affordances (things you can act on there: buttons, fields, links, and also important pieces of copy) and connection lines (arrows showing where an affordance takes you).

  
Why words? Because a breadboard has no layout, it cannot get stuck on layout. You can see in two minutes that your "add book" flow has five places when it could have two, or that the "duplicate found" case has no exit. These are the expensive mistakes, and visual mockups hide them behind good looks.


  

  
Fat marker sketches

  
When the idea is inherently visual (a calendar, a reading progress graphic, a layout where position matters), breadboards are not enough. Singer's answer is the fat marker sketch: drawn with a pen so broad that adding detail is impossible. It fixes the arrangement of the important elements and leaves everything else open. Its purpose is to set boundaries for the build, not to specify it, so you keep room to discover the details while making.


  

    
    
  


  
Appetite and rabbit holes

  
Shape Up pairs these sketches with two more ideas worth stealing whole. Appetite: decide up front how much time a problem deserves (Shape Up uses small batches of a week or two and big batches of six weeks), and design a solution that fits that budget. This is "fixed time, variable scope", the opposite of estimating. Rabbit holes: before building, list the parts most likely to explode (sync conflicts, an unclear edge case, a technical unknown) and decide in advance how you will avoid them, often by cutting them.


  
For the solo creator
You shape and you build, so the risk is that shaping silently becomes building. Keep them apart in time: shape on Monday morning on paper, write the appetite on the brief, and only then open the editor. If halfway through the build you discover the shape was wrong, stop and reshape on paper for an hour rather than improvising in code for a day.


  

    
Do
  • Breadboard any flow with more than two places.
  • Check every place has an exit and every error a path.
  • Use fat markers only for the visually essential parts.
  • Write an appetite before you start building.

    
Don't
  • Specify pixel details in a shaping sketch.
  • Estimate first and let scope decide the time.
  • Ignore the rabbit holes you can already see.
  • Treat the sketch as a contract; it is a boundary.

Wireframes and prototypes: pick the fidelity that answers the question

  
Taught by Bill Buxton, with Houde & Hill ("What do prototypes prototype?", 1997)

  
Every prototype exists to answer one question; choose the cheapest fidelity that can answer it, and throw it away (or grow it) once it has.

  
Stephanie Houde and Charles Hill's 1997 paper "What do Prototypes Prototype?" sorted prototypes by the question they explore: the role a product plays in someone's life, its look and feel, or its implementation (can it be built, will it perform). Different questions need different prototypes. A clickable Figma file is good at "do people find the path?" and useless at "does this scroll feel smooth with 2,000 books?". A paper sketch is good at "is this the right screen?" and useless at "is this type comfortable for an hour?".

  
Fidelity has several separate dimensions: visual (how real it looks), content (real or fake data), interaction (does it respond) and data (does it handle real volumes and edge cases). You can mix them. A grey wireframe filled with your real library of 300 books is low visual fidelity but high content fidelity, and it will reveal problems that a beautiful mockup with six perfect book covers never will.


  

  
Wireframing fast

  
Wireframes earn their place only when layout is genuinely uncertain: a dense dashboard, a settings screen with 30 options, a first-run screen that must explain a lot. For everything else, go from sketch to code. When you do wireframe, use three greys (text, rule, fill), one real typeface at real sizes, real content, and no colour. Colour invites aesthetic debate before structure is settled. Time-box each screen to 20 minutes.

Designing in code: the design-engineer workflow

  
Taught by Loren Brichter and Silvio Rizzi, with the design-engineer roles at Vercel and Linear

  
If you can build, the material is the best design tool: design the hard 20% on paper, then shape the rest directly in code, with a playground that shows every state of every component at once and reloads in under a second.

  
Loren Brichter designed and built Tweetie himself and introduced pull-to-refresh in Tweetie 2 (2009), an interaction that is hard to imagine coming from a static mockup: it is about how a list feels when you pull past its top. Silvio Rizzi has designed and built Reeder alone for over a decade. Both work as designer-engineers. Their advantage is not speed; it is that they can feel the product while shaping it, and adjust the parts mockups cannot show: timing, scrolling, loading, real text, edge cases.

  
In recent years companies such as Vercel and Linear have hired for this hybrid role and called it "design engineer": someone who designs at the level of the shipped interface, in code, with taste. For a solo maker it is not a job title. It is simply how you work, and the goal is to do it on purpose rather than by accident.

    2. 
    
  2. A component playground. One page (or preview) that renders every component in every state side by side: default, hover, pressed, focus, disabled, loading, empty, error, long text, right-to-left, dark mode. Storybook is the full version; "Storybook-lite" is a single /playground route in your own app. Ladle is a lighter Storybook-compatible option for React.
    3. 
    
  3. Tokens as the only source of values. Every colour, size, radius and duration comes from a named token, so tuning a value in one place updates the whole product (see Systems & Platforms).
    4.

What taste is, and the Gap

  
Taught by Ira Glass, with Kathy Sierra and Paul Graham

  
Taste is the trained ability to perceive quality and explain why; it develops faster than skill, so for a long time you will see that your work is not good enough, and that gap is the sign you are on the right path.

  
In a widely shared 2009 interview about storytelling, Ira Glass, the host of This American Life, described what he wished someone had told him as a beginner. Paraphrased: people get into creative work because they have good taste. For the first few years, what they make is not as good as what their taste tells them it should be. That gap is painful, many quit inside it, and the only way through is a large volume of work, made on deadlines, so that skill slowly catches up with taste.

  
Two things follow for you. First, the discomfort you feel looking at your own app next to Things or iA Writer is not evidence of lack of talent; it is evidence that your taste works. Second, the way out is volume and practice, not more consumption alone.


  

  
Taste is perception plus reasons

  
Kathy Sierra, in Badass: Making Users Awesome (2015), describes how experts in many fields develop "perceptual knowledge": they can tell good from bad quickly, often before they can explain it, after high-quantity exposure to high-quality examples with feedback. Her example is chicken sexers, who learn to classify chicks by being corrected over many examples, not by memorising rules. The design equivalent: look at many excellent screens, compare them with mediocre ones, and get feedback on your judgements.

  
Paul Graham's essay "Taste for Makers" (2002) argues the other half: taste is not merely personal preference, because some things really are better, and the qualities of good design (simplicity, timelessness, solving the right problem, suggestiveness) can be discussed. So training taste has two parts: seeing (perceptual exposure) and saying (explaining why). Seeing without saying gives you vague feelings you cannot act on. Saying without seeing gives you rules you apply without judgement.


  

    
    
  


  
Four ways to grow taste, in order of power

  
    
    
  1. Consume great work daily, briefly. Ten minutes with excellent products, books, type and objects. Exposure calibrates what "good" looks like.
    2. 
    
  2. Analyse: ask "why" five levels deep. Why is this list calm? No separators. Why does that work? Whitespace groups. Why is it enough? Rows are tall. Why can they be? Few items per day by design. Now you have learned something about Things' philosophy, not its pixels.
    3. 
    
  3. Copy exactly. Recreate a great screen pixel for pixel (chapter 12). Copying forces you to measure what you only glanced at.
    4. 
    
  4. Make and compare. Make your version, place it next to the best example, and list every difference. This is where perception becomes skill.
    5. 
  


  
For the solo creator
Nobody around you will say "the spacing is off". So build your own comparison habit: every Friday, put a screenshot of your main screen next to a screen from a product you admire, same size, side by side, and write three differences. Over months this does what a design lead's critique would do.


  

    
Do
  • Study a few great things deeply rather than many things briefly.
  • Put words to every judgement.
  • Study outside software: books, signage, furniture, film titles.
  • Keep old work so you can see your taste moving.

    
Don't
  • Confuse taste with trendiness.
  • Stop making because your work looks worse than your heroes'.
  • Only study apps in your own category.
  • Treat "I like it" as an argument.

  


  
In the wild

    A task manager often cited for restraint: a quiet palette, a single accent and careful motion. A great study object because each decision is small and visible.
    •

    •

Studying an app like a designer

  
A protocol, drawing on heuristic evaluation (Jakob Nielsen) and the breakdown guides in this course

  
Study an app in five deliberate passes (purpose, structure, screens, details, time), each with its own question, and write as you go; casual use teaches you almost nothing because good design is designed to be invisible.

  
Good design disappears in use. That is its job, and it is why using a great app daily does not, by itself, teach you why it is great. You need to break the spell on purpose: slow down, change your viewing distance, and ask a different question on each pass. Doctors call this a systematic examination, and the reason is the same: an unstructured look finds the obvious and misses the rest.


  

  
The protocol

  
    
    
  1. Purpose (15 min). Read the app's site and App Store page. Write: who is it for, what job does it do, and what does it obviously refuse to do? The refusals are the strategy.
    2. 
    
  2. Structure (20 min). Map every place as a breadboard (chapter 4). Count the top-level destinations, the depth, and where settings live. Name the navigation model.
    3. 
    
  3. Screens (30 min). Screenshot the three most important screens. On each, measure: margins, the spacing scale, the type sizes and weights, how many colours and what each colour means. Squint at it to see the hierarchy.
    4. 
    
  4. Details (30 min). Hunt for empty, loading, error, offline and very long content. Read every label and message aloud. Try to break it: rotate, enlarge text, switch to dark mode, turn on VoiceOver.
    5. 
    
  5. Time (a week, 2 minutes a day). Use it for real. Note what delights on day 1 and irritates on day 7, how notifications behave, how fast it opens, what you stop noticing.
    6. 
  

  
Finish with a one-paragraph verdict: the product's single strongest idea, its biggest weakness, and one thing to steal. The breakdown guides (Breakdowns: Software and Breakdowns: Reading) are worked examples of this protocol; read one before doing your own.


  

    
    
  


  
For the solo creator
Study one app a fortnight, alternating between apps in your category and apps far outside it (a camera app, a banking app, a game's menus). Keep each study in your swipe-file folder. After six months you will have twelve studies, which is a better design education than most courses.


  

    
Do
  • Measure, do not estimate: zoom screenshots and count pixels.
  • Hunt for rare states on purpose.
  • Note what the product refuses to do.
  • End with one thing to steal.

    
Don't
  • Study only the first screen.
  • Confuse your own preference with the user's need.
  • Skip the "time" pass; many qualities only appear after days.
  • Write a review instead of an analysis.

Deliberate practice

  
Taught by Anders Ericsson (with Robert Pool, Peak, 2016)

  
Skill grows from practice that has a specific goal, full attention, immediate feedback and a stretch just beyond your current ability; repeating what you already do comfortably, even for years, makes you no better.

  
Anders Ericsson spent his career studying how experts become expert. His 1993 study with Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer of violinists at a Berlin music academy found that the best students had accumulated far more hours of solitary, deliberate practice. That finding was later popularised (and oversimplified) as the "10,000-hour rule", a framing Ericsson himself criticised: hours alone do not make expertise; the kind of hours does.

  
In Peak he distinguishes naive practice (doing the activity again and again) from purposeful practice, which has four features: specific goals, focus, feedback, and getting out of your comfort zone. Deliberate practice adds a teacher and a well-understood path of skills. As a solo designer you rarely have a teacher, so you replace them with reference examples and structured self-comparison.

  
The mechanism Ericsson emphasises is mental representations: detailed internal models of what good looks like and how it is made. An expert typographer looking at a page sees rhythm, measure and colour where a novice sees "text". Deliberate practice builds those representations, and the representations are what let you design faster and better without thinking about rules.

Myth
"Practice makes perfect." Repetition without feedback makes permanent, not perfect: you get faster at your current mistakes. And "10,000 hours" was never a rule in Ericsson's work; the number was an average for one group, and he stressed that the quality of practice matters far more than the count.


  
For the solo creator
Separate practice from production. Production is your app, where you must ship and cannot take risks. Practice is 30 minutes a day on drills where failure is free. Mixing them makes both worse: you take no risks on the app, and you never practise anything that is not urgent. Put practice first in the day, before you open the codebase.

  • Write the goal of each session as one testable sentence.
  • Practise your weakest sub-skill, not your favourite.
  • Use references as feedback, side by side.
  • Repeat the same drill with one variable changed.

    
Don't
  • Count hours as progress.
  • Practise only on your production app.
  • Fix ten things at once.
  • Stay where it feels easy.

Design katas: daily drills, copywork and the weekly redesign

  
Drawing on Anders Ericsson, Benjamin Franklin's copying method, and the code kata tradition (Dave Thomas)

  
Practise in small, repeatable, timed drills with a clear output (katas), copy great work exactly to learn its measurements, and once a week redesign one real screen from start to finish.

  
In martial arts a kata is a set form practised many times until it becomes automatic. Programmers borrowed the idea (Dave Thomas popularised "code katas" in the 2000s) for small exercises done for practice, not production. Design katas work the same way: thirty minutes, one skill, one output, done often. Their power is the volume. Thirty minutes a day, five days a week for 24 weeks is 60 hours of focused practice, more than most designers do deliberately in years.


  
Three practice formats

  

    
30 minutes, every weekday, first thing. Pick a drill from the list below (or draw one with the generator), set a timer, make the output, compare it with a reference, write one line about the gap. Rotate skills across the week, weighting your weakest. Keep every output in one dated folder; the folder becomes your progress record.

    
Recreate a great UI exactly. Take a screenshot of a screen you admire, put it in your design tool or a blank HTML file, and rebuild it on top at 50% opacity until they match: margins, type sizes, line heights, colours, radii, icon weights. Then switch the overlay off and write down every value. Like Franklin rewriting Spectator essays, the point is the comparison: you discover that the "simple" screen uses a 20 px margin, a 17/24 body and three greys you would never have guessed. Do it once a week. Never publish copywork as your own.

    
One real screen, start to finish, once a week (2 to 3 hours). Monday: pick a screen from an app you use (or your own). Write its job in one sentence. Tuesday: study it (passes 3 and 4 of the protocol). Wednesday: Crazy 8s on the same job. Thursday: build the best direction in code or your tool. Friday: put it side by side with the original and write what is better, what is worse and why. Over 24 weeks that is 24 redesigns, a real portfolio of thinking.

    
Constraints force invention. Add one to any kata: one typeface only; greyscale only; no lines or borders; one font size plus weight; 320 px wide; no icons; only system components; maximum five words per label; keyboard only; must work at 200% text. Constraints remove your default moves, which is exactly what makes you find new ones.

    13
    Getting feedback as a soloist
    
  
    Taught by Ed Catmull (the Pixar Braintrust), with Steve Krug
    
  
    You need a small, deliberate feedback system: users for whether it works, peers for craft, the occasional paid expert for blind spots, and a method for sorting what you hear, because the most useful critique is specific, asked for, and about the work's goal.
    
  
    Working alone, the absence of feedback is the default state. Months can pass in which nobody but you looks closely at your screens. Worse, the feedback that does arrive is random: a friend's "looks nice", a stranger's "I hate the font", an app review complaining about a feature you never built. Random feedback is either flattering or noisy, and neither helps.
    
  
    Ed Catmull's Creativity, Inc. (2014) describes Pixar's Braintrust: experienced filmmakers who watch a film in progress and give candid notes, with one important rule. The Braintrust has no authority. The director decides what to do with the notes. That is your model: gather candid input from people who know the craft, then you decide, and log the decision.
    

  

  
    Where to find people
    
  
      
    
    • Peers for critique swaps. One or two other solo makers or designers at a similar level. Swap 30 minutes of focused critique every fortnight. This is the most valuable relationship in this chapter.
      • 
    
    • Communities. Design circles on Bluesky, Mastodon and X; subreddits such as r/UI_Design and r/userexperience; Discord and Slack communities around tools and indie making. Useful for quick reactions and for finding swap partners, weaker for deep critique.
      • 
    
    • Dribbble and showcase sites. Good for visual inspiration and reach; limited for product critique, because shots are judged in isolation, without the user's goal, flow or data.
      • 
    
    • Mentorship platforms. Sites such as ADPList connect you with experienced designers for free or paid calls. Arrive with one specific question and screens ready.
      • 
    
    • Paid expert reviews. Once or twice a year, pay an experienced product designer for a heuristic review or a one-hour critique. Brief them with your principles and goals so they judge the product you are making, not the one they would make.
      • 
    
    • Users. The final critics. Watch them, do not just ask them (see Testing & Iteration).

    14
    Deciding alone, and knowing when to stop
    
  
    Drawing on C. Northcote Parkinson, Paul Saffo, Jeff Bezos's two types of decisions, and Douglas Hofstadter
    
  
    Decide quickly when a choice is reversible and slowly when it is not, use your principles as tie-breakers, time-box everything, and stop polishing when another hour would change something no user will notice.
    
  
    With nobody to disagree with you, you face two opposite failure modes. One is drift: you reopen settled questions every week because nothing marks them as settled. The other is stubbornness: nobody challenges your first idea, so it survives by default. The tools below fix both.
    

  
    Reversible and irreversible
    
  
    In his 2015 letter to shareholders, Jeff Bezos described two types of decisions: Type 1 decisions are consequential and nearly irreversible ("one-way doors") and deserve slow care; Type 2 decisions are reversible ("two-way doors") and should be made quickly by individuals or small groups. Most design decisions are two-way doors: a colour, a label, an icon, a spacing value. A few are one-way doors for practical purposes: your data model, URL structure, pricing model, navigation paradigm, the product's name. Spend your deliberation on those.
    

  

  
    Principles as tie-breakers
    
  
    When two options are both reasonable, do not ask "which do I like?" Ask "which does principle 1 prefer?" Your principles (chapter 2) exist exactly for this moment. If neither principle decides it, the choice probably does not matter much: pick one, log it, move on. If the principles conflict, you have discovered that their order matters; number them.
    

  
    Strong opinions, weakly held
    
  
    The futurist Paul Saffo described a forecasting habit he called "strong opinions, weakly held": reach a firm, specific conclusion quickly so you can act and test it, but abandon it readily when evidence contradicts it. For a solo designer this is the antidote to both failure modes. Commit firmly enough to build and ship. Write the "revisit if" line in your decision log so you know in advance what evidence would change your mind.
    

  
    Bikeshedding and the time-box
    
  
    C. Northcote Parkinson's "law of triviality" (in Parkinson's Law, 1957) observed that a committee will spend little time approving a nuclear reactor, which few understand, and far longer debating a bicycle shed, which everyone feels qualified to judge. Hence "bikeshedding". Alone, you bikeshed with yourself: three evenings on icon corner radii while the onboarding flow stays unexamined. The fix is a time-box written before you start: "Icon style: 45 minutes, then ship whatever is best." Remember Hofstadter's law, from Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979): it always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's law. Time-boxes protect you from that too.
    

  
    When to stop polishing
    
  
  
    A practical stop test, three questions: Would a user notice the difference? (If you need to zoom to 400% to see it, no.) Does it break a principle or a system rule? (If yes, fix it; consistency matters.) Is there a screen with a bigger problem? (There almost always is; the screen inventory tells you which.)
    

  
    For the solo creator
    Write down your decision deadline with the decision itself: "Choose the reading font by Thursday 18:00." Deadlines you make for yourself are weak, so make them visible (a sticky note, a calendar event) and attach a default: "If undecided by then, ship Source Serif." A default turns indecision into a decision automatically.
    

  
    
    
    Do
    • Classify each decision as one-way or two-way.
    • Time-box and set a default before you start.
    • Break ties with numbered principles.
    • Log the decision with a "revisit if".
    
    
    Don't
    • Reopen logged decisions without new evidence.
    • Polish one screen while others are broken.
    • Spend days on two-way doors.
    • Decide one-way doors in a hurry at midnight.

    15
    Energy and sustainability
    
  
    Drawing on Cal Newport (Slow Productivity, 2024), Paul Jarvis (Company of One, 2019) and Christina Maslach's burnout research
    
  
    Your capacity is the real constraint on your product, so design the product to fit it: budget hours for maintenance and support before features, scope to what you can sustain for years, and work in seasons rather than permanent sprints.
    
  
    A team product survives one person's bad month. A solo product does not. If you burn out, the product stops: no fixes, no replies, no updates, and users notice within weeks. So sustainability is not self-care advice tacked onto a design course; it is a design constraint, as real as screen size. Every feature you add is a permanent maintenance cost paid in your hours.
    
  
    Christina Maslach's research describes burnout in three dimensions: exhaustion, cynicism (detachment from the work and its users), and a reduced sense of effectiveness. The World Health Organization's ICD-11 describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. For solo makers the early sign is usually cynicism: support emails start to feel like attacks and you stop opening the app for fun.
    

  

  
    Scope to capacity
    
  
    Before adding a feature, estimate its lifetime cost, not its build cost. A sync feature might take three weeks to build and then two hours a week forever in conflicts, edge cases and support. A setting doubles the states you test. A new platform (Android, web, Windows) can nearly double maintenance. Paul Jarvis's Company of One argues for questioning growth itself: a business can deliberately stay small and still succeed. For a solo product, "smaller and excellent" is a strategy, not a compromise. It is also the calm-product philosophy of Honest & Calm applied to your own work.
    

  
    Seasons of building
    
  
    Cal Newport's Slow Productivity (2024) proposes three principles: do fewer things, work at a natural pace, and obsess over quality. "Natural pace" includes seasonality: periods of intense work followed by lighter ones. Plan your year in seasons rather than a permanent sprint.

    The capstone: design and ship a small real product
    
  
    The whole course, applied
    
  
    Over the 24 weeks, design and ship one small, real, calm product for real users (one job, three to five screens, done excellently), so that every guide's ideas meet a real constraint and you finish with something people use.

    Portfolio and case studies for a solo maker
    
  
    Drawing on Austin Kleon (Show Your Work!, 2014) and makers who publish their process
    
  
    For a solo maker the shipped product is the portfolio; a case study adds the one thing the product cannot show: the problem, the options you rejected, the evidence, and what you would do differently.
    
  
    A designer at a company shows mockups because the product belongs to someone else. You own the whole thing, so you can show something more convincing: a live product with real users, plus the reasoning behind it. Anyone evaluating you (a client, an employer, a collaborator, a future user) is really asking two questions: can this person make good things? (the product answers) and can they think? (the case study answers).
    
  
    Austin Kleon's Show Your Work! argues for sharing process as you go, not only finished results. For a solo maker that habit compounds: your changelog, a short post per milestone and your decision log are already 80% of a case study by the end.
    

  

  
    
    
    
  
    

  
    A case study template
    

    # [Outcome-shaped title, e.g. "Logging a finished book in 6 seconds"]
Role: everything (solo) · Timeline: [weeks] · Platform: [..] · Link: [live]

1. Problem       Who, what situation, evidence (quotes, numbers).
2. Constraints   Time, being one person, platform, what I chose not to do.

    Show process honestly, which means including what went wrong. A case study where every decision was right reads as fiction.     Do
    • Link to the live product first.
    • Title case studies by outcome, not by app name.
    • Show rejected options and the reasons.
    • Use real numbers or say you have none.
    
    
    Don't
    • Inflate results or invent metrics.
    • Show only final, tilted mockups.
    • Write 6,000 words nobody finishes.
    • Hide the fact that you worked alone; it is a strength.

    Skills self-assessment
    
  
    Levels after the Dreyfus model of skill acquisition (Stuart and Hubert Dreyfus, 1980)
    
  
    Rate yourself honestly on twelve competencies at four levels, three times over the curriculum (weeks 1, 12 and 24), and spend most of your practice on the two lowest; the shape of the chart matters more than its size.
    
  
    Stuart and Hubert Dreyfus described skill acquisition as a series of stages, from the novice who follows context-free rules, through the advanced beginner who starts to recognise situations, the competent performer who plans deliberately and feels responsible for outcomes, to the proficient performer who sees what matters intuitively (and, beyond, the expert). The four levels below adapt the first four stages to product design. Expert is left off on purpose: it takes years, and a self-rating of "expert" is usually a sign of the opposite.
    
  
    A self-assessment is only useful if it is honest and specific. Rate yourself by evidence, not feeling: "Competent in typography" should mean you can point to a screen where measure, scale and leading were deliberate. When unsure between two levels, choose the lower. Then ask your critique partner to rate you too; the differences are the interesting part.
