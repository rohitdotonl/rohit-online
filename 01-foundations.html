Diagnostic: what do you already believe?

Three quick questions. Do not look anything up. Getting them wrong is useful: it shows you which chapters to read slowly.

Diagnostic 1

People keep tapping the title of a card in your app, expecting it to open. Only the small arrow on the right opens it. What is the most accurate description of the problem?

The card looks like one object, so people expect the whole thing to act. That is a mismatch between what the design signals and what it does. A bigger arrow treats a symptom. The fix is to make the whole card the target, or to stop it looking like one tappable object. Chapter 4 covers this.
Diagnostic 2

You are choosing between two layouts for a settings page and you cannot decide which looks better. What is the best next step?

When a surface decision feels impossible, the problem usually lives on a lower plane: you have not decided what the page is for. Decide the structure and the purpose first, and the surface choice often makes itself. Chapter 2 calls this "going down a plane".
Diagnostic 3

A user deletes a project by accident because the Delete button sits right next to Duplicate. What kind of error is this, and what is the strongest fix?

The user had the right goal and the wrong action came out: a slip. Slips are best fixed by design (distance, grouping) and forgiveness (undo). Confirmation dialogs on everything train people to click "OK" without reading. Chapter 8 goes deep on slips and mistakes.
Part one

What design is

Before principles and processes, you need a clear picture of the job itself. These three chapters define product design, give you a model of the layers every product is built from, and reframe design as what it really is: a long chain of decisions made under constraints.

1

What product design is

Taught by Don Norman, with Steve Jobs and Charles Eames

Product design is deciding what a product should do, for whom, and how it should behave and look, so that a person can reach their goal with as little friction and as much confidence as possible.

The word "design" gets used for very different jobs. A graphic designer arranges type and images to communicate. A UI designer draws the screens. An interaction designer decides what happens when you press, drag or wait. A UX designer looks at the whole experience across time. A product manager decides which problems to solve and in what order. An engineer makes it real and keeps it working.

A product designer works across all of those boundaries, with one question at the centre: what should this product be, so that it is worth using? In a large company those jobs belong to different people. In your company of one, they all belong to you. That is the most important fact about your situation, and it is good news. The most common failure in big teams is the hand-off: a decision made in one discipline gets lost or distorted in the next. You do not have hand-offs. You can let a database decision shape the interface, or let an interface sketch kill a feature before you build it.

Design is not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works.Steve Jobs, interviewed by Rob Walker, The New York Times Magazine, 2003

That line gets quoted to justify everything, so read it carefully. Jobs was rejecting the idea that design is a coat of paint applied at the end. The look and feel matter, but they are the visible part of something larger: how the product behaves, what it lets you do, what it refuses to let you do. The iPod's scroll wheel was a visual decision, an interaction decision and an engineering decision at once. Separating them would have produced a worse product.

Three tests for "is this good design?"

You will meet many principles in this guide. Under all of them sit three plain tests that apply to every decision:

  1. Does it help someone do something they actually want to do? (Usefulness. A beautiful, easy feature nobody needs is still waste.)
  2. Can they do it without help, without fear, and without wasted effort? (Usability. Find it, understand it, do it, know it worked, recover if it did not.)
  3. Does it feel right, so they want to come back? (Desirability. Calm, trustworthy, pleasant, even delightful.)

The order matters. Usefulness first, because nothing rescues a useless product. Usability second, because nothing rescues a product people cannot operate. Desirability third, not because it is unimportant, but because it grows best on the first two. You will meet the ancient version of this triad in chapter 10.

Key idea

The screens are the record of the design, not the design. The design is the set of decisions.

For the solo creator

Write your decisions down. A plain text file called decisions.md beside your code, one line per decision with the date and the reason ("2026-03-04: no folders, tags only, because users have under 200 items"). It is the cheapest design tool there is, it stops you re-arguing settled questions with yourself at midnight, and it becomes the raw material for your changelog and your landing page.

Do

  • Treat every feature request as a design question: whose goal, which problem, what is the smallest thing that solves it?
  • Let technical constraints into design early; they are information, not obstacles.
  • Judge design work by what people can now do, not by how the screen looks in a screenshot.
  • Learn enough of each neighbouring discipline to know when you are wearing its hat.

Don't

  • Think of design as the last step before launch.
  • Confuse "designed" with "decorated": gradients and shadows are not design decisions about the product.
  • Copy the job titles of large companies. You need the decisions, not the org chart.
  • Let "I'm an engineer, not a designer" excuse a confusing flow. You designed it either way.

In the wild

  • iPodApple, 2001
    The scroll wheel was one decision that solved a product problem (navigating a thousand songs on a tiny screen), an interaction problem (fast, physical, proportional scrolling) and a visual problem (a clean front with almost nothing on it) at once. The best design decisions dissolve the boundaries between disciplines.
  • Dropbox2008
    Instead of designing a file-sync interface, Dropbox made the interface a normal folder on your computer. Most of the "design" is a decision about what not to build. Sometimes the best interface is one the user already knows.
  • Gmail Undo SendGoogle, Labs feature 2009, default 2015
    Email cannot be unsent once delivered. Gmail holds the message for a few seconds before it really goes, which makes "undo" possible. This is an engineering decision made for a design reason. Behavior is design, even when it is invisible.

    A writing app whose main design decisions are things it leaves out: few formatting options, few settings, one carefully chosen typeface per mode. For focused tools, the feature list is the design.

Common mistakes

Starting in Figma

Drawing screens feels like progress, so it is tempting to open a canvas before you know what the screen is for.

Start with a sentence: "This screen lets [person] [do thing] so that [outcome]." If you cannot write it, you are not ready to draw it.

Thinking design is someone else's job

Solo developers often say "I'll hire a designer later", then make dozens of design decisions by default in the meantime.

Accept that every choice of default, label, order and error message is a design decision. Make them on purpose.

Treating taste as the whole job

Visual polish is the most visible part of design, so it gets most of the attention.

Spend your design time in proportion to impact: what the product does first, how it behaves second, how it looks third.

Check yourself

A friend says, "My app is well designed; I paid for a premium UI kit." Users still churn after a day because they cannot work out how to import their data. Which statement is most accurate?

A UI kit only gives you the look of components. Whether people can bring their data in and get value on day one is a product design question about flows and behavior. Emails might help, but they patch a gap that design should close.

The five planes of a product

Taught by Jesse James Garrett, The Elements of User Experience (2002, 2nd ed. 2010)

Every product is built from five layers, from abstract to concrete: strategy, scope, structure, skeleton and surface. Each layer constrains the one above it, so most "surface" problems are really problems on a lower plane.

Garrett drew the model to explain why web projects kept failing despite good visual design. His answer: the visible part sits on top of four invisible decisions. If those are wrong or missing, no amount of visual work can save the result. The model is now over twenty years old and it is still the clearest map of what you are actually deciding when you "design an app".

Each plane in detail

1. Strategy: why does this exist?
Two halves: the user's needs and your objectives. For a solo creator, your objectives are honest and simple: revenue, time, joy, learning. Write both down. A product whose strategy is only "I wanted to build it" can be fine, but then say so, and do not pretend that a missing market is a design problem.
2. Scope: what is in and out?
The list of features and content. The most valuable thing on this plane is the "out" list. Every feature you add raises the cost of every future change, and every item you leave out makes the remaining ones easier to find. Garrett splits scope into functional specifications (what it does) and content requirements (what it contains).
3. Structure: how does it fit together?
Two parts: interaction design (how the system behaves in response to the user) and information architecture (how content is organized so people can find it). This is where your conceptual model lives: what the objects are (articles, highlights, tags), how they relate, and what verbs act on them. See Structure & Interaction.
4. Skeleton: where do things go?
Interface design (arranging controls), navigation design (moving between places) and information design (presenting information so it is understood). The wireframe is the skeleton's natural form.
5. Surface: what do the senses meet?
Visual design: type, color, imagery, spacing, motion, sound. It is the most visible plane and the one people judge first. See Visual Design and Typography.

Two rules that make the model useful

Rule one: problems flow upward, so diagnose downward. When something feels wrong on the surface, step down one plane at a time and ask whether the problem started there. A cluttered toolbar (surface) is often a skeleton problem (too many controls on one screen), which is often a scope problem (too many features), which is often a strategy problem (no clear idea of who the product is for).

Rule two: work overlaps, but finishes in order. Garrett did not argue for a waterfall. You can sketch surfaces while you are still deciding scope; often you must, because a sketch reveals what a feature really costs. But you should not finish a plane before the plane beneath it is settled. Polishing the surface of an undecided structure is the most expensive way to waste a week.

Seeing the planes in a real screen

Myth

"UX is the lower planes and UI is the top one, so UX designers and UI designers do separate jobs." The planes describe kinds of decisions, not kinds of people. Every good designer moves up and down the stack all day. A typographic decision (surface) can expose a structure problem (two headings that mean the same thing); a strategy shift can make half the surface obsolete.

For the solo creator

Keep a single page for your product with five short sections, one per plane, bottom first. Strategy in three sentences. Scope as an in/out list. Structure as a list of objects and verbs. Skeleton as a list of screens. Surface as your type, color and spacing tokens. Update it when you change your mind. When you are stuck, read it from the bottom up.

Do

  • Diagnose downward: when the surface feels wrong, check the plane beneath first.
  • Write an explicit "out of scope" list and reread it before adding anything.
  • Sketch the surface early to test what a scope decision really costs.
  • Name your objects and verbs on the structure plane before drawing screens.

Don't

  • Spend a weekend on colors while you still have not decided what the main screen is for.
  • Treat the model as a waterfall that forbids early sketches.
  • Answer a strategy question ("who is this for?") with a surface change ("let's make it look more professional").
  • Let scope grow because the skeleton "has room".

In the wild

  • Basecamp37signals, 2004 onward
    Basecamp's founders wrote openly about saying no to most feature requests and keeping scope small (see their book Getting Real, 2006). A firm scope plane is what lets a small team ship a coherent surface.
  • Things 3Cultured Code, 2017
    Its structure (areas contain projects, projects contain to-dos and headings; "Today" and "This Evening" are views, not containers) is so clear that the surface can be almost bare. Strong structure buys you a quiet surface.
  • Google Search1998 onward
    The home page has remained mostly one field and a logo for decades, because the strategy (find anything, fast) and the scope (search, not a portal) never wavered. Stable lower planes produce a stable surface.
  • Kindle e-readersAmazon, 2007 onward

Common mistakes

Surface-first redesigns

Restyling is fun and visible. Rethinking scope is uncomfortable because it means deleting work.

Before any redesign, write one sentence per plane describing what changes. If only the surface line has content, ask whether that is truly where the problem is.

Skipping structure

Developers often jump from a feature list (scope) straight to screens (skeleton), and the objects and relationships emerge accidentally from the database.

Draw the objects as boxes with arrows for "contains" and "belongs to", and list the verbs. Do this before any screen. It takes 20 minutes.

A strategy that is only your objectives

"Make ₹1 lakh a month" is a real objective, but it says nothing about user needs, so it cannot guide a single design decision.

Pair every objective with a user need sentence: "People who [situation] need [outcome]."

Check yourself

Users of your habit tracker say the home screen "feels cluttered". It shows habits, a streak calendar, a mood log, a journal prompt and a stats card. Which plane should you check first?

Five unrelated features compete on one screen. That is a scope question first, and possibly a strategy one (is this a habit tracker or a journal?). Layout and styling changes would only rearrange the clutter.

Design is deciding under constraints

Taught by Charles Eames, with Herbert Simon and Jeff Bezos

A design is a set of choices between options, made against criteria, inside constraints. Getting good at design mostly means getting good at that sequence: seeing more options, naming the criteria, respecting the constraints, and knowing which decisions are cheap to change.

Herbert Simon, in The Sciences of the Artificial (1969), defined design broadly: everyone designs who devises courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones. It is a useful definition because it strips away the visual mystique. You design when you choose a default, name a button, decide what happens offline, or decide not to build sync this year.

Charles Eames, in a well-known question-and-answer session linked to the 1969 Paris exhibition Qu'est-ce que le design? (later filmed as Design Q&A), was asked whether design admits constraint. He answered that design depends largely upon constraints, and went on to describe the designer's job as recognizing as many of them as possible and accepting them with enthusiasm. That is the attitude to take. Constraints are not what stops you designing; they are what turns an infinite space of possible apps into a finite space you can search.

Step 1: generate more than one option

The single most reliable habit of strong designers is refusing to evaluate the first idea alone. The first idea is usually the most obvious one, which means it is the one every competitor also had. Force yourself to produce at least three genuinely different options, and make them different in kind, not in color: a list, a calendar, a single "next thing" card. Three is the minimum because two options turn into a debate, while three turn into a comparison.

Step 2: name the constraints

Constraints are facts you cannot change in this decision. Platform conventions (an iOS app has a back gesture whether you like it or not). Technical limits (you cannot sync in real time without a server). Your own time and skill. Legal requirements. The user's context (one hand, on a train, poor network). Write them down; they will kill some options immediately, which is a gift.

Step 3: write the criteria, in order

Criteria are what "good" means for this decision. They must be ranked, because they will conflict. "Fast" and "can't lose data" may pull in opposite directions; the ranking says which wins. A ranked list of three or four criteria turns a taste argument with yourself into a quick, almost mechanical comparison.

Step 4: know what kind of decision it is

Jeff Bezos, in his 2015 letter to Amazon shareholders, split decisions into two types. Type 1 decisions are one-way doors: hard or impossible to reverse, so they deserve slow, careful thought. Type 2 decisions are two-way doors: if you are wrong, you walk back through. His warning was that organizations tend to treat every decision as Type 1, and so they slow down. A solo creator usually has the opposite problem: treating one-way doors (data formats, pricing model, URLs, public APIs, the core object model) as casually as button colors.

Key idea

No options, no design. Generate three, kill some with constraints, rank by criteria, and match your care to how hard the decision is to undo.

For the solo creator

Your scarcest constraint is your own time, and it is a legitimate design criterion. "I can build and maintain this alone" belongs on every criteria list. A slightly less elegant design you can ship in two days and maintain for five years beats a perfect design that needs a team. Say it openly rather than letting it silently bend your choices.

Do

  • Produce three options that differ in kind before choosing.
  • Write constraints first; let them eliminate options cheaply.
  • Rank criteria so conflicts resolve themselves.
  • Label each decision one-way or two-way, and slow down only for one-way doors.

Don't

  • Evaluate a single idea in isolation; you will always find it acceptable.
  • Treat constraints as excuses for a weak result; treat them as the shape of the problem.
  • Agonize over two-way doors such as copy and color.
  • Change a data format or pricing model on a whim.

In the wild

  • Twitter's 140 characters2006
    The limit came from SMS, which allowed 160 characters per message, leaving room for a username. A technical constraint became the defining feature of the product's writing style. A constraint accepted fully can become identity.
  • Instagram at launch2010
    Square photos, a small set of filters, and only a phone app. Each was a constraint that kept the product focused and made weak phone photos look better. Fewer options for the user can be a design decision made for them.
  • Apple WatchApple, 2015
    A tiny screen forced a new interaction vocabulary (the Digital Crown, glances later replaced, complications). Whatever you think of individual choices, every one is a response to a hard size constraint. The tighter the constraint, the more original the design has to be.

Common mistakes

The single-option trap

The first idea arrives with a feeling of rightness, and generating more feels like wasted time.

Make "three options" a rule, not a mood. Set a ten-minute timer and sketch three thumbnails before choosing anything.

Hidden criteria

You choose by a criterion you would not admit to ("it's easier for me to build"), and then rationalize.

Put your honest criteria on paper, including build cost. Hidden criteria are only bad because they are hidden.

Casual one-way doors

Solo creators move fast and treat everything as reversible.

Keep a short list of one-way doors for your product. Anything on it gets options written down and a 24-hour wait.

Check yourself

You are choosing how to store notes in your app: a proprietary database format or plain Markdown files in a folder. Which approach to the decision fits best?

Storage format shapes sync, export, trust and every future feature, and migrating real user data is risky. It is a classic one-way door. Votes and demos do not capture the long-term criteria.
Connect the ideas · Part one recap

Your app's export feature works but nobody uses it. You notice it sits in Settings → Advanced → Data. Using chapters 1 to 3, which is the best first move?

Low usage of a buried feature is not proof that nobody needs it. Placement is a skeleton problem grounded in structure (where does "export" belong in the user's model?). Generate placement options before restyling (surface) or deleting (scope).

Part two

Norman's model of everyday things

Don Norman's The Design of Everyday Things (first published in 1988 as The Psychology of Everyday Things, revised and expanded in 2013) is the closest thing design has to a shared textbook. It gives you a vocabulary for why people get stuck. The examples are doors, stoves and taps, but every idea transfers to software almost unchanged. These five chapters cover the whole model, in more depth than the summary you have seen before.

4

Affordances and signifiers

Taught by Don Norman, building on James J. Gibson

An affordance is what an object lets you do. A signifier is what tells you so. Users cannot see affordances directly; they act on signifiers. When the two disagree, people fail, and they blame themselves.

The psychologist James J. Gibson coined "affordance" in the late 1970s to describe the action possibilities the environment offers an animal: a flat, firm surface affords standing; a handle-sized branch affords grasping. Norman brought the word into design in 1988. It spread so fast that designers started saying things like "I added an affordance" when they meant "I added a visual hint". In the 2013 revision of his book, Norman introduced signifier to fix this. The affordance is the relationship (this glass panel can be pushed). The signifier is the perceivable cue (a flat metal plate at hand height that says push here).

On screens the distinction matters even more, because almost everything has the same physical affordance: you can tap, click or press any pixel. What differs is whether anything happens. So in software, design work around "affordance" is really work on signifiers: shape, position, color, text, cursor, motion and convention that tell people what will respond and how.

The Norman door

A "Norman door" is the nickname for any door whose design tells you the wrong thing: a pull handle on a door you must push, or identical handles on both sides so you cannot tell which way it opens. The tell-tale sign of a Norman door is a label. If a door needs a sign saying PUSH, the design has already failed, and the sign is a patch. Try it.

Open the door

You are walking into a café. Open the door.

Same café, same door, different hardware. Open it.

Without: the bar handle is a powerful signifier for "grab and pull". The tiny PUSH sign has to fight it, and usually loses.

Signifiers on screens

Software has its own Norman doors, and you can find them in almost any app. Text that looks like a link but is not. A link that looks like body text. A card with a hover shadow where only the tiny title responds. An icon button that looks like a decorative glyph. A "flat design" period in the mid-2010s produced many of these: when buttons lost their borders, fills and shadows, people lost the signifiers that told them where to press. Research by the Nielsen Norman Group around 2017 found that people needed more time and attention to find clickable elements on weakly signified flat interfaces.

Watch out

Touch screens have no hover. Any signifier that only appears on hover (row actions, tooltips, underline on hover) is invisible on a phone. Design the resting state so it signifies enough on its own.

Key idea

If it needs a label to explain how to operate it, look first for a design that would not.

For the solo creator

You know where every clickable thing is, so you are the worst person to judge signifiers. Use the "squint and point" test: blur your eyes (or take a screenshot and blur it) and point at everything you think is clickable. Then compare with the real list. Better still, ask someone who has never seen the app to do it.

Do

  • Make interactive things look different from non-interactive things, consistently, everywhere.
  • Make the whole visible object the hit target when it looks like one object.
  • Use platform conventions as free signifiers that users already learned.
  • Check every hover-only signifier on a touch device.

Don't

  • Style labels as buttons or buttons as labels.
  • Rely on a tooltip or help text to explain how a control works.
  • Use "affordance" when you mean "visual hint"; say signifier.
  • Remove borders and fills from buttons just to look minimal.

Common mistakes

The invisible gesture

Swipe actions, long-press menus and keyboard shortcuts feel elegant to the designer who knows they exist.

Treat hidden gestures as accelerators only. Every action must also have a visible path (a button or menu item).

Hit targets smaller than the signifier

Only the text or icon is wired up, while the visual row or card looks like a single object.

Make the clickable area match the visible object. On touch, keep targets at least about 44 by 44 points (Apple) or 48 by 48 dp (Android Material).

Fixing with labels

Adding "Click here to edit" is faster than redesigning the control.

Treat every explanatory label as a bug report about the design. Keep it only if no better signifier exists.

Check yourself

In your notes app, a "+" icon in the top right creates a note. New users keep typing into the search field instead, expecting it to create a note. What is going on?

An empty field is one of the strongest "type here" signifiers in software. Its placeholder (or lack of one) does not disambiguate. Options include a clearer placeholder ("Search notes"), or making typing in it offer "Create note with this title", as several launcher-style apps do.

Mapping and constraints

Taught by Don Norman

Mapping is the relationship between controls and their effects. When the layout of the controls mirrors the layout of what they control, people need no labels and make fewer errors. Constraints narrow the possible actions so the right one is the obvious one.

Natural mapping: the stove

Norman's favourite example is the kitchen stove. Four burners sit in a rectangle. The four knobs often sit in a straight line. Which knob controls which burner? With a straight row, there are 24 possible arrangements, and the cook has to read tiny labels or remember. If the knobs are arranged in the same pattern as the burners, the mapping is obvious, and labels become unnecessary.

Mapping in software

Screens are full of mapping decisions, and most are invisible until they go wrong. A slider for text size should get bigger to the right and show a small "A" on the left and a large "A" on the right. A brightness control should increase upward or rightward. The order of items in a settings list should match the order of the things they affect on screen. A volume key on the side of a phone should raise the volume with the upper button.

Good mappings come from three sources. Spatial correspondence: the control's layout mirrors the effect's layout (the stove, or a window-snapping control drawn as a little screen diagram). Cultural metaphor: more is up, time flows left to right in left-to-right languages. Biological or perceptual: louder is "bigger", brighter is "more". A mapping that uses all three at once almost never needs explaining.

Constraints: four kinds

Norman describes four kinds of constraint that guide people toward correct actions. His illustration is a small Lego motorcycle kit: even without instructions, most people assemble it correctly, because the pieces only fit some ways (physical), the rider faces forward (cultural and semantic), and the last piece must go in the last remaining spot (logical). Every kind has a software equivalent.

Watch out

Cultural constraints are local. Red means danger in much of the world but also luck and celebration in parts of East Asia; in India, red and green carry many meanings beyond stop and go. Use color as a supporting signal, never the only one. More in Accessible & Inclusive.

Key idea

Design the controls in the shape of the thing they control, and make wrong actions impossible rather than merely discouraged.

For the solo creator

Constraints are cheaper to build than error handling. A select menu instead of free text, a stepper instead of a number field, a disabled button with a reason, a format mask on a phone number: each removes a whole category of error messages, validation code and support emails. When you are the support team, constraints pay for themselves within a week.

Do

  • Arrange controls spatially like the things they affect.
  • Use direction metaphors consistently: more is up or right.
  • Prefer input methods that can only produce valid values.
  • When a button is disabled, show why next to it.

Don't

  • Use abstract numbers ("Level 3") for perceptual qualities like size or brightness.
  • List settings in an order unrelated to the screen they affect.
  • Let people type a value you will later reject.
  • Rely on color alone as a constraint.

In the wild

  • macOS display arrangementApple, long-standing
    You arrange multiple monitors by dragging rectangles that mirror their physical positions. Moving the mouse off the right edge then lands where the picture says. The control is a miniature of the world.
  • Natural scrollingmacOS Lion, 2011
    Apple reversed trackpad scrolling so content moves with your fingers, as on a touch screen. Many people found it wrong at first because their old mapping (move the scroll bar) was in their heads. Mappings that conflict with learned ones carry a switching cost, even if they are more "natural".
  • Car seat controlsMercedes-Benz and others
    Some cars put the seat adjustment controls on the door in the shape of a seat: push the "back" part to tilt the back. The most famous physical example of spatial mapping, and a good model for settings panels.
  • UPI PIN entryIndia, 2016 onward
    Payment apps show a fixed number of PIN boxes (four or six) that fill as you type. The structure physically constrains length and shows progress. A constraint can also be feedback.

Common mistakes

Arbitrary toggle lists

Settings get appended in the order they were built.

Order settings by the screen layout or by the user's sequence of thought, and group by the object they affect.

Validate after, not constrain before

Free text fields are the default component, and validation feels thorough.

For every field, ask: can the input method make invalid values impossible? Use pickers, masks, steppers and smart defaults.

Disabled with no reason

Disabling a button is an easy logical constraint, but it leaves people stuck guessing.

Either keep the button enabled and explain what is missing when pressed, or show the reason beside the disabled button.

Check yourself

Your split-view writing app has a setting for "Preview position" with options Left, Right, Top, Bottom, shown as a text dropdown. Which redesign uses mapping best?

The control becomes a miniature of the window, so position maps to position. Icons help a little, but a vertical list still maps four directions onto one dimension.

Feedback and conceptual models

Taught by Don Norman, with Robert Miller and Jakob Nielsen on response times

Every action needs an immediate, informative response, and the product as a whole must give people an accurate story of how it works. Feedback answers "did that work?"; a conceptual model answers "how does this thing work at all?"

Feedback: closing the loop

When you press a light switch, the light comes on. That is feedback: the world tells you the result of your action. Software breaks this loop all the time, because the result happens somewhere invisible (a server, a file, a background queue). Good feedback has four properties:

  1. Immediate. Classic response-time research, from Robert Miller (1968) and later summarized by Card, Moran and Newell and by Jakob Nielsen, gives three thresholds: about 0.1 second feels instantaneous; about 1 second keeps the flow of thought but the delay is noticed; about 10 seconds is the limit for keeping attention on the task. Above 0.1 s, show that something is happening. Above about 1 s, show progress. Above about 10 s, let people do something else and notify them later.
  2. Informative. "Error" is feedback, but useless. "Couldn't save: you're offline. Your changes are kept on this device and will sync when you reconnect" is feedback that lets people act.
  3. Proportional. Small actions get small feedback (a checkbox ticks). Big or risky actions get bigger feedback (a toast with Undo). Too much feedback is noise: a modal for every success trains people to dismiss modals.
  4. Located. Feedback should appear where attention already is. A "Saved" message in a status bar the user is not looking at barely exists.

Conceptual models: the story in the user's head

A conceptual model is a simplified explanation of how something works. It does not need to be technically accurate; it needs to be accurate enough to predict what will happen. "Files live in folders" is a conceptual model. So is "the trash keeps deleted things until I empty it", and "a draft is private until I publish it".

Norman's key insight is that there are three models, not one, and the designer only controls one of them directly.

Norman's classic example is the home thermostat. Many people believe that turning it far higher makes the room heat faster, like opening a valve wider. In most homes it does not: the heater runs at one speed until the target is reached, like a timer switch. The wrong model leads to wasted energy and overheated rooms, and nothing on the thermostat corrects it. In software, the same thing happens with sync ("is my phone copy the real one?"), with deletion ("does removing it from this list delete the file?"), and with sharing ("can people with the link edit, or only view?").

Key idea

You never explain your product in person. The interface is the explanation, so every label, state and error message is teaching a model, right or wrong.

For the solo creator

Write your conceptual model in five sentences of plain language before you build ("A notebook holds pages. Pages can be in one notebook only. Deleting a notebook moves its pages to Trash for 30 days…"). If you cannot write it simply, users cannot learn it. Then make sure every label in the UI uses the exact nouns and verbs of those sentences.

Do

  • Give feedback within 0.1 s for every action, even if it is only a pressed state.
  • Show progress for anything over about a second.
  • Use the same nouns and verbs everywhere to teach one model.
  • Make system state visible: saved, syncing, offline, shared.

Don't

  • Let any action pass in silence.
  • Use modals for routine success messages.
  • Use one word ("Delete") for actions with different scopes.
  • Leave people to guess whether their data is safe.

In the wild

  • WhatsApp ticksMeta, read receipts from 2014
    One gray tick: sent. Two gray ticks: delivered. Two blue ticks: read. A tiny, consistent feedback system that hundreds of millions of people learned without instructions. Feedback can be compact if it is consistent.
  • Google Docs statusGoogle
    A small status near the title tells you when changes are saved, and switches to an offline state when you lose connection. Persistent, located status beats one-off messages.
  • macOS document dotApple
    On a Mac, an unsaved document shows a dot in the window's close button. Unsaved state can be signalled with almost no pixels.
  • Dropbox sync iconsDropbox, 2008 onward
    Small badges on files (synced, syncing, online-only) make the hidden sync model visible in the file browser itself. Bring invisible system state to the object it concerns.

Common mistakes

Optimistic silence

Developers know the request usually succeeds, so they skip loading and success states.

Design four states for every action: idle, working, success, failure. Build all four.

Model drift in words

Over time, the UI says "project" in one place, "workspace" in another and "board" in a third, for the same thing.

Keep a glossary of your product's nouns and verbs and grep the codebase for synonyms.

Feedback far from the action

A global toast area is convenient to build.

Put feedback on or next to the control that caused it; use toasts only for actions whose result is elsewhere, and include Undo.

Check yourself

Users of your photo backup app keep deleting photos from their phones and then panicking, asking whether the backup copy is gone too. What is the root cause?

"Copy" and "mirror" behave opposite on deletion. If the interface never makes clear which one it is (for example: "Safe in your backup. Deleting from your phone won't delete it here"), people fill the gap with fear or wrong guesses.

The two gulfs and the seven stages of action

Taught by Don Norman, from work with Edwin Hutchins and James Hollan

Between a person and their goal lie two gulfs: the gulf of execution (how do I do it?) and the gulf of evaluation (did it work?). Design bridges both. The seven stages of action tell you exactly where on the bridge someone fell.

The gulfs were described by Hutchins, Hollan and Norman in the mid-1980s, and Norman made them central to his book. The idea is simple. When you want something, you have to translate your goal into actions the system accepts. That distance is the gulf of execution. Then the system changes, and you have to interpret what you see and compare it with what you wanted. That distance is the gulf of evaluation. Signifiers, mapping and constraints bridge execution. Feedback and a good conceptual model bridge evaluation.

The seven stages of action

Norman breaks every interaction into seven stages: one for the goal, three for execution, and three for evaluation. Most of the time they happen in a fraction of a second and nobody notices. They become visible when something goes wrong, which is exactly why they are useful for diagnosis.

Norman also points out that goals come in levels. "Send Asha the photos" sits under "keep the friendship warm", which sits under "be a good friend". Most products serve the lowest level, but the best ones understand the levels above. A photo app that suggests "Share this album with the people in it" has understood the higher goal. This links directly to Cooper's goal-directed design in chapter 11 and to jobs to be done in Problems Worth Solving.

Key idea

When someone gets stuck, ask "which stage?" before asking "which pixel?". The answer tells you whether to fix a signifier, a word, or a piece of feedback.

For the solo creator

Use the seven stages to read support emails. "I can't find how to…" is stages 2 to 3. "I clicked but…" is stage 5. "What does … mean?" is stage 6. "Did it actually…?" is stage 7. Tag each email with its stage for a month; the pattern tells you which bridge to rebuild first.

Do

  • Diagnose problems by stage before redesigning.
  • Make the result of an action appear as something new in the world (an item, a status, a change of state).
  • Design for the goal one level above the immediate task.

Don't

  • Assume a problem is visual when people cannot even form a plan.
  • Clear a form as the only sign of success.
  • Treat evaluation as optional because "it obviously worked".

    Typing what you want ("rename", "dark theme") narrows the gulf of execution: you specify your intent in words, and the palette shows the action. Let people state goals in their own language.
  • Swiggy and Zomato order trackingIndia, 2010s
    After paying, you see the order move through clear states (confirmed, being prepared, picked up, arriving) with a map. Long processes need a visible path for evaluation, or anxiety fills the gap.
  • SpreadsheetsVisiCalc 1979 onward
    Change a number and every dependent cell updates at once. The gulf of evaluation almost disappears, which is a big part of why spreadsheets became so widely used. Instant visible consequences make complex systems learnable.

Common mistakes

Fixing the wrong stage

"Users can't find export" gets fixed with a bigger button (perform) when the real gap is that they did not know export existed (plan).

Ask people to think aloud and note the first moment they hesitate. That moment's stage is where the fix belongs.

Feedback that needs interpreting

An icon changes, but its meaning is unclear (stage 6).

Pair new or rare status icons with words, at least until people have learned them.

Check yourself

A user sets a reminder in your app. The app shows a toast: "Scheduled ✓". The next morning the user opens the app to check whether the reminder really exists. Which stage is weakest?

They perceived and understood the toast, but it disappeared and did not say what was scheduled or when. A persistent, specific result ("Reminder: Call Dad · Tomorrow 9:00", visible in a list) supports the comparison.

Knowledge, errors and human-centered design

Taught by Don Norman, with James Reason on human error

People do not carry precise knowledge in their heads; they rely on what the world shows them. When they err, it is usually a slip (right goal, wrong action) or a mistake (wrong goal or plan). Design for both, and design with people, not just for them.

Knowledge in the head and knowledge in the world

Norman's demonstration is the coin test: most people cannot draw the front of a coin they have handled thousands of times, yet they never fail to recognize it. We store just enough to tell things apart and rely on the world for the rest. Precise behavior does not require precise knowledge, because the world fills in the gaps through signifiers, constraints and visible options.

This has a direct design consequence. Anything you put in the world (a visible menu, a recent-files list, a label, an autocomplete suggestion) can be recognized. Anything you leave in the head (a keyboard shortcut, a command syntax, a code from an email) must be recalled. Recognition is far easier than recall, which is why Nielsen made "recognition rather than recall" one of his ten heuristics.

Slips and mistakes

Norman builds on the psychologist James Reason's work on human error. The core split is between slips, where the intention was right but the action went wrong, and mistakes, where the intention itself was wrong. They need different fixes, so telling them apart is a practical skill.

Designing for error

Norman's advice is to assume every error that can be made will be made, and to design accordingly. In order of strength:

  1. Prevent: use constraints so the error cannot happen (chapter 5).
  2. Make reversible: undo, trash with a grace period, version history. Reversibility turns errors into exploration.
  3. Detect early: warn at the moment of risk, specifically, not in general ("You mentioned an attachment but none is attached").
  4. Explain and help recover: when an error does happen, say what happened, why, and what to do next, in plain words.
  5. Confirm, rarely: confirmation dialogs are the weakest tool, because people learn to press OK without reading. Save them for irreversible, high-cost actions, and make them specific ("Delete 'Thesis draft' and its 14 versions? This can't be undone").

Human-centered design

Norman's book ends with a process: human-centered design, an approach formalized in the international standard ISO 9241-210. Its loop is observation, idea generation, prototyping and testing, repeated. The point is not the steps but the attitude: assume your first understanding of the problem is wrong, and use contact with real people to correct it, early and often. He also warns that the right problem is rarely the one first stated. "Solve the correct problem" is the first job (more in Problems Worth Solving).

Key idea

There is no such thing as "user error" in a design review. There is only error the design made likely, and error the design made recoverable.

For the solo creator

Undo is the highest-leverage feature you can build. It makes every other part of your product safer to explore, it lets you drop most confirmation dialogs, and it turns support emails ("I deleted it by accident") into non-events. If your architecture makes undo hard, that is a one-way door worth revisiting (chapter 3).

Do

  • Show the shortcut beside the menu item, so recognition teaches recall.
  • Make destructive actions undoable before you add any confirmation.
  • Separate destructive controls from common ones.
  • Write error messages that say what happened and what to do next.

Don't

  • Blame users in copy ("Invalid input").
  • Use generic "Are you sure?" dialogs.
  • Add modes without a loud, persistent indicator.
  • Require people to remember anything from one screen to the next.

Confirmations feel safe and are quick to build.

You test your own flows by following the happy path, with full knowledge.

Rams' ten principles, translated to software

Taught by Dieter Rams, with the Braun design team

Dieter Rams' ten principles describe good design as innovative, useful, aesthetic, understandable, unobtrusive, honest, long-lasting, thorough, environmentally friendly and as little design as possible. Each one translates directly into concrete software decisions.

Rams was head of design at Braun from 1961 to 1995 and designed furniture for Vitsœ, including the 606 Universal Shelving System (1960), which is still made today. Around the late 1970s, worried that the world was filling up with a confusing clutter of forms, colors and noises, he asked himself whether his own design was good design, and wrote his answer as ten principles. His summary of them is three words, in German Weniger, aber besser.

Less, but better.Dieter Rams, his motto, and the title of his 1995 book

The principles are often reduced to "minimalism". That misses most of them. Only the tenth is about reduction. The others are about purpose (useful), clarity (understandable), restraint (unobtrusive), ethics (honest), time (long-lasting), care (thorough) and responsibility (environmentally friendly). Read them as a checklist of the qualities a product earns, not a style.

Click through the ten. Each has Rams' principle, its meaning in software, a drawn don't and do, and a one-line test you can run on any screen.

Principle 1 of 10

Good design is innovative.

Innovation in software is not novelty; it is using what is newly possible (a new sensor, a faster chip, a new model) to remove effort that used to be unavoidable. Rams added that innovative design always develops in tandem with innovative technology, and is never an end in itself.

Principle 2 of 10

Good design makes a product useful.

A product is bought to be used, so design must serve its primary purpose first and disregard anything that could detract from it. In software, usefulness is measured at the moment someone opens the app: does the first screen help with the reason they came?

Principle 3 of 10

Good design is aesthetic.

Rams argued that aesthetic quality is integral to usefulness, because products we use every day affect our wellbeing. On screens, beauty mostly comes from order: alignment, consistent spacing, one type family, restrained color. It is not ornament.

Principle 4 of 10

Good design makes a product understandable.

It clarifies the product's structure. Better still, Rams said, it makes the product talk: at best, it is self-explanatory. In software that means the interface teaches its own model (chapter 6) without a manual.

Principle 5 of 10

Good design is unobtrusive.

Products that fulfil a purpose are like tools, Rams wrote: neither decorative objects nor works of art. Their design should be neutral and restrained, leaving room for the user's self-expression. In software: the user's content is the hero; the chrome steps back.

Principle 6 of 10

Good design is honest.

It does not make a product appear more innovative, powerful or valuable than it really is, and it does not attempt to manipulate the consumer with promises that cannot be kept. In software this is the line between persuasion and dark patterns (see Honest & Calm).

Principle 7 of 10

Good design is long-lasting.

It avoids being fashionable and therefore never appears antiquated. In software, long-lasting design means two things: a visual language that will not date in a year, and data and URLs that outlive the app (open formats, export, stable links).

Principle 8 of 10

Good design is thorough down to the last detail.

Nothing must be arbitrary or left to chance. Care and accuracy in the design process show respect towards the user. In software, the last details are the states nobody draws: empty, loading, error, very long text, zero, one, many, offline.

Principle 9 of 10

Good design is environmentally friendly.

Rams meant conserving resources and minimizing pollution across a product's lifecycle. Software has a footprint too: energy in data centres and devices, battery, data plans, and old phones forced into the bin by bloated apps. Lighter software is kinder and more widely usable.

Principle 10 of 10

Good design is as little design as possible.

Less, but better, because it concentrates on the essential aspects, and the products are not burdened with non-essentials. Back to purity, back to simplicity. In software: remove every element that does not do work, then make the remaining ones excellent.

Myth

Key idea

Rams' principles are a description of respect: for the user's purpose, attention, intelligence, money, time and planet. Minimal looks are a side effect.

For the solo creator

Principle 8 (thorough) is where solo products most often show their seams, because edge states are boring to build and you rarely see them yourself.   • Run all ten tests on your main screen once a month.
  • Use one accent color to mark the single most important control.
  • Design the edge states (empty, error, long text) before calling a screen finished.
  • Prefer open formats and export as a design decision for longevity.

Don't

  • Treat "less" as removing labels and borders that people need.
  • Copy Braun's surface (rounded corners, dots, gray) and skip its purpose.
  • Follow this year's visual trend for core UI.
  • Use urgency, pre-ticked boxes or hidden fees.

Frameworks that evaluate: Vitruvius to Tognazzini

Taught by Vitruvius, Jakob Nielsen, Ben Shneiderman and Bruce Tognazzini, with Aarron Walter

A handful of tested checklists let you evaluate any interface quickly. Vitruvius gives the top-level triad (firm, useful, delightful); Nielsen, Shneiderman and Tognazzini give you detailed rules. Use them as lenses for finding problems, not as recipes for creating solutions.

Firmness, commodity and delight

The Roman architect Vitruvius, writing De architectura in the first century BC, said a building should have firmitas, utilitas and venustas. The English diplomat Henry Wotton's 1624 rendering, "commodity, firmness, and delight", is the version designers still quote. Two thousand years later it still works as the shortest possible definition of good design, and it maps neatly onto software.

Aarron Walter's Designing for Emotion (2011) turned the same idea into a pyramid adapted from Maslow: a product must first be functional, then reliable, then usable, and only then can it be pleasurable. Each layer depends on the ones below it.

Jakob Nielsen developed his heuristics with Rolf Molich around 1990 and published the refined list of ten in 1994. They are "heuristics" because they are rules of thumb, not precise guidelines: they help you spot problems, and they are the basis of the heuristic evaluation method covered in Testing & Iteration. Shneiderman's eight golden rules

Ben Shneiderman, a computer scientist at the University of Maryland, published his eight golden rules of interface design in Designing the User Interface (first edition 1986), and has refined them through later editions. They overlap with Nielsen's but emphasize two things Nielsen does not name directly: closure and the user's sense of control.

  1. Strive for consistency. Same situations, same actions, same words.
  2. Seek universal usability. Design for novices and experts, different ages, abilities and devices. (Earlier editions: "enable frequent users to use shortcuts".)
  3. Offer informative feedback. Modest for frequent actions, substantial for rare and major ones.
  4. Design dialogs to yield closure. Sequences have a beginning, middle and end, and the end is clearly signalled ("Order placed. Arrives Thursday").
  5. Prevent errors. And when they happen, offer simple, specific recovery.
  6. Permit easy reversal of actions. It relieves anxiety and encourages exploration.
  7. Keep users in control. Experienced users want to feel they are in charge, and that the system responds to them, not the other way round.
  8. Reduce short-term memory load. People hold only a few items in mind; don't make them carry information between screens.

Tognazzini's first principles

Bruce "Tog" Tognazzini founded Apple's Human Interface Group and wrote early editions of Apple's Human Interface Guidelines. His essay "First Principles of Interaction Design", maintained on his site asktog.com and revised in 2014, lists principles that are more concrete than Nielsen's. A selection worth knowing:

Anticipation
Bring people the information and tools they will need for the next step, before they ask.
Autonomy
Keep users in control, but within boundaries, and keep them informed of status so they can make their own decisions.
Defaults
Defaults should be easy to replace (selected so typing overwrites them), and should reflect what most people want.
Efficiency of the user
Optimize the user's time, not the computer's. The user's time is the expensive resource.
Explorable interfaces
Make actions reversible and offer stable perceptual landmarks, so people can explore without fear of getting lost or breaking things.
Fitts's law
The time to acquire a target depends on its distance and size. Big, close targets are fast; screen edges and corners are effectively infinitely large for a mouse. (More in The Human Mind.)
Latency reduction
Make the interface respond instantly, even if the work takes time; acknowledge every click immediately.
Protect users' work
Never let users lose their work through system failure or their own error.
State tracking
Remember where people were and what they were doing, and bring them back there.

Three arguments: goals, humane interfaces, information software

Taught by Alan Cooper, Jef Raskin and Bret Victor

Cooper argued that you design for people's goals, not tasks or features. Raskin argued that interfaces should never waste human attention or harm human work. Victor argued that most software exists to show information, so interaction should be a last resort. Together they push you toward software that does more and asks less.

Alan Cooper: design for goals

Alan Cooper, often called the "father of Visual Basic" for the visual programming tool he sold to Microsoft, became one of the first prominent voices for interaction design as a profession. In About Face (1995) and The Inmates Are Running the Asylum (1999) he argued that software was hard to use because programmers designed it around how computers work, and he proposed goal-directed design.

The core distinction is between goals and tasks. Goals are end states people want and are fairly stable ("never miss a client payment"). Tasks are the steps a particular technology makes them do ("export CSV, open spreadsheet, filter by date"). Tasks change when technology changes; goals do not. Design for the goal, and you are free to eliminate tasks. Cooper also distinguished personal goals (not feeling stupid, not making mistakes) from practical ones, and pointed out that software that makes people feel stupid fails a goal they never state.

Personas, and their limits

To keep goals concrete, Cooper introduced personas in Inmates: precise descriptions of hypothetical archetypal users, built from research, each with a name, context and goals. His argument was that designing for "the user" lets every feature in, because some imagined user always wants it (he called this the "elastic user"), whereas designing for one specific person forces choices.

Personas became hugely popular, and widely misused. Common failures: personas invented without research, full of demographic trivia (age, favourite coffee) that changes no decision; personas used to stereotype; and personas that sit in a slide deck and never affect a design. Many teams now prefer jobs-to-be-done statements and real interview notes. For a solo creator, the useful core of Cooper's idea is simple: design for one specific, real person's goals at a time, ideally a person you have actually talked to.

Perpetual intermediates

Cooper also observed in About Face that most users are neither beginners nor experts. People pass through the beginner stage quickly (or quit), and few ever invest enough to become experts. The majority settle as perpetual intermediates: they know the core features well and the rest vaguely.

Jef Raskin: the humane interface

Jef Raskin started the Macintosh project at Apple in 1979 and later designed the Canon Cat. His book The Humane Interface (2000) defines an interface as humane if it is responsive to human needs and considerate of human frailties. Several of his ideas sharpen what you learned from Norman:

Locus of attention
People have only one locus of attention at a time. Anything outside it (a status bar message, a corner badge) may as well not exist. Put feedback where attention is.
Habituation
Any often-repeated action becomes automatic. People will habituate to pressing "OK" on a confirmation dialog, which is why confirmations fail. Design so habits are safe.
Modes
A mode is when the same action produces different results depending on a state that is not your locus of attention. Raskin considered modes a major source of errors. His alternative: quasimodes, states held only while you physically hold them (like holding Shift), which cannot be forgotten.
Monotony
Raskin used "monotony" for interfaces that offer one obvious way to do each thing, so habits form fast and reliably. Many redundant ways to do the same thing slow habit formation.
Protect the user's work
He framed this as a parody of Asimov's laws of robotics: a computer should never harm your work, or through inaction allow it to come to harm; and it should never waste your time or make you do more work than necessary.

Bret Victor: information software

In his 2006 essay Magic Ink: Information Software and the Graphical Interface, Bret Victor divided software into three kinds: information software (helping you learn something: what's on my calendar, when is the next train), manipulation software (helping you create: an editor, a design tool) and communication software. His claim was that most software people use is information software, yet it is designed like manipulation software, full of controls, forms and navigation.

Information software, he argued, is closer to graphic design than to machine design. Its job is to show the right data, arranged to answer the user's questions, using context (time, location, history) to infer what they want. Interaction is a cost the user pays when the software failed to infer; he titled one section "Interaction considered harmful". Victor illustrated this with a redesign of a San Francisco BART train-schedule widget that showed upcoming trains at a glance instead of asking for a query.

Raskin's "don't waste the user's time" and Victor's "interaction is a last resort" meet in the same place: every tap, field and menu must earn its existence. Try the difference below.

Part four

A process for one person

Big-company design processes assume researchers, workshops, reviews and hand-offs. You have none of those, and you do not need them. You need a small loop you can run every week: understand, decide, make it real at the right fidelity, check, ship. These three chapters give you that loop, a ladder of fidelities to choose from, and the thinking habits that keep a one-person studio honest.

The double diamond, for one

Taught by the UK Design Council, with Bill Buxton and Ryan Singer

Good design alternates between opening up (exploring many possibilities) and narrowing down (choosing one), twice: first to find the right problem, then to find the right solution. A solo creator can run both diamonds in a week.

The UK Design Council published the Double Diamond in 2005 after studying how design teams at large companies actually worked. It has four phases: Discover (explore the problem widely), Define (narrow to a clear problem statement), Develop (explore many solutions) and Deliver (narrow to one, test, ship). The shape matters more than the labels. It says that you should diverge before you converge, and that you should do it for the problem before you do it for the solution.

Divergence and convergence

The two motions need different mindsets, and mixing them is the most common way to stall. When you diverge, you defer judgment: quantity, variety, wild options, no "but that won't work". When you converge, you judge hard: criteria, constraints, kill your darlings. Trying to do both at once produces one cautious idea, judged before it was ever fully imagined.

A one-person week

Here is the double diamond scaled to a solo creator with a product already in use. It assumes about one focused design hour a day alongside building; adjust to your rhythm.

  1. Monday, discover (60 min). Read the last week's support emails, reviews and analytics. Talk to or message one user. Use your own product for a real task. Write every observation as a fact, not a solution.
  2. Tuesday, define (45 min). Cluster the observations. Pick one problem. Write a problem statement: "[Person] trying to [goal] struggles with [obstacle], which causes [consequence]."
  3. Wednesday, develop (60 min). Sketch at least five solutions on paper, of different kinds (chapter 3). Pick two. Make each just real enough to judge (chapter 13).
  4. Thursday, deliver (build time). Build the stronger one, in code, at the lowest fidelity that can ship.
  5. Friday, check and ship (45 min). Use it yourself on a real task. If possible, watch one person use it. Ship it, note it in your changelog, and write what you will look for next week.

Fixed time, variable scope

Ryan Singer's Shape Up (Basecamp, 2019) adds a useful twist for small teams: decide the appetite first (how much time is this problem worth: two days? two weeks?) and then shape a solution that fits it, instead of estimating how long a fixed solution will take. For a solo creator this is a lifesaver. It turns "how long will this take?" (unknowable) into "what is the best solution I can build in three days?" (a design question).

Key idea

Diverge, then converge, on the problem first and the solution second. Fix the time, flex the scope.

For the solo creator

Your discover phase does not need a research budget. It needs a habit: a folder or note where every user message, review and observation lands, and a weekly hour to read it. Ten real messages a week will teach you more than any persona workshop. Guide 15 covers testing alone in detail.

Do

  • Write a problem statement before sketching solutions.
  • Treat feature requests as clues to a problem, not as specifications.
  • Separate generating time from judging time.
  • Set an appetite before shaping a solution.

Don't

  • Treat the double diamond as a months-long project plan.
  • Skip the first diamond because "I already know the problem".
  • Judge ideas while you are still generating them.
  • Estimate a fixed scope and then let it grow.

In the wild

    Six-week cycles, work shaped at the right level of abstraction (rough but solved), and a cool-down between cycles. Published free online. Process designed for small teams that build what they design.
  • Superhuman onboardingSuperhuman, late 2010s
    The email app became known for one-on-one onboarding calls with new users in its early years. Each call doubled as discovery research. Discovery can be built into the way you serve customers.
  • Indie Mac and iOS appsmany
    Solo developers often run public changelogs and feedback boards, and some develop in public on social media, collecting reactions to rough ideas before building them. Cheap, continuous divergence with real people.

Endless discovery

Problem statements often sneak a solution in ("users need folders").

If the statement names a feature, rewrite it as an obstacle ("users can't find notes older than a month").

Requests are clues. "Reading in bed at night" might best be solved by an automatic night theme with warm tones and lower brightness, not only a dark toggle. A short discovery step makes the solution better and sometimes cheaper.

The fidelity ladder

Taught by Bill Buxton, with Bret Victor

Every representation of a design (sketch, wireframe, mockup, prototype, code) answers different questions at a different cost. Choose the lowest fidelity that can answer the question you have right now, and climb only when you need to.

Bill Buxton's Sketching User Experiences (2007) makes a distinction every designer should carry: a sketch and a prototype are not just rough and polished versions of the same thing. A sketch is for exploring: it is quick, cheap, disposable, plentiful and deliberately ambiguous, and it invites questions. A prototype is for testing: it is more refined, more expensive, more specific, and it seeks answers. You make many sketches to find a direction, and a few prototypes to check it.

Fidelity has costs in both directions. Too low, and you cannot judge what matters (a paper sketch cannot tell you whether an animation feels fast). Too high, and you have spent time on details that will be thrown away, and you become attached to them. Worse, high fidelity invites the wrong feedback: show someone a polished mockup and they will comment on colors, not on whether the flow makes sense.

When to skip rungs, and designing in code

The ladder is not a staircase you must climb step by step. Skip rungs when the question you have can only be answered higher up, or when a higher rung is cheap for you. Three common cases:

  • Skip to code for feel. Scrolling, gestures, animation timing and performance cannot be judged in static images. If the core question is "does it feel fast?", build a thin slice in code.
  • Skip mockups when you have a system. Once you have type, color and spacing tokens and a set of components, a wireframe plus your system is effectively a mockup. Many solo creators go sketch → code.
  • Never skip the sketch. It costs minutes and it is the only rung where you can afford ten directions. Skipping it means your first idea becomes your only idea.

Designing in code is a real advantage for a developer-designer: real data, real content lengths, real speed, and no translation step. The risk is that code makes changes feel expensive, so you stop exploring, and that you design what is easy to build instead of what is right. The fix is discipline: sketch at least three options on paper first, then build the one you choose, and use throwaway branches for experiments so nothing feels precious.

Key idea

Match fidelity to the question. Sketch to explore, prototype to test, code to confirm and ship.

For the solo creator

Keep a paper notebook (or a cheap tablet with a pen) next to your keyboard. When you catch yourself opening the editor to "try an idea", stop and sketch three versions first. It takes five minutes and it is the single habit most likely to improve your designs. Bret Victor's later talks argue that creators need an immediate connection to what they make; for layout and flow questions, a pen gives you exactly that.

Do

  • Write the question before choosing the fidelity.
  • Sketch at least three options before any digital work.
  • Go straight to code for questions of feel, speed and real data.
  • Use throwaway branches for exploration in code.

Don't

  • Polish a mockup to answer a flow question.
  • Ask for feedback on structure with a pixel-perfect screen.
  • Treat each rung as mandatory.
  • Let "it's already built" decide what is right.

In the wild

  • Paper prototypinglong-standing practice; see Carolyn Snyder, 2003
    Teams test whole flows with paper screens a person "operates" by swapping sheets. Snyder's book Paper Prototyping documented the method. You can test navigation before writing any code.
  • Figma prototypesFigma
    Linking frames with transitions turns static mockups into clickable prototypes in minutes, good for testing navigation and order. The prototype rung has become cheap; use it for flow questions.
  • SwiftUI previews and hot reloadApple, 2019; Flutter, React tooling
    Live previews make code feel closer to a sketching medium for many developers. Fast feedback tools move code down the cost curve, which makes designing in code more viable.

Common mistakes

Pixel-perfect too early

Polish feels like progress and looks good in a portfolio.

Never leaving paper

Some questions (speed, feel, real content) cannot be answered on paper, but staying there feels safe.

"Feels satisfying" is about motion, timing and touch, which only exist on a real device in an interactive build. Paper and static mockups can answer whether the action should exist, but not how it feels.

Thinking like a designer, alone

Taught by Don Norman, Larry Tesler and Ryan Singer, with Taiichi Ohno's five whys

Design thinking for a soloist comes down to four habits: frame the problem before solving it, use constraints as tools, make trade-offs explicitly, and spend craft where it compounds. Every decision has a cost that keeps growing after you make it.

Framing: the problem behind the request

Norman argues that designers should never start by solving the problem they were given, because the stated problem is usually a symptom. A simple tool for getting underneath it is the "five whys" associated with Taiichi Ohno and the Toyota Production System: ask why a problem happens, then why that happens, a few times over, until you reach a cause you can design for. Five is a rule of thumb; stop when the answer becomes something you can act on.

Another framing tool is the "How might we…" question, popularized by design consultancies such as IDEO. "How might we help freelancers get invoices approved faster?" is open enough to allow many answers and narrow enough to act on. Compare it with "How might we add export?" (too narrow, a solution in disguise) and "How might we help freelancers?" (too broad to start).

Constraints as tools

Chapter 3 treated constraints as facts you must accept. You can also choose them on purpose, as creative tools. A self-imposed constraint shrinks the search space and forces originality. Some that work well for software:

  • One screen. What if the whole product had to fit on one screen? You will discover what is essential.
  • No settings. Every setting you want to add must become a good default instead.
  • One typeface, two sizes, one accent. Hierarchy must come from position, weight and space.
  • Works offline first. Forces a clear data model and honest sync states.
  • Three-day appetite. The best version you can build in three days, not the ideal version.

Trade-offs: every design is a compromise

No design maximizes everything. Simplicity trades against power. Speed of building trades against polish. Flexibility trades against learnability. The designer's job is not to avoid trade-offs but to make them on purpose and say which way they went.

Larry Tesler, the computer scientist known for cut, copy and paste and for his work at Xerox PARC and Apple, gave this a sharper form, often called Tesler's law or the law of conservation of complexity: every application has an inherent amount of complexity that cannot be removed, only moved. The question is whether the user deals with it or the system (and its designer) does. A timezone-aware calendar is complex; you can either make each user think about timezones, or absorb that complexity in the code so they never have to.

Good enough versus craft

You cannot polish everything. A solo creator who tries will never ship. But a product with no polish anywhere feels careless. The answer is to concentrate craft where it compounds: on the hot path, the few screens and actions people use every single session. A rough edge in the export dialog, seen twice a year, costs little. A rough edge in the main list, seen fifty times a day, costs a lot.

"Good enough" still has a floor. Every screen must be correct, clear and consistent with your system. It is the extra layer (custom motion, perfect empty-state illustrations, bespoke micro-interactions) that you ration.

The cost of a decision over time

An old observation in software engineering, associated with Barry Boehm's work in the 1970s and 1980s, is that the later a problem is found, the more it costs to fix. Design decisions follow the same curve. Changing your mind on a sketch costs seconds. Changing it after launch costs code, migration, documentation, user re-learning, and sometimes trust. And every feature carries an ongoing cost after it ships: maintenance, support, testing, and the attention it takes from everything else on screen.

Key idea

Complexity is conserved, polish is rationed, and decisions get more expensive every day. Frame first, choose the trade-off, then spend your craft where people live.

For the solo creator

Keep a "carrying cost" column in your feature list: for each feature, how many support emails, bugs and minutes of maintenance it costs per month. Features with high carrying cost and low use are candidates for removal. Removing a feature is a design decision too, and often the best one available to a person with limited hours.

Do

  • Ask "why?" until you reach a problem you can design for.
  • Pick one or two self-imposed constraints per project.
  • Write down which way each trade-off went.
  • Absorb complexity in the system when it would otherwise land on every user.
  • Concentrate craft on the hot path.

Don't

  • Solve the request as stated without understanding it.
  • Promise simple, powerful and quick to build all at once.
  • Push complexity onto users through settings to avoid deciding.
  • Polish rarely-seen screens while the main list has rough edges.

In the wild

  • Apple Photos "Memories"Apple, 2016
    Grouping photos into events and stories is complex (faces, places, dates), but the app absorbs it so the user just scrolls. Tesler's law: complexity moved from user to system.
  • Notion2016 onward
    Chose power and flexibility (blocks, databases, relations), accepting a steeper learning curve and offering templates to soften it. A deliberate point on the triangle, with compensation for what it gave up.
  • Craigslist1995 onward
    Very little visual craft, but fast, predictable and focused on its core path for decades. "Good enough" surface on a firm, useful core can last a very long time.

Common mistakes

Settings as indecision

Adding a toggle lets you avoid choosing between two good options.

Choose a default. Add a setting only if real users with real needs require both, and even then, consider whether it belongs in the product at all.

Uniform polish

Perfectionism is not selective.

List screens by frequency of use. Assign a craft budget per screen, top-heavy.

Forgetting carrying costs

Features are celebrated when shipped, never when maintained.

Review your feature list every quarter with a "keep, improve, remove" verdict for each.

Check yourself

Your meeting scheduler asks every user to set their timezone, and then asks invitees to convert times themselves. Users complain. What does Tesler's law suggest?

Timezones are inherent complexity. The choice is who handles it. Detecting and converting in the system moves it from every user to one implementation, which is exactly the trade Tesler recommends.

The designer's vocabulary

A working glossary, drawn from the whole canon

Precise words let you see precise problems. "It feels off" cannot be fixed; "the primary action has weaker contrast than the secondary one" can. Learn these terms and use them in your notes, your critiques and your decisions file.

The glossary below has about fifty terms, grouped so that related ideas sit together. Each links to the guide where it is taught in depth. You do not need to memorize it in one sitting. Read it once now, then use the flashcard exercise at the end of the chapter to make it stick. The full course glossary lives in the Reference Library.

Mind and perception

Mental model
A person's internal explanation of how something works. Formed from experience and the system image (chapter 6).
Conceptual model
The simplified explanation the designer intends people to form; ideally the mental model matches it.
Cognitive load
The mental effort a task demands. Extraneous load (caused by poor design) is the kind you can remove. See The Human Mind.
Recognition versus recall
Recognizing an option you can see is far easier than recalling it from memory (chapter 8).
Locus of attention
Raskin's term for the single thing a person is attending to right now (chapter 11).
Habituation
Repeated actions become automatic, including dismissing warnings.
Preattentive attributes
Visual properties (color, size, orientation, motion) noticed in a fraction of a second, before focused attention.
Gestalt principles
Perceptual grouping rules (proximity, similarity, closure, continuity, common region) that explain why some layouts read as groups. See Visual Design.
Fitts's law
Time to hit a target grows with distance and shrinks with size (chapter 10).
Hick's law
Decision time grows with the number and complexity of choices (roughly logarithmically for equally likely choices).
Jakob's law
Nielsen's observation that users spend most of their time on other products, so they expect yours to work the same way.

Interaction

Affordance
The relationship between an object and a person that makes an action possible (chapter 4).
Signifier
A perceivable cue that tells people what action is possible and where.
Mapping
The relationship between controls and their effects; natural when the layout matches (chapter 5).
Constraint
A limit that guides people toward correct actions: physical, cultural, semantic or logical.
Feedback
Communication of the result of an action, ideally within 0.1 s.
Feedforward
Information before an action about what will happen if you take it (a button label that says "Delete 3 files", a preview while dragging).
State
The condition of an element or the system at a moment: default, hover, focus, pressed, disabled, loading, error, empty, success.
Mode / quasimode
A mode changes how the same input is interpreted; a quasimode does so only while held (chapter 11).
Direct manipulation
Acting on visible objects with immediate, reversible results (dragging a file, resizing a window), described by Shneiderman in the early 1980s.
Microinteraction
A small, contained moment built around a single task (a toggle, a like, a pull-to-refresh). Named and described by Dan Saffer in his 2013 book.
Progressive disclosure
Showing only what is needed now and revealing advanced options on request.
Hit target
The area that responds to a tap or click, which can be larger than the visible element.

Structure

Information architecture (IA)
How content and functions are organized, labelled and connected so people can find them. See Structure & Interaction.
Navigation
The means of moving between places: tabs, sidebars, stacks, search, links.
Flow
A sequence of screens and actions that completes a task (also, separately, Csikszentmihalyi's state of absorbed focus).
Happy path
The flow when everything goes right. Designing only this is a classic mistake.
Edge case
An unusual but possible situation: empty data, huge data, very long text, no network.
Empty state
What a screen shows when there is nothing to show yet. Prime space for signifiers and teaching.
Primary action
The one most important action on a screen, given the strongest visual weight.
Object model
The nouns of your product and their relationships (a project contains tasks), on Garrett's structure plane.

Visual

Visual hierarchy
The order in which the eye perceives elements, set by size, weight, contrast, color and position.
Grid
An underlying structure of columns and rows that aligns elements.
Whitespace (negative space)
Empty space that separates, groups and gives rest. It is a material, not leftover.
Alignment
Placing elements along shared edges or axes; the cheapest way to look ordered.
Contrast
Difference between elements (in size, weight, color, luminance) that creates emphasis. Also, specifically, the luminance contrast ratio used for legibility (WCAG). See Accessible & Inclusive.
Rhythm
Repetition of spacing and sizes that makes a layout feel coherent.
Density
How much information fits in a given space; a deliberate choice, not a fixed virtue.
Type scale
A set of related font sizes used consistently. See Typography.
Measure
Line length in characters; comfortable body text is often cited around 45 to 75 characters.
Leading (line height)
Vertical distance between lines of text.
Skeuomorphism
Imitating physical materials and objects in UI (stitched leather, paper textures), dominant in early iOS until 2013.
Flat design
A style that removes simulated depth and texture; risks losing signifiers if taken too far.

Process and system

Fidelity
How closely a representation resembles the final product (chapter 13).
Wireframe, mockup, prototype
Structural layout; static visual design; interactive simulation.
Problem statement
A sentence naming who, which goal, which obstacle and what consequence, with no solution inside.
Jobs to be done (JTBD)
A framing in which people "hire" a product to make progress in a situation. See Problems Worth Solving.
Persona
A research-based archetype of a user, with goals (chapter 11).
Appetite
The amount of time a problem is worth, set before shaping a solution (Shape Up).
Design system
A set of reusable decisions (tokens, components, patterns, guidelines). See Systems & Platforms.
Design token
A named design value (color, spacing, radius) stored as data so it can be used consistently in code.
Component
A reusable interface building block with defined states and variants.

Quality and ethics

Heuristic evaluation
Reviewing an interface against a set of rules of thumb, such as Nielsen's ten (chapter 10).
Usability
How effectively, efficiently and satisfyingly people can use something to reach a goal (the ISO 9241-11 definition).
Perceived performance
How fast something feels, which can differ from how fast it is. See Motion & Feel.
Dark pattern (deceptive pattern)
A design that tricks people into actions against their interests; term coined by Harry Brignull in 2010. See Honest & Calm.
Accessibility
Designing so people with disabilities can perceive, operate and understand the product; guided by WCAG.
Calm technology
Technology that informs without demanding attention, from Mark Weiser and John Seely Brown's work at Xerox PARC in the 1990s.
Key idea

For the solo creator

Do

  • Use the same term for the same concept every time.
  • Learn ten terms a week with active recall.

Don't

  • Use jargon in your product's UI; this vocabulary is for thinking, not for users.
  • Use "intuitive" as an explanation. Say what makes it learnable (signifiers, convention, mapping).
  • Treat "clean" as a design quality; say what is aligned, grouped or removed.

How to look at software

Taught by Adam Connor and Aaron Irizarry on critique, with painters' studio habits

Designers see more than other people because they look in a fixed order, with named questions, and write down what they see. Use a ten-point observation sequence on any screen, keep a swipe file of what works, and critique against objectives rather than taste.

Most people look at an app the way they look at a face: all at once, with an overall impression ("nice", "messy"). That impression is real data, but it is not usable. A designer slows the looking down into separate passes, each asking one question. After a few weeks of practice, the passes run in parallel in a few seconds, and you start noticing things you have walked past for years.

The ten-point observation sequence

  1. Purpose. What is this screen for, in one sentence? Whose goal? If you cannot tell, that is the first finding.
  2. First glance. Look for two seconds, look away. What did you see first, second, third? Is that the order the purpose needs?
  3. Hierarchy. How many levels of importance are there? How is each level marked (size, weight, color, position)?
  4. Grid and alignment. Trace the shared edges. Count how many different left edges there are. Fewer is calmer.
  5. Spacing and grouping. Which things look related? Is it because they are near each other, or boxed together, or similar? Are spacing values repeated?
  6. Type. How many families, sizes and weights? What does each one mean?
  7. Color and contrast. What does each color mean? Is the accent reserved for one meaning? Is text contrast sufficient?
  8. Actions and signifiers. What can you do here? What is the primary action? Can you tell what is clickable without hovering?
  9. Words. Read every label. Are they verbs for actions, nouns for things? Do they use the user's language?
  10. States and edges. Imagine it empty, loading, offline, with 10,000 items, with a 120-character title. Which states have been designed?
For the solo creator

Do one ten-point pass a day on an app you use, written in a small notebook or a text file. It takes ten minutes. After a month you will have thirty analyses and, more importantly, the sequence will be automatic. Then run it on your own product once a week. You will see your own screens as a stranger would.

You cannot see your own work fresh, because you know what it is supposed to be. Painters have long used tricks to break that familiarity, such as looking at a canvas in a mirror. Screen equivalents:

Keeping a swipe file

A swipe file is a collection of examples you have found good (or instructively bad), with notes on why. Copywriters have kept them for a century; designers keep them as screenshots. The collection is not the point; the notes are. A screenshot without a note is a souvenir. A screenshot with "empty state teaches the main gesture in one line, uses the same illustration style as onboarding" is a lesson.

A simple system: one folder (or a tool such as Eagle, Are.na or a notes app), file names in the form pattern--product--detail, and a one-sentence note per item naming the mechanism. Patterns to collect first: empty states, onboarding, settings, errors, confirmations and undo, search, lists, paywalls, and any screen that made you feel calm. Reference libraries of app screens, such as Mobbin, are useful for breadth, but your own annotated file is what trains your eye.

The language of critique

Critique is analysis against objectives. In Discussing Design (2015), Adam Connor and Aaron Irizarry distinguish critique from both praise and judgment, and propose a simple structure of questions:

  1. What is the objective of this design (for this screen, for this user, right now)?
  2. Which elements of the design relate to that objective?
  3. Are those elements effective at achieving it?
  4. Why, or why not?

This structure works for self-critique too, and it changes the tone of the conversation you have with yourself. "This is ugly" becomes "The objective is to start reading quickly; the large header pushes the first article below the fold; that delays the main job". The second version contains its own fix.

Key idea

Look in passes, write it down, and judge against the objective. Taste grows from structured looking, not from staring.

Do

  • Use the same ten-point sequence every time until it is automatic.
  • Write a one-line "why it works" note for every swipe-file item.
  • Critique against an objective, in the order objective, elements, effective, why.
  • Use distance tricks on your own screens.

Don't

  • Save screenshots without notes.
  • Judge your own work right after making it.
  • Confuse "I like it" with "it works".
  • Only collect beautiful screens; bad ones teach just as much.

Taste, and how to train it

Taught by Ira Glass, Paul Graham and Benjamin Franklin, with every master you choose to study

Taste is trained judgment: the ability to tell whether a design fits its purpose, and why. It is built by looking closely at a lot of good work, making a lot of your own, and comparing the two honestly. For years your taste will run ahead of your skill. That gap is normal, and the only way through it is volume.

What taste is, and what it is not

Taste is often treated as a mysterious gift or as mere preference. It is neither. Paul Graham's essay "Taste for Makers" (2002) argues that there really is good and bad taste in making things, and lists qualities that good designs tend to share: they are simple, timeless, solve the right problem, look easy, and are often redesigns of something earlier. You can disagree with his list and still accept the main point: taste can be wrong, which means it can improve.

The practical distinction is between preference (what you like) and judgment (what works for this purpose and these people). Good designers hold both and know which they are using. You may personally love dense, dark, information-heavy interfaces, and still recognize that a meditation app should not look like that.

The gap

In a widely shared 2009 video interview about storytelling, the radio host Ira Glass described something every creative beginner meets. Paraphrased: people get into creative work because they have good taste. For the first few years, what they make is not as good as their taste tells them it should be, and they can see it. Many quit at this point. His advice was to do a large volume of work, on deadlines, because only volume closes the gap between your taste and your ability.

Watch out

The gap can also run the other way. If you are never disappointed by your work, your taste may be behind your skill. The fix is the same as for the other gap, in reverse: study more great work, more closely, especially in the top-left quadrant.

Copying as learning

Copying is how most crafts have always been taught. Art students copy paintings in museums. Musicians transcribe solos. Benjamin Franklin, in his Autobiography, describes teaching himself to write by taking notes on essays from The Spectator, setting them aside for a few days, rewriting the essays from his notes, and then comparing his version with the original to find his faults. That is a precise method, and it transfers directly to interface design:

  1. Choose a master screen. A screen from a product widely respected for craft, in a domain close to yours.
  2. Study it with the ten-point sequence (chapter 16) and write notes on every decision you can see.
  3. Wait a day, then rebuild it from your notes, in code or your design tool, without looking at the original.
  4. Compare, side by side or overlaid. Every difference is a lesson: spacing you guessed wrong, a weight you missed, a state you did not notice.
  5. Apply one lesson to your own product. Copy the reasoning, not the pixels.

This is a private exercise for learning, not a way to ship someone else's design. The point is to feel the decisions from the inside. Copy to learn; never ship a copy as your own work.

Studying masters

Choose a small "faculty" of masters and study them over months, not a huge list skimmed once. A balanced faculty for a calm, reader-first software creator might be: one industrial designer (Rams, Fukasawa), one typographer or graphic designer (Vignelli, or the designers behind a well-made typeface), two or three software products known for craft in your domain, and one writer on design (Norman, Victor). Guides 18, 19 and 20 are structured studies of exactly these.

For each master, ask the same three questions: What problem were they solving? What did they refuse to do? What would they remove from my product? The third question is the useful one.

Key idea

Taste is judgment you can train: look closely, make a lot, compare honestly, copy the reasoning, never the surface.

For the solo creator

Without colleagues, your taste is calibrated only by what you choose to look at. Curate it deliberately. Follow fewer, better sources. Use the products you admire for real tasks, not just screenshots. And ship: shipping is the only feedback loop that tests taste against real people. Guide 17, The Solo Practice, turns this into a six-month plan.

Do

  • Separate "I like it" from "it works" in every critique.
  • Rebuild a master screen from notes once a month.
  • Choose a small faculty of masters and study them deeply.
  • Finish and ship many small things; volume closes the gap.

Don't

  • Wait until your skill matches your taste before shipping.
  • Copy styles across products with different purposes.
  • Confuse following trends with having taste.
  • Ship a copy of someone else's design as yours.

Common mistakes

Consuming instead of making

Browsing great work is pleasant and feels like learning.

Keep a ratio: for every hour of looking, two hours of making.

Quitting in the gap

Your work disappoints you, so you conclude you lack talent.

Read the disappointment as evidence your taste is working. Increase output; keep the before/after folder.

Taste as a single style

Admiring minimal products, you make everything minimal.

Ask what each product needs. A data-dense trading tool and a calm reader can both show excellent taste.

Check yourself

You admire a famously minimal note-taking app. You strip all labels and borders from your accounting app to match it, and support tickets go up. What went wrong?

Taste is fit to purpose. The reasoning behind the note app (very few actions, one main task) did not apply to an accounting app with many precise actions. Copy reasoning that transfers, not style.

Wrap-up

You now have the foundation: a definition of the job, a model of the layers, Norman's vocabulary for why people struggle, Rams' qualities to aim for, a shelf of frameworks, a process small enough for one person, and a way of seeing. The other twenty guides build on all of it.

Ten things to remember

  1. Design is the set of decisions; screens are only their record. "Design is how it works."
  2. Products are built in five planes, strategy to surface. Diagnose downward; finish in order.
  3. No options, no design. Generate three, constrain, rank criteria, and slow down only for one-way doors.
  4. People act on signifiers. If something needs a label to explain how to operate it, look for a better signifier.
  5. Map controls to effects, and constrain inputs so errors are impossible rather than reported.
  6. Every action needs immediate, located feedback; the system image is your only way to teach the model.
  7. Diagnose by stage: execution problems need signifiers and mapping; evaluation problems need feedback and visible state.
  8. Slips and mistakes need different fixes. Undo beats confirmation, almost always.
  9. Rams' ten are about respect, not minimalism; Nielsen, Shneiderman and Tog are lenses for review; Cooper, Raskin and Victor push toward asking less of people.
  10. Sketch to explore, prototype to test, code to confirm. Spend craft on the hot path. Train taste with structured looking and a large volume of finished work.

Reading list

  • The Design of Everyday Things (revised and expanded edition)
    Don Norman, 2013 · The source for Part two. Read the chapters on the psychology of everyday actions and on human error twice.
  • The Elements of User Experience (2nd edition)
    Jesse James Garrett, 2010 · Short, and the clearest map of the five planes, with the full diagram.

    Dieter Rams, 1995 · Rams in his own words, with his principles and many Braun products. Pair with Gary Hustwit's documentary Rams (2018).
  • The Humane Interface
    Jef Raskin, 2000 · Modes, attention and habituation, argued with rigor. Some technical sections can be skimmed.
  • Magic Ink: Information Software and the Graphical Interface
    Bret Victor, 2006 (free essay on worrydream.com) · The case that most software should show, not ask. Will change how you design forms.
  • About Face: The Essentials of Interaction Design
    Alan Cooper and co-authors, 4th edition 2014 · Goal-directed design, personas done properly, and a huge catalogue of interaction patterns.
  • Sketching User Experiences
    Bill Buxton, 2007 · Why sketching is different from prototyping, and why designers need many cheap options.

    Ryan Singer, 2019 (free online at basecamp.com/shapeup) · Appetites, shaping and fixed-time cycles. Written for small teams; ideal for one person.
