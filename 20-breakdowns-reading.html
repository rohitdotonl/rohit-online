Breakdowns: Reading. How the best reading products get out of the way.

Reading products are the purest test of product design: the job is to put words in front of a person and then disappear. This guide takes apart fifteen of them, from the Kindle to Wikipedia to a feed reader, decision by decision, so you can see what each one chose, what it cost, and what you can steal for your own software.

Warm-up: what do you already notice?

You read on screens for hours a day, so you already hold strong opinions about reading products. Most of them are unspoken. This guide makes them speakable. Before we start, try two questions. Don't look anything up; the point is to find out what your eye already catches.

Diagnostic 1

You open a novel on an e-reader. Which single decision most affects whether you are still reading 40 minutes later?

Defaults decide the experience for most readers, most of the time. Usability research on software settings has long found that most people rarely change defaults. A great reading product earns its reader in the first screen. Settings matter, but they are the safety net, not the floor.
Diagnostic 2

A read-later app shows "347 unread" in red on its icon. What is the most likely effect on a calm reader over a month?

A count frames reading as a debt. That is exactly why several readers in this guide (Reeder's 2023 redesign, Readwise Reader's triage, Instapaper's quiet list) chose to soften or hide the number. You'll see the trade-off in detail in the RSS chapter.

Part one

The lens

Before we take products apart, we need a shared vocabulary. Two chapters: a scorecard of ten criteria you can apply to any reading product, and a method for watching a reading session as it actually happens rather than as the marketing page describes it.

What makes a reading product great

Taught by Oliver Reichenstein, with Matthew Butterick and Craig Mod

A reading product is great when it gives the text the best possible surface, then removes itself, and only comes back when you need to find, mark, move or keep something.

Most software wants your attention. A reading product wants you to give your attention to something else: the book, the article, the essay. That inversion is why reading products are such a good school for designers. Every pixel of chrome, every badge, every recommendation competes with the thing the person came for. The best ones are almost invisible, and the work behind that invisibility is enormous.

Oliver Reichenstein of iA put it in one line in 2006: web design is 95% typography. For a reading product the number is closer to 100% while you are reading, and then drops sharply in the other moments: opening the app, finding a book, looking up a word, coming back on your phone. A useful scorecard has to cover both: the surface (how the text looks) and the system (everything around it).

The ten criteria

Here is the lens we will use for the whole guide. Each criterion is scored 0 to 3. Zero means absent or harmful, one means present but weak, two means good, three means best in class: other products should copy it.

1 · Surface typography
Typeface designed for long reading at screen resolution, sensible size, a measure (line length) of roughly 45 to 75 characters, leading around 1.4 to 1.6, real hyphenation if text is justified, proper quotes and dashes, no widows of one word on a page. See Typography for the underlying rules.
2 · Defaults
How good it is untouched. Most people never open settings, so the default is the product.
3 · Controls
Can you change what actually matters (size, theme, spacing, margins, face) in one or two taps, with a live preview, without breaking the layout?
4 · Navigation & progress
Do you know where you are and how much is left, in units that mean something to you? Can you jump to a chapter and jump back?
5 · Annotation
Highlighting and notes that are quick to make, easy to find again, and travel with you.
6 · Sync & continuity
Your place, highlights and settings follow you across devices and even formats (text to audio).
7 · Library & collection
Finding, sorting, grouping and pruning what you own or saved. A library that grows past 500 items without collapsing.
8 · Discovery vs distraction
Helping you find the next thing to read without pulling you out of the current one.
9 · Accessibility
Wide size range, high contrast options, dyslexia-friendly options, screen reader and text-to-speech support, one-handed use. See Accessible & Inclusive.
10 · Ownership & export
Open formats, DRM-free where possible, highlights and saves you can export in a usable format. The test: if the company died tomorrow, what would you lose?

    Why ten, and why 0 to 3

    Ten is enough to separate products and small enough to finish in a single sitting. A four-point scale forces a decision: there is no neutral middle to hide in. If you are tempted to give a 2, ask "would I tell another designer to copy this?" If yes, it's a 3. If you'd say "it's fine", it's a 2. If you'd say "it's there, but…", it's a 1.

    Scores are not the point. The observations behind them are. A score of 1 on Navigation is useless; "the progress bar shows percentage only, and I can't tell how long the chapter is" is a design insight you can act on.

    Do

    • Score after a real reading session of at least ten minutes, not a tour of the settings.
    • Write one observation per criterion before the number.
    • Score the product you would actually use (device or app), and say which.
    • Re-score after a week: the system criteria only show up over time.

    Don't

    • Reward feature counts. Twelve fonts can score lower than three good ones.
    • Score from screenshots or reviews. Reading is felt over minutes.
    • Average away a 0. One zero (say, no export) can outweigh three 3s for some readers.

    For the solo creator

    You may not be building an e-reader, but almost every product has a reading surface: docs, onboarding, release notes, a help center, long settings descriptions, an email digest. Run the scorecard on that surface of your own product. Usually 1, 2 and 4 are cheap to fix in an afternoon (better type, better default size, a progress hint), and they're the ones people feel most.

    Common mistakes

    Confusing "settings" with "controls"

    A long settings screen looks like control. But if changing the font requires leaving the page, you can't judge the result.

    Score controls on how fast you reach the setting and whether you see the change live on the page you're reading.

    Scoring the brand, not the product

    We like or dislike companies, and it leaks into scores.

    Write the observation first, with the device and the exact screen. If you can't write one, you can't score it.

    Check yourself

    An app offers 14 fonts, 6 themes and 30 sliders, but the default is 14px sans-serif at full screen width on a tablet. How should it score on Defaults and Controls?

    A full-width line on a tablet can run to 120+ characters, far past a comfortable measure, at a size too small for long reading. That default fails most readers, who will never open settings. Controls are judged on whether the right changes are quick and visible, not on how many exist.

    How to take a reading session apart

    Taught by Don Norman, with Craig Mod

    A reading product is not a screen; it is a session with six moments, and each moment has a different design job.

    When you take apart a reading app, the temptation is to look at the reading page and stop. But people don't experience a page; they experience a session that starts before the page and ends after it. Breaking the session into moments shows you where products actually differ. Two e-readers with identical text rendering can feel completely different because one opens straight to your book and the other opens to a store.

    The protocol

    1. Cold open. Kill the app. Open it. Count the taps and seconds until you are reading the sentence you left. Note everything on screen in between.
    2. Settle. Change one thing (size or theme). Count taps. Is the change previewed live? Does your place on the page survive the reflow?
    3. Immerse for ten minutes. Don't analyse. Afterward, write what you remember of the interface. If you remember nothing, that's a compliment.
    4. Mark. Highlight a passage, add a note, look up a word. Then try to find that highlight again from the library.
    5. Leave mid-chapter. Close it while partway through a page.
    6. Return elsewhere. Open the same item on a second device (or the web). Where does it put you? Does it ask?
    Key idea

    Measure a reading product by what you remember of its interface after ten minutes of reading. The less, the better.

    Do

    • Time the cold open and the return. They are the moments where products differ most.
    • Test with a real book or article you want to read; boredom hides problems.
    • Note the units of progress each product uses (pages, %, location, minutes).

    Don't

    • Judge only the reading page.
    • Test on a fresh install only; libraries and highlights behave differently at 300 items.
    • Skip the second device. Continuity is half of modern reading.

    In the wild

    • Kindle e-readersAmazon
      The home screen has gone back and forth over the years between a plain list of your books and a mix of your library with store recommendations. The Arrive moment is where business goals push hardest into reading.
    • Apple BooksApple, 2010–
      Opens to a Home tab with "Continue" at the top: the book you were reading is the first thing you see. Put the most likely next action first.
    • Reader modesSafari, Firefox, Edge
      Exist purely to fix the Immerse moment on web pages that fail it. When a whole category of tool exists to remove your interface, your interface is too loud.
    For the solo creator

    You can run this protocol on your own app in 20 minutes with a stopwatch and a notes file. Do it on your phone, not your dev machine: you'll catch the Return moment (state restoration, scroll position, deep links) that you never see when your laptop keeps everything warm.

    Common mistakes

    Analysing while immersing

    If you watch the UI while reading, you notice everything and feel nothing.

    Read first, analyse after. Write down what you remember, then go back and look.

    Forgetting state restoration

    On your own device, apps stay warm in memory. Real readers come back after the OS killed the app.

    Force-quit before every cold open test.

    Check yourself

    A news app shows a bottom tab bar, a floating "Listen" button and a "Related stories" carousel that slides in at 50% scroll. Which moment does it hurt most, and what is the smallest fix?

    All three elements live in the middle of the session, competing with the text. Collapsing chrome on scroll-down (and showing it on scroll-up) and placing discovery at the natural end of the article restores immersion without removing features.

    Books on screens

Their problems are typesetting without a typesetter, progress without pages, and continuity across devices. Their business models (selling books, selling devices, lending books for free) shape every screen.

    Kindle

    Kindle's big idea is not the e-ink screen; it is the system around it: your place, notes and dictionary follow you everywhere, so the book becomes a service you can open anywhere.

    MakerAmazon
    Since2007 (first device)
    CategoryE-reader device + apps
    Study it forSync, progress units, reading aids, the store tension

    When the first Kindle shipped in November 2007 it was an odd beige slab with a keyboard. What made it matter was the wireless delivery: you bought a book and it appeared in about a minute, with no computer involved. Everything Kindle has done since builds on that idea of the book as a live, connected object. Your reading position syncs (Whispersync). Your highlights live in the cloud. The dictionary is built in. The text knows what's in it (X-Ray). Each of these is a design decision with a cost, and Kindle is the best place to study those costs because it has been making them for almost twenty years.

    1. Typesetting that respects the book. In 2015 Amazon introduced Bookerly, a typeface it commissioned from Dalton Maag for reading on its screens, together with a new typesetting engine (marketed as enhanced typesetting) that improved spacing, kerning and later hyphenation, and added drop caps. Before that, justified Kindle text often had visible "rivers" of white space because there was no hyphenation. The cost: older books and badly made files don't benefit equally, so quality varies from book to book.
    2. Justified text with hyphenation by default. Print books are justified, so Kindle matches the expectation. Justification only works well with hyphenation and a long enough line; at large font sizes on a small screen, gaps return. Kindle lets you switch to left-aligned (ragged right), which is the safer choice at big sizes.
    3. Popular Highlights. Since around 2010 Kindle has aggregated highlights across readers and shows passages many people marked. It's a fascinating social signal, and it raised a long-running privacy question: your highlights are data Amazon collects. It can also steer your attention ("I should notice this"), which some readers find spoils the book. You can turn it off. The cost of a default here is ethical as much as visual. See Honest & Calm.
    4. Reading aids at the word level. Tap a word for the dictionary and Wikipedia. Word Wise (2014) puts short definitions above harder words, with a slider for how many words get hints. Vocabulary Builder collects every word you looked up into flashcards. Together they turn a lookup into learning, without leaving the page.
    5. Time left, not just pages. Kindle learns your reading speed and can show "time left in chapter" or "time left in book". This answers the question readers actually ask at 11pm ("can I finish this chapter before sleep?"). The estimate needs a few pages of data before it's good.
    6. Locations, then pages. Reflowable text has no fixed pages, so Kindle invented locations: fixed positions in the file that don't change with font size. They are stable for citations and sync, but meaningless to humans. Many books now map to real print page numbers, shown when available. Percentage is always there.
    7. X-Ray. Introduced in 2011, X-Ray shows the characters, places and terms in a book and where they appear. It solves "who is this again?" in long novels without spoilers past your position. It needs per-book data, so not every book has it.
    8. Whispersync and Page Flip. Whispersync keeps your furthest page, bookmarks and notes in sync across devices, and asks before jumping you forward. Page Flip (2016) lets you peek at other pages in a thumbnail view while your place stays pinned, the digital version of keeping a finger in the book.

    Trade-offs and criticisms

    • The store in the library. Kindle's home screen and lock screen (on ad-supported models) carry recommendations and offers. It pays for cheaper hardware, but it puts the store into the Arrive moment of every session.
    • Closed format. Kindle books are generally tied to Amazon's apps and devices. Amazon has added EPUB support to Send to Kindle (converted on the way in), but the purchased library stays inside the ecosystem.
    • Highlight export is limited. Notes and highlights are viewable on the web and some can be exported, often with publisher limits on how much. Tools like Readwise exist partly to fill that gap.

    Steal this for your software

    • Progress in time, learned from the user's own pace, beats progress in abstract units.
    • When syncing a position forward, ask ("Go to page 212, the furthest you read on your phone?") rather than silently jump.
    • Lookups that stay on the page (popover, not navigation) keep people in flow.
    • Turn incidental behaviour into value: every looked-up word becomes a flashcard.
    • Make social signals (popular highlights, reading counts) opt-in or easy to switch off.

    Do

    • Offer progress in at least one human unit (minutes or real pages).
    • Keep a "peek" mode that never loses the reader's place.
    • Give reading aids an intensity control, like Word Wise's slider.

    Don't

    • Show a raw internal unit (locations, offsets) as the only progress.
    • Put commerce on the screen people see every time they open their book.
    • Collect reading behaviour silently.

    In the wild

    • Kindle PaperwhiteAmazon, 2012–
      Front-lit e-ink made the Kindle usable in bed without a lamp, and later models added warm light. Hardware decisions are reading-design decisions: light colour affects how long people read at night.
    • Kindle Vocabulary BuilderAmazon
      Collects words you looked up, with the sentence they came from, as flashcards. Context is the memory hook; keep the sentence.
    • Kindle NotebookAmazon
      A per-book list of highlights and notes, filterable, linking back to the passage. Annotation is only useful if you can find it again.
    For the solo creator

    The "time left" estimate is cheap to build: words remaining divided by a reading speed (start with about 230–250 words per minute, then learn from the user's actual pace). Any long page in your product (docs, a lesson, a changelog) can show "4 min" honestly. Don't fake precision: round to whole minutes and hide the estimate below one minute.

    Common mistakes

    Copying Kindle's justification without its engine

    CSS text-align: justify looks bookish in a mock-up and terrible on a phone, because browsers hyphenate poorly or not at all unless asked.

    On the web, use left alignment, or justify only with hyphens: auto, a correct lang attribute and a measure of at least 60 characters.

    Jumping the reader without asking

    Sync that moves you to "furthest read" can be wrong (someone else used your account, you peeked ahead).

    Offer the jump; don't force it. Kindle and Apple Books both ask.

    Check yourself

    You build a course app with long lessons. Users complain they never know if they can finish a lesson on their commute. What do you add first?

    The user's question is about time, so answer in time. Percent and word counts make the user do the conversion. Learning their own pace (as Kindle does) makes the estimate personal and more trustworthy.

    Kobo

    Kobo bets that serious readers want control and openness: more typographic settings than anyone, open EPUB files, library borrowing and read-later articles built in.

    MakerRakuten Kobo (Rakuten since 2012)
    Since2009 (as Kobo)
    CategoryE-reader devices + apps + store
    Study it forDeep controls, open formats, physical buttons

    If Kindle is the system, Kobo is the toolbox. Its e-readers have long given readers controls that other makers hide or skip: font weight and sharpness sliders, fine margin and line spacing steps, justification options, and the ability to load your own fonts. It reads EPUB, the open standard most of the publishing world uses, so a DRM-free book from anywhere just works. It has built-in library lending through OverDrive in many regions. And for years its devices had Pocket built in, so web articles you saved appeared next to your books; when Pocket closed in 2025, Kobo moved that role to Instapaper in a software update later that year.

    1. A half-sheet, not a settings page. Kobo's type settings slide up over the bottom of the page. The text stays visible, so you see the change as you make it. This is the single most important pattern for reading controls.
    2. More steps, finer steps. Size, line spacing and margins each have many increments. A reader with low vision or a love of tight text can both get exactly what they want.
    3. Weight and sharpness. E-ink renders thin fonts faintly, especially on older screens. Kobo's Advanced sliders let you thicken strokes and sharpen edges. It solves a real hardware problem with a typographic control rather than a hardware fix.
    4. Buttons for one-handed reading. The Kobo Libra line (2019 onward) has physical page-turn buttons on a wide side grip, and the device flips its layout for left or right hand. Tapping a screen with a thumb stretched across it is a tiring, error-prone gesture; a button under the thumb isn't.
    5. Open by default. EPUB support means DRM-free books from Standard Ebooks, independent authors or publishers like Tor work without conversion. Kobo also lets you sideload fonts. Openness is a feature for the reader and a moat-reducer for the company.

    About 62 characters per line
    Try it. Push size to 28 with small margins, then turn on justification and watch the word gaps. Then find a setting you would pick as the default for everyone. The line counter shows when you leave the comfortable 45–75 character range.

    Trade-offs

    Many settings means many combinations, and some of them are ugly. Kobo accepts that cost for its audience of keen readers. Its defaults are good, which is what keeps the controls from becoming a burden. The other trade-off is ecosystem: Kobo's store is smaller in some markets, and its sync between devices and apps has historically been less seamless than Kindle's.

    Steal this for your software

    • Any appearance setting should preview live, on the real content, in a sheet that leaves the content visible.
    • Hide expert controls under one "Advanced" label rather than removing them or showing them to everyone.
    • Use open, standard formats for anything the user owns; it builds trust and lets other tools help you.
    • Partner instead of build: Kobo didn't make a read-later service; it integrated one, then swapped partners when one closed.

    Do

    • Give fine steps for size; low-vision readers need large sizes that still lay out well.
    • Remember settings per device, and offer a reset to defaults.
    • Plan for partners disappearing: keep the integration replaceable.

    Don't

    • Put type settings on a separate screen with no preview.
    • Expose controls you haven't tested at their extremes.
    • Treat physical ergonomics as someone else's job; in software, it's thumb reach and tap zones.

    In the wild

    • Kobo Libra seriesRakuten Kobo, 2019–
      Asymmetric body with page buttons; screen rotates for either hand. Design for how the device is held for an hour, not how it looks in a photo.
    • Kobo + OverDriveRakuten Kobo
      Borrow library ebooks directly on the device in supported regions. Integrate the source people already use, not just your store.
    • Kobo + Instapaper2025
      Replaced the Pocket integration after Pocket closed. Integrations are dependencies; design so one can be swapped.
    For the solo creator

    A preview-while-you-change settings sheet is one of the cheapest delights to build: it's a bottom sheet with three sliders bound to CSS variables. If your product has any theme or density setting, build it this way rather than as a list of options on a separate screen.

    Common mistakes

    Sliders without sensible ranges

    A margin slider that goes to zero or a line-height that goes below 1.1 creates broken pages that users blame on the app.

    Set ranges you have tested; every position should produce something readable.

    Settings that lose your place

    Reflowing text after a size change can drop the reader pages away.

    Anchor on the first visible word before reflow and scroll it back to the top after.

    Check yourself

    Your markdown notes app has a theme picker on a separate Preferences window. Users rarely change it, and the ones who do often switch back. What is the most likely cause, and fix?

    Appearance is judged on real content. A separate window forces guess-and-check. Kobo's half-sheet teaches the fix: keep the content visible and apply changes live.

    Apple Books

    Apple Books shows the full arc of a design idea: it began as a vivid imitation of paper books, stripped that away, and kept only what helped reading, ending with themes that bundle many settings into one choice.

    MakerApple
    Since2010 (as iBooks, with the first iPad)
    CategoryEbook and audiobook app
    Study it forSkeuomorphism's rise and fall, themes as presets, reading goals

    iBooks launched with the iPad in 2010 and its demo was unforgettable: a wooden bookshelf, and pages that curled under your finger, following it in real time, with the next page showing through. It taught a whole generation that a tablet could hold books. Over the next decade the wood went away (the iOS 7 redesign of 2013 flattened nearly everything), the app was renamed Apple Books in 2018 with a "Reading Now" home, reading goals arrived in 2019, and in 2022 the reading view was rebuilt around a small set of themes. Following that arc is a lesson in when metaphors help and when they get in the way.

    1. The page curl as an explanation. In 2010, nobody knew how to read on a tablet. A page that bent under your finger made the gesture self-evident: grab the corner, pull. It was a teaching device as much as an animation. See Motion & Feel for why direct manipulation feels right.
    2. Dropping the curl as the default. In the 2022 redesign the default turn became a quicker slide; after complaints, the curl came back as an option. Once a gesture is learned, a heavy animation costs more (time, visual noise) than it teaches. Keeping it optional respects people who love it.
    3. Continue first. The home tab puts the current book (and audiobook) at the top with progress. The Arrive moment is one tap.
    4. Themes as presets. Instead of asking readers to tune five settings, the 2022 reading view offers a handful of named themes that set face, weight, spacing and background together, each customisable. A preset is a decision made on the reader's behalf that they can still undo. Good presets carry a design opinion; that's the point.
    5. Scrolling view. Apple Books offers a continuous vertical scroll as an alternative to pages. Some people (and some content, like technical books) read better in scroll; pages give stronger landmarks ("it was near the top of a left page").
    6. Reading goals. A daily minutes target (5 minutes by default, changeable) and a streak. Small default, real habit. Goals are a calm motivator only if they are easy to switch off and don't nag. See Honest & Calm.
    7. Books and audiobooks in one library. The same app holds both, with a shared "Continue" row. Listening is treated as reading, not a separate product.
    Pages vs scroll
    Paged: fixed landmarks (left page, near the top, page 212) help spatial memory and make progress feel chunked. A page turn is a tiny reward.
    Scrolling: no breaks mid-sentence and natural for code, tables and long figures, but fewer landmarks; your place is a scroll offset. Offer both, default to the one that suits the content.

    Steal this for your software

    • Use a metaphor while it teaches; drop it when it only decorates. Keep the beloved version as an option.
    • Bundle settings into named presets that each make a complete, tested design.
    • Put "continue where you left off" first on any home screen of a long-form product.
    • Offer pages and scroll; pick the default by content type.

    Do

    • Name presets by feeling or purpose ("Quiet", "Focus"), not by values.
    • Keep goals small by default and easy to turn off.
    • Treat audio and text as two views of one item.

    Don't

    • Keep a metaphor because it demoed well once.
    • Remove a loved animation without leaving an option; Apple had to bring the curl back.
    • Make streaks punitive.

    Presets are a solo maker's friend: three themes you tested thoroughly cost less to support than a dozen independent toggles that produce combinations you've never seen. Start with presets; add a "customise" panel only when people ask for it.

    Skeuomorphism as nostalgia

    Paper textures and curls feel warm in a mock-up.

    Presets that are just colours

    Libby

    Libby makes a complicated system (library cards, licences, waitlists, loan periods, formats) feel like a friendly app anyone can use, by hiding the machinery behind warm words and very few choices.

    MakerOverDrive
    Since2017
    CategoryPublic library ebooks and audiobooks
    Study it forApproachable design for all ages, waitlist UX, voice

    Borrowing an ebook from a library is a strange transaction. Libraries license digital copies, often one reader at a time, for a limited number of loans or months. So there are waitlists for ebooks, which surprises people. The older OverDrive app exposed much of this machinery. Libby, launched in 2017, kept the same back end and redesigned the experience around one person with a library card and a phone. OverDrive later retired its older app in favour of Libby. Its users range from teenagers to readers in their 80s, so it's a valuable study in designing for everyone.

    1. Plain words for a complicated system. "Loans", "Holds", "Due in 9 days", "You're #4 in line". Libby's copy is warm and a little playful in its onboarding, but on the shelf it's plain and specific. Older readers don't need to learn library software jargon.
    2. Honest waits. A hold shows your place in line, how many copies the library owns and an estimated wait. Uncertainty is shown, not hidden. People tolerate waiting far better when they can see why.
    3. Deliver later. If a hold comes in when you're busy, you can let the next person go ahead while keeping your place near the front. It turns a rigid queue into a polite one. It's a lovely example of designing the edge case.
    4. Tags instead of folders. You can tag any book you see, Libby adds a few automatic "smart" tags for things like books you have sampled or borrowed, and tags work across your libraries and searches. A flexible structure with almost no learning cost.
    5. Read in Libby, or send elsewhere. Libby has its own reader and audio player. In the US, many ebooks can also be sent to a Kindle. Libby meets readers on the device they already love.

    Steal this for your software

    • Translate system states into human positions and times ("#4 in line, about 2 weeks").
    • Design the "not now" branch for any arrival: snooze, defer, deliver later.
    • Limit primary navigation to four or five destinations for wide-audience apps.
    • Integrate with the reading device people already have.

    Do

    • Write for your oldest likely user; it helps everyone.
    • Show estimates with their uncertainty.
    • Use tags when users' categories will vary.

    Don't

    • Expose licensing, back-end or format jargon on primary screens.
    • Force an immediate decision when something becomes available.
    • Hide where the user is in a queue.

    Standard Ebooks and Project Gutenberg

    The same public-domain text can be a chore or a pleasure to read; the difference is a thousand small typographic decisions, and Standard Ebooks writes every one of them down.

    MakersProject Gutenberg (Michael Hart, 1971); Standard Ebooks (volunteer project led by Alex Cabal)
    CategoryFree public-domain ebooks
    Study it forTypography as craft, style manuals, semantic markup

    Project Gutenberg is the oldest digital library: Michael Hart typed the US Declaration of Independence into a mainframe in 1971 and shared it. Since then volunteers have transcribed tens of thousands of public-domain books. Its focus has always been access: get the text out, free, in simple formats. Standard Ebooks, a newer volunteer project, takes those transcriptions (and scans) and produces carefully typeset, consistently formatted ebooks with modern conventions, semantic markup and a public Manual of Style that explains every rule. Compare the same novel from both and you'll see what "typography is invisible" really means.

    1. Write the rules down. Standard Ebooks publishes a Manual of Style covering punctuation, typography, semantics, metadata and file structure. Every volunteer follows the same rules, so every book feels like part of one edition. A style manual is a design system for prose.
    2. Semantic markup first. Text is marked up for what it is (a letter, a poem, a quotation, an abbreviation), not how it looks. That makes books work across readers, with screen readers, and when styles change later.
    3. Modernised, consistently. Some archaic spellings are updated according to documented rules, so a reader isn't tripped by "to-day" or inconsistent hyphenation, while the author's words are kept. The rules say what is changed and what is left alone.
    4. Covers from public-domain art. Each book gets a cover using a public-domain painting with a consistent title treatment. A library of them looks like a collection, not a pile.
    5. Free, DRM-free, multiple formats. EPUB, Kindle-compatible files and others. The books scorecard a full 3 on Ownership.
    6. Gutenberg's choice: access over polish. Project Gutenberg's plain-text-first approach meant texts survived decades of format changes. Its HTML and EPUB editions have improved a lot over the years, but its core value is breadth and permanence. Both choices are right for their goals.

    CHAPTER I.

    "It is a truth universally acknowledged," said he--quoting, as he
    so often did, from _some book or other_--"that a man in possession
    of a good fortune must be in want of a wife." It was 1811-1812.

    I

    Rough: hard line breaks from the source, straight quotes, double hyphens, underscores for italic, monospace. Every flaw costs a fraction of a second of attention.
    Typeset: reflowing text, a reading serif, curly quotes, real dashes, italics, an en dash in the range and a clean heading. (Austen's opening line, adapted as a typesetting sample.)

    Steal this for your software

    • Write a one-page style manual for your product's text: quotes, dashes, numbers, dates, capitalisation, units.
    • Automate the fixable parts (smart quotes, en dashes in ranges, non-breaking spaces before units) in your rendering pipeline.
    • Mark up meaning, then style it; your future redesigns get cheaper.
    • Make a collection look like a collection: consistent covers, consistent metadata.

    Do

    • Use typographer's quotes and apostrophes everywhere text is shown.
    • Use en dashes for ranges, and real ellipses and multiplication signs.
    • Link notes both ways: to the note and back.

    Don't

    • Render user text with straight quotes because "that's what they typed".
    • Encode styling into content (underscores, ALL CAPS for emphasis).
    • Fix typography by hand, one screen at a time.

    In the wild

    • Standard Ebooks Manual of Stylestandardebooks.org
      Public, detailed, versioned rules for every book. Write the decisions down once; let many hands produce one voice.
    • Project Gutenberg1971–
      Decades of free texts in durable formats. Permanence and access are design goals too.
    • SmartyPantsJohn Gruber, 2002
      A small tool that converts straight quotes and dashes to typographic ones, used in many publishing systems. Automate typography where you can.
    For the solo creator

    You can add a typography pass to almost any product in an hour: a small function that converts quotes, dashes and ellipses before rendering user-facing copy, plus a style list for your own writing. Your product will look more finished without a single visual redesign. See Words for the writing side.

    Common mistakes

    Smart quotes in code

    A global "smart" conversion also curls quotes inside code blocks and breaks them.

    Skip code, URLs and inputs in your typography pass.

    Notes that strand the reader

    Endnote links that jump to the end with no way back lose the reader's place.

    Every note needs a return link, or show it in a popover (see Gwern.net in chapter 17).

    Check yourself

    Your blogging tool lets authors write in Markdown. Readers say posts "look amateur" but can't say why. Which change is most likely to help, with least effort?

    Instapaper

    Instapaper turned a habit (leaving tabs open to read later) into a product with one button and one beautiful text view, and proved that people would pay for reading done well.

    MakerMarco Arment (2008); later Betaworks, Pinterest, now independent
    Since2008
    CategoryRead-later service and apps
    Study it forOne-button capture, text view, paid simplicity

    Marco Arment built Instapaper in 2008 as a side project: a "Read Later" bookmarklet that saved a page to a list, and a way to read that list later, especially offline on the then-new iPhone. The key insight was splitting reading into two moments that happen at different times: finding (at your desk, busy) and reading (on the train, in bed, unhurried). The app's text view stripped each article down to words and set them beautifully. Arment charged for the app instead of filling it with ads, and that business choice kept the product calm. He sold a majority of it to Betaworks in 2013; Pinterest bought it in 2016; since 2018 it has been run independently.

    1. One button, no decisions. Saving asks nothing: no folder, no tag, no note. The moment of finding is the busiest moment, so the cost there must be near zero. Organisation can happen later, if ever.
    2. A list that tells you length. Each item shows the source and an estimate of reading time. Length is the main thing you need to pick what to read now.
    3. Text view as the product. The extraction (getting the article out of the page) and the typesetting of the result are the core. Instapaper offered a small set of good fonts, size, line spacing, margins and themes, in a compact settings popover over the text.
    4. Paid, therefore calm. Arment's model was simple: people pay, so the product serves readers rather than advertisers. Instapaper later moved to a free tier plus Premium (with features like full-text search and unlimited notes). The business decision shows up on every screen as an absence of ads and engagement tricks.
    5. Speed Reading. Instapaper added a speed reading mode that flashes one word at a time at a chosen pace. It's a fascinating experiment and a useful lesson: it raises speed by removing the eye's ability to go back, which hurts comprehension for many texts. A feature can be clever and still be a niche tool.

    Steal this for your software

    • Separate capture from processing. Make capture one action, with no required fields.
    • Show length (time) in lists of long items.
    • Let your business model protect your reading surface; if you charge, don't also sell attention.
    • Treat experiments like speed reading as optional tools, not the main experience.

    Do

    • Make "save" work from everywhere: share sheet, browser extension, email.
    • Download content for offline reading by default.
    • Show the source domain so people can judge trust.

    Don't

    • Ask users to organise at save time.
    • Show article images by default if they slow down reading or offline sync.
    • Fill the list with recommendations the user didn't save.

    Extraction fails on some sites, dropping images, code or tables that matter.

    Pocket

    Pocket made read-later mainstream, with saving from everywhere and a recommendation engine, and then its closure in 2025 taught millions of people what ownership and export are worth.

    MakerNate Weiner (as Read It Later, 2007); Mozilla from 2017
    Lifespan2007–2025
    CategoryRead-later + discovery
    Study it forUbiquitous capture, discovery, the end of a product

    Pocket started as Read It Later, a Firefox extension, in 2007. In 2012 it was renamed Pocket and relaunched as a free app with a cleaner design. It grew huge by being everywhere: browser extensions, a share-sheet action in thousands of apps, an email address you could send links to, and built-in integrations (Kobo e-readers among them). Mozilla bought it in 2017 and put Pocket recommendations into the Firefox new tab page. In May 2025 Mozilla announced it would shut Pocket down; the service closed in July 2025, with a window to export data that ran into October. This chapter is half breakdown, half obituary, because the ending is the lesson.

    1. Free and everywhere. Becoming free in 2012 and integrating with as many apps as possible made Pocket the default "save" target for a decade. Distribution was the design.
    2. Discovery built in. Pocket used what people saved to surface popular and recommended articles, later inside Firefox. This helped people find good reading, and blurred the line between "my list" and "a feed".
    3. Tags, highlights, listen. Pocket added tags, highlights and text-to-speech. Useful, but each made the data more valuable to the user, and therefore made export more important.
    4. The ending. Mozilla gave notice, kept export open for months after the service stopped, and pointed people at alternatives. That's a decent ending as endings go. But many people discovered that their tags, highlights and years of saved reading lived in a format other apps imported only partly.

    Steal this for your software

    • Count your "ways in" and make each one work: share sheet, email, extension, API.
    • Ship export on day one, including the user's metadata (tags, notes, timestamps), in plain formats.
    • Write your shutdown plan now: notice period, export window, recommended alternatives.
    • Keep "my list" and "suggestions" visually and structurally separate.

    Do

    • Test your export by importing it into a competitor.
    • Include everything the user made, not just URLs.
    • Give generous notice if you ever close.

    Don't

    • Treat export as an admin feature hidden in support docs.
    • Mix recommendations into a personal list.
    • Assume a big parent company means permanence.

    In the wild

    • Google ReaderGoogle, 2005–2013
      Its 2013 shutdown pushed millions to Feedly and others; Google Takeout let people export subscriptions as OPML. An open export format (OPML) made the migration possible.
    • Pocket shutdownMozilla, 2025
      Announced in May, service ended in July, with an export window into October. Notice and export are the minimum ethical ending.
    • ObsidianDynalist Inc., 2020–
      Stores notes as plain Markdown files in your folders. The strongest export is no lock-in at all.
    For the solo creator

    As a solo maker, your users' biggest unspoken fear is that you'll stop. Export is your answer. A visible "Export everything" button, and a sentence in your FAQ about what happens if you shut down, can win customers who would otherwise choose a bigger company. See Honest & Calm.

    Common mistakes

    Exporting only the obvious column

    A list of URLs feels like an export. It loses tags, highlights, dates and read status, which are the user's real work.

    Export every field the user created or changed.

    Proprietary-only formats

    A JSON dump in your own schema is technically an export, but no other tool reads it.

    Offer at least one standard or human-readable format alongside the full dump.

    You run a small highlights app with 2,000 paying users. Which single change does most for user trust?

    Trust in a small product rests on "what happens if it ends". Pocket's closure made this concrete for many readers. A visible, complete, standard export answers it.

    Readwise Reader

    Readwise Reader treats everything you read (articles, newsletters, PDFs, ebooks, feeds, video transcripts) as one stream with one set of tools, and then helps you remember it through daily review.

    MakerReadwise
    SinceReadwise (highlights) late 2010s; Reader beta 2022
    CategoryRead-later, feeds, annotation, review
    Study it forUnified inbox, triage, keyboard-first power use, spaced review

    Readwise began as a service that pulled your Kindle highlights (and later highlights from many apps) into one place and resurfaced a few each day by email or in the app: a Daily Review. Reader, launched in public beta in 2022, is the reading app built to feed that system. Its premise is that the boundaries between "articles", "PDFs", "newsletters" and "feeds" are artificial. To a reader they're all text to triage, read, mark and remember. Reader is dense and powerful, and it is a useful counterpoint to the minimal products in this guide: power done thoughtfully.

    1. Library vs Feed. Things you chose to save go to the Library; things that arrive by subscription (RSS, newsletters) go to the Feed. This draws a firm line between "I decided this" and "this arrived", which keeps the Library from flooding.
    2. Triage states, not folders. Inbox, Later, Shortlist, Archive. Each item is in exactly one state, and moving between them is one key. It's email triage applied to reading: decide quickly, read deliberately.
    3. Every format, one toolset. Highlighting a PDF, an EPUB, a web article or a video transcript works the same way, and all highlights flow to the same place. One mental model across formats.
    4. Keyboard first, mouse welcome. Nearly every action has a shortcut, with a command palette to find them. Power users can triage dozens of items a minute; newcomers can still click. See Breakdowns: Software for the same idea in Linear and Superhuman.
    5. AI at the side. Ghostreader offers summaries, definitions, simplification and questions about the document, using a language model. It lives in the side panel and in explicit commands, not as text injected into what you're reading. See Designing with AI.
    6. Review as the point. Readwise's Daily Review resurfaces a handful of past highlights each day, with spaced repetition options for ones you want to memorise. Reading isn't finished when you close the article; it's finished when you remember it.

    Steal this for your software

    • Separate what users chose from what arrived. Two inboxes are calmer than one flooded one.
    • Use a few mutually exclusive states (like Inbox/Later/Archive) instead of open-ended folders.
    • Give every action a shortcut, and a palette to discover them.
    • Put AI next to content, invoked on purpose, never mixed into the user's text.
    • Close the loop: resurface what users saved so it pays them back.

    Do

    • Make one highlight model work across all content types.
    • Show type and length in every list row.
    • Teach shortcuts in context (tooltips, palette), not in a manual.

    Don't

    • Let subscriptions flow into the same list as deliberate saves.
    • Ship power features without sane defaults for newcomers.
    • Let AI rewrite or summarise inline without a clear label.

    • Readwise Daily ReviewReadwise

    Copying the density, not the structure

    Power apps look impressive; their three-pane density overwhelms people who came to read one thing.

    Copy the states and the shortcuts. Keep the default view as calm as possible; let density be opt-in.

    Review that nags

    A daily email you feel guilty ignoring becomes noise and gets unsubscribed.

    Matter and Omnivore

    Matter treats listening and reading as one activity, with audio that follows the text word by word; Omnivore showed what an open-source reader could be, and how fast a free product can disappear.

    MakersMatter (independent startup); Omnivore (open-source team)
    SinceBoth early 2020s; Omnivore shut down in late 2024
    CategoryRead-later with audio; open-source read-later
    Study it forText + audio sync, highlight-while-listening, open source and sustainability

    The read-later category got a second wind in the early 2020s. Two products are worth studying side by side. Matter, an iOS-first app, built its identity around natural-sounding text-to-speech: you can listen to a saved article with the current word highlighted in the text, switch between reading and listening without losing your place, and highlight while listening. Omnivore was a free, open-source read-later app with highlights, labels, newsletters, PDFs and plugins for note apps like Obsidian. In late 2024 the Omnivore team joined ElevenLabs and the hosted service was shut down within weeks, with a short window to export. Its code remained available, but most users don't self-host.

    1. One position for two modes. Reading and listening share a single place in the text. Stop listening on the street, keep reading at home, from the same sentence.
    2. Follow-along highlighting. Showing the spoken word in the text helps people who want to read and listen at once (a big help for some readers with dyslexia and for language learners), and makes it easy to drop back into reading.
    3. Highlight while listening. A tap marks the passage just spoken. Annotation shouldn't require eyes on the screen.
    4. Omnivore's openness. Open source meant transparency, self-hosting and community plugins. It didn't guarantee continuity of the hosted service people actually used. Openness of code and continuity of service are different promises.

    Steal this for your software

    • If you offer audio of text, keep one shared position and highlight the current word or sentence.
    • Make key actions possible without looking (highlight, skip 15 seconds).
    • If you're open source, still publish an export path and a continuity plan for the hosted service.

    Do

    • Offer speed control in small steps (0.1×).
    • Resume audio a few seconds before where it stopped, so the sentence makes sense.
    • Make the export easy before you need it.

    Don't

    • Treat "it's open source" as an ownership guarantee for users.
    • Read image captions, ads and navigation aloud.
    • Make the player full screen when the text is the anchor.

    Reader modes: Readability, Safari Reader, Firefox Reader View

    Reader modes are a public verdict on web design: they exist because so many pages bury the text, and they work by guessing which part of the page is the article, then handing typography back to the reader.

    MakersArc90 (Readability, 2009); Apple (Safari Reader, 2010); Mozilla (Firefox Reader View)
    CategoryContent extraction + reader-controlled view
    Study it forHeuristics, semantic HTML, user-controlled typography

Apple built Safari Reader (2010) using Readability's open-source code. Mozilla maintains its own Readability.js library, which powers Firefox Reader View. Today every major browser has some reader mode. The interesting part for a designer is the mechanism: your page is being scored by a machine trying to find your article. Semantic HTML helps it, and helps everyone.

    RSS readers: NetNewsWire, Reeder, Feedbin, Feedly

    A feed reader decides how you feel about the world's output: as a debt to pay down (unread counts) or a river you dip into (timelines). Each of these four made a different choice.

    Medium and Substack

    Medium made the single article page beautiful by default and gave it reading time and inline highlights; Substack moved reading into the inbox and then into its own app. Both show how a writing platform's reading page shapes what gets written.

    MakersMedium (Ev Williams, 2012); Substack (2017)
    CategoryPublishing platforms with reading surfaces
    Study it forReading time, default typography, highlights as social reading

    Medium launched in 2012 when most blogs looked like templates from 2005. Every Medium post got the same thing: a generous serif column, big images, a clean title, and nothing in the margins. Writers didn't have to design anything, and readers got a consistent, good page. Medium added an estimated reading time to every post. Its help documentation describes it as based on an average adult reading speed of about 265 words per minute, with extra time for images. Readers could highlight text and see the passages others highlighted most ("Top highlight"). Substack, founded in 2017, made the newsletter the primary format: posts arrive by email and also live on a web page and, later, in Substack's own reading app.

    1. One layout for everyone. Writers can't change fonts or colours. This removes bad design choices from the platform and makes every post feel like part of one publication. The cost is personal expression; Substack and others later offered more customisation.
    2. Reading time in the byline. "7 min read" answers "should I start this now?" before the first sentence. It also changed writing: authors and editors began thinking in read-minutes, and Medium at times used reading time in how it measured engagement.
    3. Highlights as social reading. Highlighting is private by default, but aggregated highlights appear to others. It turns a solo act into a quiet signal, with the same steering risk as Kindle's Popular Highlights.
    4. Inline responses and notes. Medium experimented with notes in the margin next to paragraphs and later with responses as full posts. Commenting next to the text is precise; it can also clutter the column.
    5. Substack: the inbox as the reading surface. Email arrives reliably and is owned by the reader. But the inbox is a work tool, full of competing demands. Substack's app and web reader try to give newsletters a calmer home.
    6. Paywalls and prompts. Both platforms show subscribe prompts and paywalls. Where they sit (top, mid-article, end) is a reading-design decision with a business motive. Mid-article prompts break immersion most.

    Steal this for your software

    • Show reading time next to every long piece; round to whole minutes.
    • Constrain authors' styling when consistency serves readers better than expression.
    • Put asks (subscribe, share, upgrade) at natural endings, not in the middle.
    • If you aggregate reader behaviour (highlights, claps), make it opt-out and never shame low numbers.

    Do

    • Keep the reading column around 600–720px on desktop.
    • Let images go wider than the text column for emphasis.
    • Keep actions in a quiet bar outside the text.

    Don't

    • Insert modals or banners at scroll depth thresholds.
    • Show vanity metrics above the article.
    • Use reading time as a proxy for quality.

    Wikipedia's Vector 2022

    Wikipedia's 2023 desktop redesign shows how hard it is to change a reading page used by hundreds of millions of people: limiting line length, a sticky header and a persistent table of contents were well supported by reading research, and still deeply contested by the people who used the old page most.

    MakerWikimedia Foundation, with community input
    Default on English WikipediaJanuary 2023
    CategoryReference reading, desktop web
    Study it forMeasure, wayfinding, redesign at scale

    For over a decade, Wikipedia's desktop skin (Vector, from 2010) stretched text across the full width of the browser window. On a big monitor that meant lines of 150 characters or more. Vector 2022, developed over several years and rolled out first on a number of smaller-language Wikipedias, limited the content width, added a sticky header with the article title and search, moved the table of contents into a sidebar that stays visible as you scroll, and collapsed the main menu. It became the default on English Wikipedia in January 2023, after a long and divided community discussion. Logged-in users can switch back to the old skin, and anyone can toggle full width.

    Lab: the line-length decision
    Typography

    Typography is the art and technique of arranging type to make written language legible, readable and appealing when displayed. The arrangement of type involves selecting typefaces, point sizes, line lengths, line spacing and letter spacing, and adjusting the space between pairs of letters. Readers rarely notice good typography; they notice when reading feels tiring, when lines are so long that the eye loses its place on the return sweep, or so short that the rhythm keeps breaking.

    Try it. At full width on a large window, count how often you lose the line. Then switch to limited width. The estimated characters per line updates as you go. On a phone this lab is already narrow, which is the point: the problem only existed on wide screens.
    1. Limited width. A comfortable measure makes long reading less tiring. On wide monitors the old design was far outside the range typographers recommend. The cost: empty space on the sides, which some readers saw as wasted, and tables and images that had more room before. A full-width toggle keeps the choice.
    2. Sticky header. Title, search and tools stay reachable while scrolling a long article. On a reference site, search is a primary action, often used mid-article.
    3. Table of contents in a sidebar. Long articles become navigable: you see where you are and can jump without scrolling to the top. Wayfinding, the reference-reading equivalent of "time left in chapter".
    4. Collapsed main menu. Links most readers never use moved behind a menu button, which saved space and reduced noise, and annoyed editors who used them daily.
    5. Gradual rollout, with research. The Foundation deployed to smaller wikis first, tested features, and published findings and documentation. The English Wikipedia discussion still showed many editors opposed. The lesson: research can tell you what helps most readers; it doesn't remove the cost to experts whose habits change.

    Steal this for your software

    • Cap the width of reading text on wide screens; let tables and figures break out wider.
    • For long reference pages, keep a table of contents visible with the current section marked.
    • Make search reachable from anywhere in a long page.
    • Roll out big reading changes gradually, with an opt-out for experts.

    Do

    • Explain the reasoning publicly when you change a familiar reading layout.
    • Keep a width toggle for people who need tables or have special setups.
    • Highlight the current section in the TOC as the reader scrolls.

    Don't

    • Assume "more on screen" means "easier to read".
    • Hide tools experts use daily without a fast path back.
    • Ship a redesign to everyone at once with no way back.

    Listening as reading: Audible, Apple Books, Speechify

    The best audio reading products treat a book as one work in two forms, so you can move between eyes and ears without losing your place, and use the text to support the voice.

    ProductsAudible (Amazon since 2008), Whispersync for Voice (2012), Immersion Reading; Apple Books audiobooks; Speechify
    CategoryAudiobooks and text-to-speech
    Study it forCross-format continuity, audio controls, accessibility

    Is listening to a book "reading" it? For a designer the useful answer is: it's the same book, in a different form, for a different moment. Amazon made this concrete in 2012 with Whispersync for Voice: if you own both the Kindle book and the matching Audible audiobook, your position syncs between them. Immersion Reading goes further and plays the narration while highlighting the text as it's read, on supported Kindle apps and Fire tablets. Apple Books keeps audiobooks in the same library as ebooks. Speechify, a text-to-speech app, reads any text (web pages, PDFs, scans) aloud with follow-along highlighting; its founder has spoken about growing up with dyslexia, and the product grew from that need.

    1. Align audio to text. The underlying work is alignment: knowing which sentence is spoken at which second. Every feature (sync, follow-along, highlight while listening) comes from that one map.
    2. Confirm, don't jump. Cross-format sync suggests the furthest position and asks, just like device sync on Kindle.
    3. Controls for hands-busy moments. Big skip-back (15 or 30 seconds), sleep timers, speed in small steps, lock-screen and headphone controls. Listening usually happens while doing something else.
    4. Text as support for the voice. Follow-along highlighting helps people with dyslexia, language learners and anyone who wants to read faster with audio pacing. It's an accessibility feature that many people simply enjoy.
    5. Bookmarks by time and text. An audio bookmark should show the sentence, not only "2:14:07".

    Steal this for your software

    • If content exists in two forms (video and transcript, audio and text), build one position map and share it.
    • Label time-based items with the words at that time.
    • Design controls for eyes-free use: big targets, headphone buttons, a sleep timer.
    • Treat accessibility features as features for everyone, and make them easy to find.

    Do

    • Offer 15–30 second skip-back as the most prominent secondary control.
    • Remember speed per book.
    • Back up a few seconds on resume.

    Don't

    • Keep separate libraries for listening and reading of the same work.
    • Show only timestamps in bookmarks and clips.
    • Auto-play the next item without a clear setting.

    • Immersion ReadingAmazon

    Speed controls in big jumps

    The reader-friendly web: Gwern.net, Practical Typography, Craig Mod

    A few independent websites show what reading on the web can be when the writer controls everything: sidenotes beside the text, previews instead of jumps, and typography argued for in public.

    MakersGwern Branwen (with Said Achmiz on design); Matthew Butterick; Craig Mod
    CategoryIndependent long-form websites and essays
    Study it forSidenotes, popups, web typography, the idea of digital books

    Some of the best reading products aren't apps at all. Gwern.net is a personal site of long, heavily referenced essays. Its design (developed with Said Achmiz) puts footnotes as sidenotes in the margin on wide screens, shows link and footnote previews in popups on hover so you don't leave the page, and offers a reader-friendly dark mode and other options. Matthew Butterick's Practical Typography (2013) is a web book about typography that is itself an example of its own advice: a narrow serif column, careful spacing, and a pay-what-you-want model. Craig Mod's essays, from "Books in the Age of the iPad" (2010) and "Post-Artifact Books and Publishing" (2011) to "Future Reading" (Aeon, 2015), argued that digital books should stop imitating paper and start using what screens can do: connection, annotation and the edges around the text.

    On hover or tap: a preview of a linked page or note appears in place; the reader never leaves the sentence.
    Endnote jump: the reader leaves the sentence, reads the note 2,300 words away, and has to find their way back. Many simply skip notes.
    Sidenote and popup: the note sits next to the sentence on wide screens, and appears inline on tap on phones. The margin becomes part of the reading, as it was in print.
    1. Margins are for notes (Gwern.net, Tufte CSS). On wide screens, unused margins become a place for notes, aligned to the line that references them. This uses the space that a limited measure creates, the same space Vector 2022 left empty.
    2. Preview, don't navigate. Popups for links and footnotes let readers check a reference without losing their place. They need care: delay before showing, easy dismissal, and a tap equivalent on touch screens.
    3. Typography as argument (Butterick). Practical Typography explains each rule (line length, point size, spacing, quotes) and demonstrates it on the same page. The site is its own proof.
    4. Pay what you want for reading. Butterick asks readers to pay if the book is useful (or buy his fonts). The model keeps the page free of ads and pop-ups. Business model as reading design, again.
    5. Post-artifact thinking (Mod). Mod argued that the most interesting changes in digital books happen around the text: sharing, annotation, collective marginalia, the network of readers, not in imitating page curls. Many products in this guide (Popular Highlights, Readwise, Medium highlights) are versions of that idea.

    Steal this for your software

    • Use spare width beside a capped text column for notes, definitions or related items.
    • Preview links in place in any reading surface; navigate only on a deliberate click.
    • Make your documentation demonstrate its own advice.
    • Look for value "around the text": notes, shared highlights, links between readers.

    Do

    • Delay hover previews slightly (a few hundred milliseconds) to avoid flicker.
    • Give every popup a tap equivalent and an obvious close.
    • Fall back to inline notes on narrow screens.

    Don't

    • Open popups on the slightest mouse pass.
    • Put critical content only in hover states.
    • Use sidenotes at widths where they squeeze the main column.

    Capstone: design your ideal reading app

    Taught by you, with everything above

    A good reading-product spec fits on one page: who reads what, when; the six moments and what the screen does in each; the defaults; and the few decisions you will defend.

    This is where the breakdowns turn into design. You'll write a one-page spec for a reading product, or for the reading surface inside your own product. Keep it short on purpose. A spec that fits on one page forces you to decide; a twenty-page spec lets you avoid deciding. The template below follows the structure of this guide: job, scorecard targets, session moments, defaults, decisions with costs, and what you deliberately leave out.

    A worked example

    Here is how the first blocks might look for a small product: a reader for long-form engineering blogs, built by one person. Note how the scorecard targets make later decisions easy.

    Reader: a working developer who follows 20–40 engineering blogs. Content: long technical posts with code and diagrams. Moment: evenings and weekends, mostly on a laptop, sometimes on a tablet, 15–45 minutes. Job: "When a great engineering post appears, I want to read it properly with its code and figures, so I can actually learn from it instead of skimming."

    Must be 3: Typography (including code blocks), Defaults, Ownership (OPML in, Markdown highlights out). Fine at 2: Annotation, Sync, Navigation. Deliberately low: Discovery (1). No recommendations; the user's subscriptions are the curation.

    Arrive: opens to a timeline of posts from the last 7 days, newest first, no counts. Settle: one half-sheet with three presets. Immerse: text, code and figures only; toolbar hides on scroll-down. Mark: select to highlight, highlights collect in a per-post notebook. Leave/Return: scroll position syncs; a jump of more than one screen asks first.

    D1 Timeline, no unread counts, because volume varies wildly between blogs. Cost: completionists lose a sense of "done". D2 Code blocks scroll horizontally inside their own box, never wrap, because wrapping breaks code meaning. Cost: some scrolling on tablets. D3 No AI summaries in v1, because the job is learning, not skipping. Cost: a feature competitors list. D4 Highlights export to Markdown files, because the audience lives in text editors. Cost: no rich formatting.

    • Prototype the Immerse screen in HTML before anything else.

    Wrap-up

    Ten things to remember

    1. A reading product's job is to disappear; judge it by how little of its interface you remember after ten minutes.
    2. Defaults are the product. Most readers never open settings.
    3. Settings must preview live on the real text, ideally in a half-sheet (Kobo).
    4. Presets carry a design opinion; knobs without presets push the work onto readers (Apple Books).
    5. Show progress in the reader's units: time, real pages, sections (Kindle, Medium).
    6. Split capture from reading; capture should cost nothing (Instapaper).
    7. If zero is unreachable, don't show a count (Reeder).
    8. Translate system states into positions, times and choices (Libby).
    9. Cap the measure; use the spare width calmly or for notes (Wikipedia, Gwern.net).
    10. Export is trust. Products end; what users made shouldn't (Pocket, Omnivore).
