Reading & Writing. Software that gets out of the way of words.

Most software is something you use. Reading and writing software is somewhere you stay, sometimes for hours. This guide teaches you to design that place: the page, the light, the way through a long text, the margin where you think back, and the blank page where you write. Calm here is not a style. It is the whole job.

Attention is the product

Taught by Oliver Reichenstein, with Naoto Fukasawa and Kenya Hara

In reading software the user's attention is not a resource you spend on your interface; it is the thing you are protecting. Every pixel of chrome, every animation, every prompt is paid for out of the reader's concentration.

Think about how most software is used. You open a banking app, do a task in 40 seconds, and leave. The interface is the experience, so it is reasonable for it to be visible, labelled and explicit. Reading is the reverse. A reading session lasts 20 minutes to two hours. During that time the reader is building something fragile in their head: a model of an argument, a world, a set of characters. Psychologists call this a situation model. It takes minutes to build and seconds to collapse.

That gives you the mechanism behind every rule in this guide. Any element that pulls the eye away from the text, even for a quarter of a second, forces the reader to put the model down and pick it back up. One interruption is cheap. Ten interruptions in a chapter turns reading into skimming, and the reader blames the book, not your app. They just feel that reading on your app is tiring.

Naoto Fukasawa's "without thought" idea is useful here: good design fits behaviour people already have, so they act without stopping to think about the object. For reading, the behaviour already exists and is centuries old: eyes move along a line, hands turn a page, a finger keeps a place. Your job is to fit that behaviour, not to teach a new one. Kenya Hara's idea of emptiness (an empty vessel that can hold whatever the user brings) is the other half. A reading screen should be close to empty, so that the text can fill it.

The three jobs of a reading interface

Strip it down and a reading interface has three jobs. Get the reader in (open to the right place, fast, with the text already set well). Keep the reader in (stay out of the way, never surprise them). Get the reader back (when they leave, make return effortless: same place, same settings, maybe a reminder of where they were). Almost every reading feature belongs to one of those three jobs. Features that belong to none of them, like streak badges or social prompts mid-chapter, deserve hard suspicion.

Web Design is 95% Typography.Oliver Reichenstein, essay title, iA, 2006

Reichenstein's essay title was a provocation, but for reading products it is close to literal. If the text is set well and the chrome is hidden, you have most of a great reader. Everything after that is about not breaking it.

Key idea

In a reading app, measure success by what the reader did not notice. If they finish a chapter without once thinking about your app, you won.

Do

  • Hide all chrome by default; reveal it with one tap in the centre or a mouse move, and hide it again when reading resumes.
  • Keep every surprise out of the reading surface: no banners, toasts, or tips while text is on screen.
  • Sort each feature into get in, stay in, get back. Cut what fits none.
  • Open straight to the text at the last position, in under a second.

Don't

  • Show ratings prompts, upsells or "share your progress" mid-chapter.
  • Animate anything the reader did not trigger.
  • Put persistent tab bars or floating buttons over text.
  • Measure the reading app by time on screen or taps; measure finished sessions and returns.

For the solo creator

Calm is the cheapest feature you will ever ship, because most of it is deletion. You do not need a design team to remove a toolbar. You need the discipline to not add one. When you are tempted to add a mid-reading prompt because a metric dipped, write down what attention it costs first.

Common mistakes

Treating the reader like a task app

Most of your design experience comes from forms and dashboards, where visible controls are good.

Write the session length at the top of your design doc. If it is over 10 minutes, apply reading rules: chrome hidden, nothing unprompted.

Growth features inside the text

Prompts placed where users spend the most time get the most impressions.

Move all prompts to the edges of the session: the library screen, or the end of a book. Never between two paragraphs.

Hidden means unfindable

Designers hide chrome but give no clue how to bring it back.

Show the chrome on first open, then fade it after a few seconds; one tap anywhere central brings it back. That is learnable in one session.

Check yourself

Your reading app's retention dipped. A teammate (or your own inner growth hacker) suggests a small "Keep your streak!" toast every 15 minutes of reading. Best response?

The toast's cost is not its size or duration; it is the eye movement and the broken situation model. Moving the message to the edges of the session (d) keeps the motivation without taxing reading. A short fade (a) still interrupts; opt-in (b) is better but still mid-text; top placement (c) changes nothing about attention.

Pages or scroll

Taught by Marco Arment (Instapaper), with lessons from Kindle and Apple Books

Pages give the reader a fixed place and a rhythm; scroll gives them continuity and speed. Choose by the length and purpose of the text, and if you offer both, make each one complete rather than half of each.

The page is the oldest reading interface we have, and it does three useful things. It gives every word a fixed place on a fixed rectangle, so your memory can attach to it ("the quote was near the top of a left page"). It creates a rhythm: read, turn, read, which gives the mind small rests. And it makes progress countable: page 112 of 300 means something.

Scroll does different useful things. It never cuts a paragraph in half. It lets you glance back at the previous line without a gesture. It handles text of any length without the machinery of pagination, which is why the web is a scroll. And on a phone, a flick of the thumb is less effort than a tap per screen for skimming.

The weaknesses mirror the strengths. Scroll's position is fuzzy: the same sentence sits at a different height every time you return, so spatial memory has nothing to hold. Continuous motion also asks the eye to track moving text, which is tiring over hours. Pages force awkward breaks (a heading at the bottom of a page, a single line at the top) and, on screens, a turn animation that can become a toy.

The page turn is a micro-interaction, not a show

Apple's iBooks launched in 2010 with a realistic page curl that followed your finger. It was a beautiful demonstration of the iPad's touch screen, and it was also skeuomorphism at its most literal. In the 2022 redesign of Apple Books the curl was replaced by a simpler slide, and enough readers missed it that Apple brought the curl back as an option in a later update. The lesson is not "curl good" or "curl bad". The turn is felt hundreds of times per book. It must be fast (under about 250 ms), it must follow the finger if you drag, and it must never be the thing you notice. Some readers love the curl because it gives the turn a physical weight; others find it slow. That is a legitimate preference, which is why it is a setting.

On e-ink, the turn has no animation at all: the screen just redraws. That turns out to be fine. Readers stop noticing the redraw within minutes, because it is consistent. Consistency matters more than smoothness.

If you offer both, finish both

Many readers offer pages and scroll as a setting: Kindle's phone apps, Apple Books, and Instapaper all have had both at various points. That is fine, but each model has its own requirements, and teams often build one properly and the other as an afterthought. A complete page model needs widow and orphan control, headings that never sit alone at the bottom of a page, images that fit a page, and stable page numbers for a given text size. A complete scroll model needs a position that survives rotation and font changes, a meaningful progress indicator, and a way to page by screen (space bar, a tap at the bottom edge) for readers who like the rhythm without the animation.

  • Pick a default per content type and make it excellent before adding the other model.

  • Let the reader page by screen in scroll mode (space bar, tap the bottom edge).
  • Keep the reader's exact sentence when they switch models, rotate, or resize text.

  • Leave a heading stranded at the bottom of a page.

Common mistakes

Losing the place on rotation or font change

Position is stored as a page number or scroll offset, both of which change when layout changes.

Store position as a text offset (character index or a stable location ID) and re-find it after every reflow.

Toy page turns

The turn is fun to build and demo.

Time it. If it is over 300 ms or cannot be interrupted by the next tap, it is too slow for the 400th turn.

Check yourself

You are building a reader for long legal documents that people search, jump around in, and cite by section. Which reading model fits best?

Reference documents are read by jumping, not cover to cover, and citations need stable addresses that do not change with text size. Reflowable page numbers (c) change with font size, so they make bad citations; section anchors do not. The curl (b) adds cost to every jump.

The text block: measure, leading, margins

Taught by Robert Bringhurst, with Matthew Butterick and the iA team

A long text is read line by line, so the line is your unit of design. Its length (measure), the space between lines (leading) and the quiet around the block (margins) decide whether the eye moves without effort for an hour or tires in ten minutes.

You met these in Typography. Here we tune them for long sessions, where small errors add up. The mechanism is the return sweep. At the end of each line your eye jumps back to the start of the next one. If lines are too long, the jump is far and the eye sometimes lands on the wrong line (you reread a line, or skip one). If lines are too short, the eye jumps constantly and phrases get chopped. If lines are too tight vertically, the next line crowds the target and the sweep misses. If they are too loose, the block falls apart into stripes.

Numbers to start from

Robert Bringhurst's The Elements of Typographic Style gives 45 to 75 characters as the satisfactory range for a single-column page, with 66 widely regarded as ideal. Matthew Butterick's Practical Typography suggests 45 to 90 characters and line spacing of 120 to 145 percent of the point size. On screens, long-form reading tends to want the upper half of that leading range or a little more, because screens are read at varying distances and in varying light. A good starting point for a desktop reading column is 17 to 21 px text, 1.5 to 1.6 line height, and a column of about 34 em (roughly 65 to 70 characters in a typical serif).

Leading and measure are linked: the longer the line, the more leading it needs, because the return sweep has further to travel and needs a wider target. If you let readers widen the column, nudge the leading up with it. Good reader apps do this quietly.

Margins are not wasted space

Book designers spent five centuries on margins. Classical page proportions, like the Van de Graaf canon reconstructed by Jan Tschichold, put the text block high and toward the spine, with the largest margin at the bottom. On screen there is no spine, so symmetric side margins are normal, but two old lessons still apply. First, margins frame the block so the eye knows where the text ends; text that touches the edge of a screen feels like it is spilling off. Second, the bottom margin should be generous, because the last line on a screen is where the eye pauses before turning, and controls placed there compete with it.

On a phone, side margins of 16 to 24 px are typical; reading apps often go wider (24 to 32 px) because the measure is already short and the frame adds calm. On tablets and desktops, the margin is whatever is left after you cap the measure. Do not widen the text to fill a wide window. Centre a column and let the margins grow.

/* A calm long-form column for the web */
article {
  max-width: 34em;          /* ~65-70 characters in most serifs */
  margin: 0 auto;
  padding: 0 clamp(16px, 5vw, 32px);
  font-size: clamp(17px, 1rem + 0.3vw, 21px);
  line-height: 1.55;
}
article p + p { margin-top: 0.9em; }   /* or indent, not both */

Do

  • Cap the measure in em, so it scales with the reader's text size.
  • Raise line height as measure grows; 1.5 to 1.6 is a safe start for long-form.
  • Give the block real margins, generous at the bottom of the screen.
  • Separate paragraphs with either space or a first-line indent, not both.

Don't

  • Let text run the full width of a desktop window.
  • Use the same tight line height you use for UI labels.
  • Put a toolbar flush against the last line of text.
  • Fix the measure in px, so it breaks when the reader enlarges the text.

Common mistakes

Measure in pixels

The layout is designed at one text size.

Set max-width in em or ch. When the reader picks a bigger size, the column grows with it and the character count stays right.

Paragraph space and indent together

Two habits from different traditions (web and print) get combined.

One global line-height: 1.2 is set for the whole app.

A reader sets the largest text size in your web reader on a 27-inch monitor. Your column is max-width: 680px. What happens, and what should you change?

A pixel cap holds the width while characters get bigger, so fewer fit per line. An em cap scales with font size and keeps roughly the same character count. Line height in unitless form (d) already scales correctly.

The book face, justification and hyphenation

Taught by Matthew Carter's screen faces, with Dalton Maag (Bookerly) and TypeTogether (Literata)

A typeface for hours of reading is not chosen for personality. It is chosen for stamina: generous x-height, open shapes, sturdy serifs, moderate contrast, and spacing that survives small sizes, low resolution and e-ink. Justification is only worth it if hyphenation comes with it.

Display typefaces are judged in a glance. Book typefaces are judged after the 300th page, when every small irritation has been seen 100,000 times. The qualities that matter are boring and physical. A large x-height makes lowercase letters bigger at the same point size, so text reads larger without taking more lines. Open apertures (the gaps in c, e, a, s) keep letters distinct when blurred or rendered with few pixels. Sturdy, slightly blunt serifs survive e-ink and low-resolution screens where hairline serifs break up. Moderate stroke contrast avoids thin strokes vanishing in dark mode or at small sizes.

Justification needs hyphenation

Books are usually justified: both edges straight. Justification works by stretching the spaces between words on each line. In a wide print column with good hyphenation, the stretch is small and invisible. In a narrow screen column without hyphenation, a long word that cannot be broken pushes to the next line and leaves huge gaps behind it. Gaps stack vertically into "rivers" of white that the eye follows down the page. That is why early e-readers and many apps looked worse justified than ragged.

Amazon's 2015 Kindle typography update paired the new Bookerly face with a new layout engine that added hyphenation and better spacing. That is the right pairing. The rule for you: justify only when you can hyphenate well and the measure is at least about 45 characters. Otherwise use left-aligned ("ragged right") text, which never makes rivers.

These three are live HTML, so your browser does the setting. If the middle one looks like the first, your browser lacks English hyphenation dictionaries, which is itself a lesson: you cannot rely on hyphens: auto everywhere.

/* Long-form web text, book-like when supported */
article { text-align: left; text-wrap: pretty; }   /* fewer short last lines */
@supports (hyphens: auto) {
  article.justified { text-align: justify; hyphens: auto; }
}
/* never justify below ~45 characters per line */

Widows, orphans and the last line

A widow is a paragraph's last line alone at the top of a page; an orphan is a first line alone at the bottom. In scroll they hardly matter, but on pages they look like errors. A related screen problem is the runt, a single short word on the last line of a paragraph. Browsers now offer text-wrap: pretty, which rebalances the last lines of paragraphs to avoid runts, and text-wrap: balance for headings. They cost nothing to add and degrade gracefully.

Do

  • Choose a face with a large x-height, open apertures and sturdy serifs for long text.
  • Test the face at small size, in dark mode and in greyscale.
  • Justify only with good hyphenation and a measure of 45+ characters.
  • Set lang on your text, so hyphenation and quotes work.

Don't

  • Pick a face because it looks elegant in a 48 px heading.
  • Use hairline serif "fashion" faces (Didone-style) for body text.
  • Justify narrow phone columns without hyphenation.
  • Ship a reading app with only your brand font.

Reader controls and defaults that are already right

Taught by Kindle, Kobo and Instapaper, with Dieter Rams on "as little design as possible"

Readers differ: eyesight, light, device, habit. So reading apps must let them change the text. But every control is a decision you push onto the reader, so the default must already be right, and the controls should be few, layered and instantly previewed.

There are two failure modes. The first is too few controls: a fixed font size in a reading app excludes anyone whose eyes do not match yours, and that is most people over 45. The second is too many: a panel of fourteen sliders invites fiddling, which is the opposite of reading. People who start adjusting kerning at 11 pm are not reading.

The way through is to rank controls by how many readers need them and how often. Text size and theme (light or dark) are needed by almost everyone and change with the situation, so they sit on the first layer, one tap away. Font, line spacing, margins and alignment are set once and rarely touched, so they sit one level deeper. Expert controls, like Kobo's font weight and sharpness sliders, sit behind an "advanced" door. Each change previews live on the real text behind the panel, never in a sample box.

How many options per control?

For each control, offer steps rather than a continuous range when the reader sets it once: five to eight text sizes, three or four line spacings, three margins. Steps are easier to return to ("I like the fourth size") and easier to sync across devices. Text size is the exception that benefits from many steps, because eyesight varies so widely; Kindle and Apple Books offer many sizes, including very large ones.

For typefaces, four to eight is enough, as long as they differ in a useful way: a sturdy serif, a screen serif, a humanist sans, one high-legibility face (such as Atkinson Hyperlegible), and the publisher's font when there is one. Twelve similar serifs are not choice; they are homework.

Defaults are your design

Most people never open the settings. Whatever you ship as default is what most of your readers will see for their entire time with the app. So spend more time on the default than on the panel. Set it per device class: a phone default (17 to 19 px, 1.5 to 1.6 leading, 20 to 28 px side margins) is not a tablet default. Follow the system text size setting (Dynamic Type on iOS, font scale on Android) as the starting point, and remember the reader's choice per device, not just per account, because the same person wants different sizes on a phone and an e-reader.

Do

  • Spend more time on defaults than on the panel. Start from the system text size.
  • Put size and theme on the first layer; font and spacing one step deeper.
  • Preview every change on the real text behind the panel.
  • Use steps for set-once controls, and remember choices per device.
  • Offer a "reset to defaults" that restores your designed defaults.

Don't

  • Ship a reading surface with a fixed text size.
  • Offer a dozen near-identical serif fonts.
  • Show a "sample text" box instead of the actual page.
  • Sync one text size to every device the reader owns.

Light, dark, sepia, night and e-ink

Taught by e-reader makers (Kindle, Kobo), with research on polarity and night reading

A reading theme is a lighting decision. Match it to the light the reader is in, keep contrast high but not harsh, and when the screen is e-ink, design for a display that is slow, grey and permanent rather than fast, coloured and glowing.

Screens emit light; paper reflects it. In a bright room, a white screen is about as bright as the paper around it and feels natural. In a dark room the same white screen is the brightest thing in your field of view, your pupils narrow, and the glare tires you. That is the real reason readers want dark themes at night: not taste, but light balance. Research on text polarity (for example, work by Piepenbrock and colleagues in 2013) has found that dark text on a light background tends to give better proofreading and legibility in normal lighting, partly because a narrower pupil sharpens the image. So the right default is light in the day and a dark option for dim rooms, ideally following the system appearance.

Dark, but not too dark

Two details separate a good dark theme from an inverted one. First, avoid pure white text on pure black for long reading. The extreme contrast makes bright letters bloom into the dark around them (halation), which is worse for the many people with astigmatism. Soften the text to an off-white or light grey and lift the background slightly off black. Second, true black (#000) on OLED phones is a legitimate choice for night reading in a dark room, because black pixels are off and the screen emits almost no light around the text. But true black can smear when you scroll on some OLED panels, and the jump from a bright UI to it is harsh, so offer it as an option, not the dark default.

Night reading and warm light

Light in the evening affects sleep. A 2015 study by Chang and colleagues (PNAS) found that people who read on a light-emitting e-reader before bed took longer to fall asleep and had suppressed evening melatonin compared with reading a printed book. The strongest levers are brightness and timing. Warmer colour (less blue) is a plausible help and is how e-readers like the Kindle Oasis, newer Paperwhites and Kobo models with "ComfortLight PRO" handle bedtime, but the evidence that a warm shift alone fixes the sleep effect is mixed. Design for both: a dark or sepia theme, brightness that can go very low, and on devices with a front light, a warm tint that can follow sunset.

Designing for e-ink

E-ink (electrophoretic) screens move tiny charged pigment particles to form an image, and the image stays without power. That makes them wonderful for reading (reflective like paper, readable in sunlight, weeks of battery) and awkward for interfaces. A full refresh takes a few hundred milliseconds and often flashes black to clear the previous image. Faster partial updates leave faint traces of the previous image, called ghosting, which builds up until the next full refresh. Most screens show 16 levels of grey. Colour e-ink exists (for example the Kindle Colorsoft from 2024 and several Kobo models), but colours are muted and colour resolution is lower than black-and-white.

So the e-ink design rules are almost the opposite of phone UI fashion: no animation (each frame costs a refresh and leaves ghosts), no subtle greys (they become mush), no thin lines, no transparency, no spinners. Use solid black on white, thick enough strokes, clear boxes, and state changes that are big and instant. Design screens so that partial refreshes only touch the area that changed (for example, a menu drawn over a fixed region), and give the reader control over how often a full refresh flash happens. Kindle exposes this as a "page refresh" option.

Do

  • Default to the system appearance; offer light, sepia and dark at minimum.
  • Soften dark themes: off-white text on near-black, around 10 to 12:1.
  • Let brightness go very low, and offer warmth on devices with front lights.
  • On e-ink: black, white, thick strokes, no motion, static status text.

Don't

  • Invert colours and call it dark mode (images and highlights break).
  • Use pure white on pure black for hours of text.
  • Use spinners, fades, transparency or light grey on e-ink.
  • Claim a warm filter "fixes" sleep; the evidence does not say that.

Finding your way in long text

A long text is a place, and readers need to know where they are in it, how far is left, how to jump, how to come back, and how to leave marks. Three chapters: progress and navigation, annotation, and the help that lives inside the text (footnotes, definitions, context).

7

Where am I, how far is left, where was I

Taught by Kindle (locations, time left, Whispersync), with Apple Books and the web

Progress in a long text answers three different questions: where am I, how much is left, and how do I get back? Each indicator answers some of them well and others badly, so pick the one that matches what your reader is deciding.

A printed book answers all three questions without any design: you feel the thickness of the pages in each hand, you see the page number, and you can put a finger or a ribbon in it. A screen removes every physical cue. The reader has no idea if the chapter has three pages left or thirty, which matters because people decide whether to keep reading based on what is left ("I'll finish this chapter, then sleep"). That decision is the real job of the progress indicator.

Reflowable text makes this harder. When the reader changes text size, page 112 becomes page 140, so pages are not stable addresses. Kindle's answer, from the start, was the "location": a number tied to a position in the text itself, independent of size. Locations are stable but meaningless as a sense of distance ("Loc 2,341 of 6,020" requires arithmetic). So Kindle also offers percent, real page numbers for books that have them mapped to a print edition, and time left in the chapter or book, estimated from your own reading speed.

Chapter-scoped beats book-scoped

The decision readers make most often is "do I have time to finish this bit?", and the natural bit is the chapter. So chapter-scoped progress (pages or minutes left in the chapter) is usually the most useful default. Book-wide percent is better for the rarer question "how far through am I?", and belongs in the menu or as an alternative. Time estimates must be personal and humble: compute them from this reader's pace on this book, show them only after a few pages of data, and round generously ("about 10 minutes").

One subtle rule: progress must never move backward when nothing changed. If changing text size makes "page 112" jump to "page 140", say why, or better, use an indicator that does not jump. Readers who see numbers jump stop trusting all the numbers.

Tables of contents and scrubbers

The table of contents is the map. It should show the current chapter highlighted, allow jumping with one tap, and in long books, show progress within each chapter or at least which ones are finished. A scrubber (a slider across the whole book) is good for big jumps and terrible for precision, so it needs two things: a preview of where you will land (chapter title and page or location) shown while dragging, and a way back to where you were. Readers who scrub to check something and then cannot find their place again are a classic support complaint. Kindle's "Page Flip" (2016) solved this with a separate peek mode: you browse other pages in a panel while your reading position stays pinned.

Coming back: sync and "where was I"

Readers switch devices: phone on the train, e-reader in bed, laptop at lunch. Position sync (Kindle calls it Whispersync) means the book opens where they stopped, on any device. The design decisions are in the edge cases. When two devices disagree, which wins? Kindle's approach is to ask, offering to jump to the furthest or most recent page read on another device, rather than silently moving the reader. Silent jumps forward can spoil a novel; silent jumps back lose a place. Ask once, clearly, with both positions.

The second part of coming back is memory. If someone returns after two weeks, they may remember the page but not the story. A light "where was I" cue helps: the last sentence they read highlighted for a moment, the chapter title and a line on how long ago they were here. Some apps go further and offer a recap of previous chapters; if you do this (with or without AI), make it opt-in, clearly marked, and spoiler-safe by only using text before the reader's position.

Do

  • Default to chapter-scoped progress; let a tap on the footer cycle formats.
  • Use stable text positions (locations or offsets) for sync, bookmarks and citations.
  • Show a landing preview while scrubbing and a "back to" chip after a jump.
  • Ask when synced positions disagree; never jump silently.
  • On return after a long gap, briefly mark the last sentence read.

Don't

  • Show a progress number that jumps around when settings change.
  • Estimate "time left" from a global average on page one.
  • Make the TOC the only way to jump, or the scrubber the only way back.
  • Generate recaps that use text beyond the reader's position.

    A tap on the bottom-left footer cycles between location, page (when available), time left in chapter, time left in book, and nothing. One quiet slot, many answers, chosen by the reader.

Highlights, notes and getting them out

Taught by Readwise, Kindle and Hypothesis, with the margin notes of every serious reader

Annotation turns reading into thinking. Make the mark fast and precise, keep the note next to its text, and treat export as a core feature, because highlights are only worth something when they can leave your app.

People highlight for three reasons: to remember, to come back, and to use later (quote, cite, write). The first two happen inside your app; the third happens somewhere else, in a notes app, a document, an essay. If your highlights cannot leave, the reader eventually learns that highlighting in your app is a dead end, and stops. That is why a whole company, Readwise (founded in 2017), exists to pull highlights out of Kindle, Apple Books, Instapaper and other apps and send them into tools like Obsidian, Notion and Logseq, with a daily review email that resurfaces old highlights. Readers paid for an export layer the reading apps did not build.

Selection on touch is the hard part

On a desktop, selecting text with a mouse is precise. On a phone, your fingertip covers several words, so selection needs help. The established pattern: long-press selects a word, handles appear at both ends, dragging a handle shows a magnified view (a loupe on iOS) of what is under your finger, and the selection snaps to word boundaries. Good reading apps add one more step: a single tap on the highlight action creates it immediately in the last-used colour, with colour and note as secondary options. Every extra tap between "I want to keep this" and "kept" costs highlights.

    Footnotes, definitions and help inside the text
    
  
    Taught by Edward Tufte (sidenotes), with Gwern Branwen, Wikipedia and Kindle X-Ray
    
  
    Any help a reader needs mid-sentence (a footnote, a definition, a translation, who a character is) should appear next to the text, be readable in a glance, and disappear without moving the reader. Never send them to another page to get it.
    
  
    The printed footnote was a clever answer to a physical problem: you cannot put the note next to the word, so you put it at the bottom of the same page, and the eye drops and returns. Endnotes, at the back of the book, were a cheaper answer that made notes much harder to read. On the web, the default became the endnote: a superscript link jumps to the bottom of the article, and a little arrow jumps back. That breaks the reading flow twice and, on long pages, lands you in a pile of notes with no sense of which is yours.
    
  
    Screens can do better, in two ways. Popover notes open in place, right where you tapped, and close without moving the page. Sidenotes, which Edward Tufte uses in his books and which Tufte CSS and Gwern.net brought to the web, sit in a wide margin beside the line they belong to, so you can read them without any action at all. Sidenotes need a wide screen; on narrow screens they must collapse into popovers or inline toggles.

    Reading the web, calmly
    
  
    Most reading now happens on the web and in feeds, where the text arrives wrapped in navigation, ads, and an endless supply of more. Two chapters: how reader modes and read-later apps rescued the article, and how to design a feed that ends.
    

    


    
  
    10
    Reader mode and read-later
    
  
    Taught by Arc90's Readability, Safari Reader, and Marco Arment (Instapaper)
    
  
    Reader modes exist because publishers' pages failed readers. They work by finding the article in a noisy page and re-setting it with good defaults. Read-later apps add time: save now, read when you have attention. If you publish text, your page should not need either.
    
  
    In 2009, the Arc90 lab released Readability, a bookmarklet that took a cluttered web page and showed just the article in a clean column. It spread quickly because it solved a pain everyone had. Apple built Safari Reader on top of it in 2010; Mozilla later wrote its own Readability.js for Firefox's Reader View, and now every major browser has some form of reader mode. That history is a verdict: given the choice, readers removed nearly everything publishers put around the text.
    
  
    How does a reader mode know what the article is? It scores blocks of the page. Paragraph-heavy blocks with lots of text, commas and few links score high; blocks with many links, short text, or class names like "sidebar", "comment", "ad" or "footer" score low. The highest-scoring container is taken as the article, cleaned, and re-set with the reader's preferred type. The same heuristics tell you how to write pages that reader modes, read-later apps, and assistive technology handle well: put the article in an <article> element, use real paragraphs and headings, mark the byline and date, and keep navigation out of the main content.

    Read-later: separating finding from reading
    
  
    Marco Arment built Instapaper in 2008 for a problem he had himself: he found long articles during the day, when he had no time to read them, and wanted to read them later on his phone during idle time like a commute. The insight is that finding and reading happen in different states of attention. Finding is fast and fragmented (a link in a chat, a tab during work). Reading needs a quiet block of time. A read-later app is a bridge between the two: one tap to save, then a calm, offline, consistently set reading surface later.
    
  
    The design of the save action matters as much as the reader. It must be instant and work everywhere: browser extension, share sheet, email forwarding, an API. Saving should confirm quietly and never ask questions (tags and folders can come later). Then the queue must not become a guilt pile. A list of 900 unread articles is a to-do list nobody can finish. Good read-later apps help you let go: archive with one swipe, sort by length so you can match an article to the time you have, and make it fine to never read something.
    

  

  
    
    
    Do
    • Publish articles in semantic HTML so reader modes and read-later apps parse them perfectly.
    • Make save one tap from everywhere, with a quiet confirmation and no questions.
    • Show length (minutes) in the queue and allow sorting by it.
    • Make archiving guilt-free: one swipe, no "are you sure".
    • Offer full export (titles, URLs, highlights) in open formats.
    
    
    Don't
    • Block reader modes or truncate articles in the page source.
    • Ask for tags or folders at save time.
    • Display "847 unread" in red on the queue.
    • Lock saved articles in a format only your service can read.

    Designing a calm feed
    
  
    Taught by Silvio Rizzi (Reeder), Brent Simmons (NetNewsWire), with Feedbin and Instagram's "all caught up"
    
  
    A feed can be a tool you use or a slot machine that uses you. Calm feeds are chronological, finite, under the reader's control, and free of counters that create guilt. They end, and say so.
    
  
    RSS readers are the old, honest version of the feed: you choose sources, items arrive in time order, and you read until there are no more. Social feeds took the same shape and changed three things: an algorithm chooses what you see, the list never ends, and counters and badges invite you back. Those three changes are the mechanism of compulsive use, covered in depth in Honest & Calm and The Human Mind. Here we look at the practical design of a feed for reading that respects attention.
    

  

  
    The unread count problem
    
  
    Unread counts look like neutral information. In practice, a big red number is a to-do list you did not write. If someone subscribes to 80 feeds and is away for a week, they return to "1,284 unread", and the reading app becomes a source of guilt. Many people end up declaring "RSS bankruptcy": marking everything read and starting over, or quitting. Silvio Rizzi's 2024 redesign of Reeder took a strong position: no unread counts at all, a single timeline that remembers where you stopped, and the freedom to skip. Other readers keep counts but let you hide them, or show them in muted grey without a badge on the app icon.

    • Make skip, mute and "mark older read" gentle and undoable.

    • Append "suggested" items after the reader's own sources end.

    Writing tools
    
  
    Writing software is reading software with a cursor, plus one terrifying responsibility: the words do not exist anywhere else yet.     The blank page, focus and typewriter scrolling
    
  
    Taught by Oliver Reichenstein and iA (iA Writer), with Hemingway Editor and every writer who has stared at a cursor
    
  
    A writing tool should make starting easy, keep the writer's eyes where the words are being made, and keep judgement (editing, styling, counting) out of the way until the writer asks for it. Drafting and editing are different modes; design them as different modes.
    
  
    The blank page is hard because writing is two activities at once: generating and judging. Generating needs momentum; judging kills it. Many writers' advice ("write drunk, edit sober" is a popular misattribution; the idea is older and widely shared) comes down to separating the two. Software can help or hurt. A toolbar full of fonts, styles and a live word count is an invitation to judge while generating. A calm window with one well-set typeface and nothing else is an invitation to keep going.
    
  
    iA Writer, released for iPad in 2010, made this its whole product. It opened to a blank document in one carefully tuned typeface, with no font choice at all. Its Focus Mode dimmed everything except the current sentence (later also the current paragraph), so the writer's attention stayed on the line being made. Its typewriter mode kept that line at a fixed height on screen, so the eyes never drifted to the bottom edge while typing. Those ideas have since been copied widely, which is the best evidence they work.

You can switch on just adverbs, for example, and see at a glance where you leaned on "very" and "really". The Hemingway Editor highlights long sentences, passive voice and adverbs. Both share a design principle: they are off by default, or easy to switch off, because their colours are judgement, and judgement belongs to the editing pass.

    • Open to a ready cursor in a new or last document, never to a dialog or a template picker.
    • Offer focus (sentence or paragraph) and typewriter scrolling as toggles with keyboard shortcuts.
    • Keep editing aids (style checks, highlighting, counts) off or quiet while drafting.
    • Pick one excellent default writing face at the right size; make font choice optional.
    
    
    Don't
    • Underline every possible grammar issue while the writer is mid-sentence.
    • Show a big live word count beside the text.
    • Ask for a title or a file name before the first word.
    • Make distraction-free mode hide the save status; writers need to know their words are safe.

    Markdown, WYSIWYG and blocks
    
  
    Taught by John Gruber (Markdown), with Bear, Typora, Notion and Google Docs
    
  
    Every editor makes a trade between seeing structure and seeing the result. Markdown shows structure as plain characters, WYSIWYG shows the result and hides structure, and block editors make structure into objects. Choose by who writes, what they produce, and where the text must go next.
    
  
    John Gruber, with Aaron Swartz, released Markdown in 2004 as a way to write for the web in plain text that was readable as-is: *emphasis*, # heading, - list. Its genius is that the source is the document. Any text editor can open it in 50 years. Its cost is that you see the markup while writing, and newcomers do not know the syntax.
    
  
    WYSIWYG ("what you see is what you get") editors, from word processors to Google Docs, hide the structure and show formatted text. They are easy to learn, but the structure is hidden in a format you cannot see, and it goes wrong in invisible ways: a heading that is really bold body text, a list that is really indented paragraphs, formatting that will not paste cleanly elsewhere.
    
  
    Between them, hybrid editors (Bear, iA Writer's preview styles, Typora, Obsidian's live preview) keep Markdown as the source but style it as you type, often fading the markup characters. Block editors (Notion, and many tools since) turn each paragraph, heading, image or to-do into a separate object you can drag, convert and embed. Blocks are powerful for structured pages and databases; they make long flowing prose feel chopped, because selection, cursor movement and copy-paste work across block boundaries less naturally than across one continuous text.

    Files, libraries, autosave, versions and export
    
  
    Taught by iA Writer and Obsidian (files), Ulysses and Bear (libraries), with Ink & Switch's local-first essay
    
  
    The first feature of a writing tool is that the words are safe. Decide where text lives (files the writer owns, or a library you manage), save continuously without asking, keep a history the writer can browse, and make leaving easy. Trust is the product.
    
  
    Writers remember lost work for years. One lost paragraph changes how a person uses a tool forever: they start copying text to a second app "just in case", which is the opposite of calm. So safety is not a technical detail under the design; it is the design.
    

  
    Where does the text live?
    
  
    There are three broad models. In the file model (iA Writer, Obsidian, most code editors), each document is a plain file in a folder the writer chooses: they can open it in any other app, back it up with any tool, sync it with any service. The app is a lens on their files. In the library model (Ulysses, Bear, Apple Notes), the app manages storage itself, often a database or package, and offers organisation, search and sync that feel seamless; the cost is that text is less directly accessible, so export becomes essential. In the cloud model (Google Docs, Notion), documents live on a server, which makes collaboration and access from anywhere easy, but the writer depends on the service and the network.
    

  

  
    The "local-first software" essay published by the research lab Ink & Switch in 2019 argued for a fourth path that combines them: data lives on the user's device first (fast, offline, owned), and syncs and collaborates through the cloud, often using data structures called CRDTs that merge concurrent edits without conflicts. For a solo creator, open-source libraries such as Automerge and Yjs make this far more practical than it used to be.
    

  
    Autosave: never ask
    
  
    "Do you want to save changes?" is a question the software should answer itself. Since Mac OS X Lion (2011), Apple's document model saves automatically and keeps versions; Google Docs saved continuously from the start. The design pattern: save after every pause (a second or two after typing stops) and on every blur or quit; show a quiet, truthful status ("Saved", "Saving…", "Offline, saved on this device"); and never show "Saved" when it is not true. If saving fails, that is the one time to be loud.
    

  
    
    
    
  
    

  
    Versions the writer can understand
    
  
    Autosave creates a new problem: the writer may want yesterday's paragraph back. A good version history is a timeline, browsable by time, with the ability to see a past version side by side, copy a paragraph out of it, or restore it as a new version (never destroying the current one). Automatic snapshots should be thinned over time (every few minutes today, hourly this week, daily after that), and writers should be able to name a version ("Sent to editor"), so the important moments stand out from the noise.
    

  

  
    Export is a promise
    
  
    For a library or cloud tool, export is how you prove you are not a trap. Offer full export of everything (not just one document at a time) in open formats: Markdown or plain text for content, with folders or tags preserved somehow, plus attachments. Offer publication formats (PDF, DOCX, HTML, ePub) for finished work. Ulysses, for example, is a library app whose export to many formats is a central feature. The read-later shutdowns in chapter 10 are the reminder: when a service ends, export is the difference between a nuisance and a loss.
    

  
    
    
    Do
    • Save continuously after each pause and on blur, quit and backgrounding.
    • Show a truthful status, and be loud only when saving fails.
    • Keep browsable, thinned versions with optional names; restore as a new version.
    • Offer full export in open formats, including attachments and structure.
    • Work offline and sync later, with clear conflict handling.
    
    
    Don't
    • Ask "Save changes?" on close.
    • Show "Saved" before the write is confirmed.
    • Silently resolve sync conflicts by discarding one side.
    • Make export per-document only, or paywall it.

    Common mistakes
    
    
    Last-write-wins sync

    Capture, desk and other people
    
  
    Taught by Drafts (Greg Pierce), Scrivener, Ulysses, with Google Docs for collaboration
    
  
    Writing happens in different places with different needs: a phone in a queue (capture), a desk with an hour (compose), and sometimes a shared document (collaborate). Design for the context: fast and forgiving for capture, deep and structured for composing, clear about who changed what for collaboration.
    
  
    A thought that arrives in a queue has a life of about ten seconds. If your app needs five taps (open, pick a folder, new note, name it, type), the thought is gone. Drafts, by Agile Tortoise, is built on this observation: it opens to a blank text field with the keyboard up, every time, and decides where the text goes later through "actions" (send to email, append to a file, post somewhere). Apple Notes added quick notes from the lock screen and corner gestures for the same reason. Capture tools optimise for one metric: time from intent to typing.
    
  
    The desk is the opposite. A novelist with a 90,000-word manuscript needs structure: chapters and scenes as separate pieces that can be reordered, research beside the draft, targets and progress. Scrivener's binder and corkboard are built for that; Ulysses' sheets and groups and its writing goals are a lighter version. Here the metric is how well the tool holds a big, changing structure.
    

  

  
    Counts, goals and the danger of the number
    
  
    Word counts help writers who work to length (articles, essays, books) and to habit (500 words a day). They hurt when they become the point. The design answer is to make counts available, scoped and quiet: a count for the selection, the document and the session; a goal the writer sets themselves; progress shown as a small ring or bar that fills, not a number that flickers with every keystroke. Ulysses shows goals as a small circle that fills as you write; Scrivener has project and session targets. Neither puts a giant counter in the text area.
    

  

  
    Writing with other people: the basics
    
  
    Collaboration adds three needs to a writing tool. Presence: who else is here, and where (named cursors and selections). Attribution: who changed what, and when (history per author). And negotiation: proposing a change without imposing it. Google Docs set the expectations: live cursors, comments anchored to text, and "suggesting" mode, where edits appear as proposals the owner accepts or rejects. Comments and suggestions are different tools. A comment discusses; a suggestion changes. Making that difference visible saves a lot of confusion.
    

  
    
    
    
  
    

  
    
    
    Do
    • Make capture open to a ready cursor in under two seconds, with no required choices.
    • Provide an inbox and batch sorting for captured text.
    • Scope counts (selection, document, session) and show goals as quiet progress.
    • Keep comments and suggestions visually distinct; attribute every change.
    
    
    Don't
    • Ask for a folder, title or tag before the first word on mobile.
    • Put a large live word count in the writing area.
    • Let collaborators' cursors and avatars cover the text being written.
    • Notify every collaborator on every keystroke-level change.

    The desktop editor is shrunk onto the phone.
    Design the mobile surface for capture and light editing first; keep deep structure tools for larger screens.

    Streaks and red "missed" days seem motivating.
    Let writers set and pause goals, celebrate quietly, and never shame a missed day.

    Listening, dyslexia and claims versus evidence
    
  
    Taught by Microsoft Immersive Reader, Kindle Immersion Reading, and reading research (Zorzi, Rello, Wery & Diliberto)
    
  
    Reading tools should let people take in text the way that works for them: by eye, by ear, or both together, with spacing and type they can adjust. Base these features on evidence, not on marketing claims, because some popular "reading hacks" do not work.
    
  
    Accessibility for reading starts with the basics you already have from Part one: real text (never text in images), adjustable size, good contrast, themes, and support for the platform's screen reader and text size. Accessible & Inclusive covers the general principles. Here we go into what is specific to long reading.
    

  
    Listening and read-along
    
  
    Text-to-speech used to sound robotic; modern system voices are good enough for hours of listening, and many people now "read" articles while walking or cooking. Two design patterns matter. First, listening needs its own controls: play and pause, speed (people often listen at 1.2 to 2 times normal), skip by sentence or paragraph, and a sleep timer. Second, read-along, where the spoken word or sentence is highlighted as it is read, helps learners, people with dyslexia, and anyone following a hard text. Microsoft's Immersive Reader (launched in OneNote's Learning Tools in 2016 and now in Edge, Word and other apps) combines read-aloud with highlighting, line focus, text spacing and syllable splitting. Kindle's Immersion Reading pairs an e-book with its Audible narration and highlights the text as it plays. The EPUB 3 standard supports this through "media overlays".
    

  
    
    
    Lab: read-along highlighting (visual only, no audio)
    
    
    Time is but the stream I go a-fishing in. I drink at it; but while I drink I see the sandy bottom and detect how shallow it is. Its thin current slides away, but eternity remains.
    
    
    
      
    
      
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
    
    Play it at 180, then at 320 words per minute. Word highlighting at high speed becomes a flicker that pulls the eye; sentence highlighting stays calm. That is why many read-along tools highlight the sentence and underline the word, and why speed must be the listener's choice. "Step" moves one word at a time (and is the only mode if you prefer reduced motion).
    
  
    

  
    Dyslexia: what helps and what is claimed
    
  
    Dyslexia is a difference in how people process written language, and it is common: estimates vary with definitions, but a figure of around one person in ten is often cited. Several typefaces have been marketed as "dyslexia fonts", such as Dyslexie and OpenDyslexic, with weighted bottoms and distinct letter shapes. Controlled studies have not found that they help. Rello and Baeza-Yates (2013) found that font type affected reading for people with dyslexia, with sans-serif, monospaced and roman (upright) styles doing better than italic ones, but OpenDyslexic gave no advantage. Wery and Diliberto (2017) found no improvement in reading rate or accuracy with OpenDyslexic for students with dyslexia, and Kuster and colleagues (2018) found the same for Dyslexie compared with Arial.
    
  
    What has stronger evidence is spacing. Zorzi and colleagues (PNAS, 2012) found that extra-large letter spacing improved reading speed and accuracy for children with dyslexia, likely by reducing crowding (letters interfering with recognition of their neighbours). Larger text, more line spacing, shorter lines, left-aligned text (no justified rivers), and avoiding italics and underlining for emphasis are all consistent with guidance such as the British Dyslexia Association's style guide. Many readers with dyslexia do say they prefer a specific font, and preference matters for comfort, so it is fine to offer one as an option. Just do not claim it treats anything.
    

  
    
    
    
  
    

  
    "Bionic" reading and other speed claims
    
  
    In 2022, "Bionic Reading" spread widely: bold the first few letters of each word, the claim goes, and the eye reads faster because the brain completes the rest. It is an appealing idea and a striking visual. But informal tests at the time, including one widely shared by Readwise, found no speed benefit, and the small number of studies since have not found reliable gains in speed or comprehension either. Speed-reading techniques like rapid serial visual presentation (one word flashed at a time) can push speed up, but research reviews have found that comprehension drops as speed rises; you cannot skip the work of understanding. Offer such modes if users ask, label them honestly, and never make them the default.

    Don't

    • Market bolding or flashing words as a proven speed boost.
    • Put text in images or canvases that screen readers cannot read.
    • Use italics or underline as the main emphasis in long text.

    Phone, tablet, e-ink and desktop
    
  
    Taught by the devices themselves, with Craig Mod's writing on books and screens
    
  
    The same text is a different reading experience on each device, because distance, size, input, light and session length all change. Design one model of the text and a different reading surface for each device class.
    
  
    A phone is read at around 30 cm, in one hand, in short sessions, often at night, with a thumb. A tablet is held further, often in landscape, in longer sessions, sometimes with a stylus. An e-ink reader is a single-purpose object read for hours, in sunlight and in bed, with page buttons or taps and no motion. A desktop is read at 50 to 70 cm, with a keyboard and mouse, often alongside other windows, for research and writing more than for novels. Each changes the right measure, the right controls and the right defaults.

    Don't
    • Stretch a phone layout onto a tablet or a desktop.
    • Sync text size blindly across devices.
    • Ship the phone's animations to an e-ink build.
    • Rely on hover for anything a touch reader also needs.

    Wrap-up
    
  
    You have now seen reading and writing software from the line up: the length of a line, the light behind it, the way through a long text, the marks you leave, the feeds that bring text to you, and the tools that make new text safely. One idea ran through all of it: these products succeed when the person forgets about them and stays with the words.
    
  
    Ten things to remember
      
    
    1. In reading and writing tools, attention is the product. Measure success by what the person did not have to notice.
      2. 
    
    2. Sort every feature into get in, stay in, get back. "Stay in" is where doing less is the work.
      3. 
    
    3. Pages for long linear reading, scroll for articles and reference. If you offer both, finish both, and store position as a text anchor.
      4. 
    
    4. Measure 45 to 75 characters (capped in em), line height around 1.5 for long text, real margins, ragged right unless you can hyphenate.
      5. 
    
    5. Choose book faces for stamina: large x-height, open apertures, sturdy serifs, moderate contrast.
      6. 
    
    6. Defaults are your design. Layer controls: size and theme first, typography second, expert settings behind a door, all previewed live.
      7. 
    
    7. Themes are about light: light by day, softened dark at night, low brightness before sleep, and no motion or greys on e-ink.
      8. 
    
    8. Progress answers a decision ("can I finish this chapter?"), so make it chapter-scoped, personal and stable, with safe jumps and asked-for syncs.
      9. 
    
    9. Highlights are worth what they are worth outside your app: one-tap marking, notes beside text, export with a link home. Notes and definitions open in place.
      10. 
    
    10. For writing: start in under two seconds, separate drafting from editing, save continuously and truthfully, keep readable versions, and make leaving easy.
