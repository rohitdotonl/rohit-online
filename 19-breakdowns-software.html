Breakdowns: Software. Eighteen products, taken apart one decision at a time.

Good software is a stack of decisions somebody made on purpose. This guide gives you a method for seeing those decisions, then walks through eighteen products, from iA Writer to UPI payments, naming what each one chose, what it gave up, and what you can steal for the product you are building alone.

By the end you will be able to

  • Explain why each of eighteen well-known products looks and behaves the way it does, in terms of the job it serves and the business that pays for it (understand).
  • Run a structured, screen-by-screen teardown of any app through nine lenses, and separate taste from mechanism (analyse).
  • Judge whether a celebrated pattern fits your own product, or only fits its original context (evaluate).
  • Write and publish a teardown of a product you use daily, and turn it into concrete changes for your own software (create).

Warm-up: what do you already see?

Before the method, a quick diagnostic. You have used most of these products, or products like them. The point is not to score well. The point is to notice how much you already sense about why software feels the way it feels, and how often that sense is vague. By the end of the guide the vague feelings should have names.

Diagnostic 1

Things 3 lets you assign a to-do to "This Evening". Which explanation is closest to the real design reason?

The feature mirrors a mental model people already have: the day has a work half and a home half. It keeps the Today list honest without adding a scheduling system. That is the habit this guide trains: ask what job a decision does for a real person, not what it looks like.
Diagnostic 2

Craigslist has looked roughly the same for over twenty years and is still heavily used. What is the most useful lesson for you?

Craigslist is a lesson in fit, not in ugliness. Its users want to scan many listings quickly, and the page does that well. Answer a overreaches: the visual design is doing real work (density, predictability, speed). You will meet this idea again in chapter 18.
How to study this guide

Do not read all eighteen breakdowns in one go. Read Part one first, because it gives you the method. Then read one breakdown per sitting, with the product open on your phone or laptop if you can. Each breakdown ends with an exercise. Do at least one exercise per part. The final chapter hands you a template, and your real assignment is to write teardowns of your own.

Part one

The method

A breakdown is not a review. A review says whether something is good. A breakdown says what was decided, why, and at what cost, so that you can make better decisions yourself. These two chapters give you the lenses and the routine. Every product chapter after this uses them.

1

Nine lenses for reading software

Taught by Don Norman, with Dieter Rams, Jesse James Garrett and the object breakdowns of Guide 18

Every piece of software is the visible end of a chain of decisions, from "what job is this for" down to "what does this button say"; reading software well means walking that chain on purpose, one lens at a time.

When you open a beautiful app, your first reaction is a feeling: calm, fast, fussy, cheap. Feelings are useful data, but they are not explanations. You cannot steal a feeling. You can steal a decision. So the first skill of a breakdown is to translate "this feels calm" into "the app hides everything except the current paragraph, uses one typeface at one size, and never shows a badge". Now you have three decisions you can test in your own product.

In Breakdowns: Objects you used seven lenses for physical things. Software needs the same seven, adapted, plus two questions that physical objects rarely force on you: how the business model shapes the screen, and what was deliberately left out. Software is infinitely extensible, so omission is the hardest and most revealing decision of all.

The lenses, briefly

Mechanism over adjectives

The most common failure in teardowns is the adjective trap: "clean", "intuitive", "delightful", "modern". These words describe your reaction, not the product. Replace every adjective with a sentence that has a decision, a reason and a cost. "Clean" becomes "uses a single accent colour, so the only coloured thing on screen is the thing you can act on; cost: nothing else can draw attention, including warnings".

Why the business model gets its own lens

A free app paid for by ads must win back your attention, so it grows notifications, feeds and badges. A subscription app must justify a monthly charge, so it ships visible features and sync. A one-time purchase must win you at the moment of sale, so it invests in the first impression and in craft that reviewers notice. None of these is evil by default. But you cannot understand why Duolingo nags and iA Writer does not without asking who pays and for what. Many design "mysteries" are just the revenue model showing through the UI.

The ninth lens is the hardest: what is not there? Things has no collaboration. iA Writer has almost no formatting toolbar. Linear does not let every team invent its own workflow. Craigslist has no images on its home page. Absences are invisible unless you list the obvious features a competitor has and ask, for each one, "did they forget, or did they refuse?"

Key idea

You cannot steal a feeling. You can steal a decision, once you have named it, found its reason, and priced its cost.

Do

  • Write each observation as decision, reason, cost.
  • Start from the job, then explain the screen.
  • Ask who pays and how that shapes what you see.
  • List the features a competitor has and this product refused.

Don't

  • Use adjectives ("clean", "intuitive") as findings.
  • Copy the visual layer without the model underneath.
  • Assume every choice was intentional; some are accidents or legacy.
  • Judge a product by your job when it was built for someone else's.

In the wild

  • The Elements of User ExperienceJesse James Garrett, 2002
    Garrett's five planes (strategy, scope, structure, skeleton, surface) are the ancestor of these lenses: decisions on the lower, abstract planes constrain the upper, visible ones. Explain a surface by looking underneath it.
  • Teardown writingMany designers, 2010s onward
    The most useful public teardowns (for example long-form app reviews on MacStories, or onboarding teardowns on sites that collect them) work screen by screen and name decisions. The weakest ones rate things out of ten. Read the good ones as models for your own notes.
  • The Design of Everyday ThingsDon Norman, 1988, revised 2013
    Norman's conceptual model and feedback ideas are lens 2 and lens 6 in this guide. The vocabulary you already have from The Human Mind is your teardown toolkit.

Common mistakes

Stopping at the surface

Visuals are what you notice first, and they are the easiest thing to write about.

Force yourself to write the Purpose and Model lenses before you are allowed to write about visuals.

Treating fame as proof

A famous product feels like a safe source of answers, so every choice looks deliberate and correct.

For each decision, also write who it fails. Famous products make bad bets too, and some survive in spite of them.

Ignoring the context gap

You copy a pattern from a 100-person team's product into a one-person product with different users.

Add a final line to each finding: "does this fit my users, my scale, my business?"

Check yourself

You notice a free photo-editing app shows a "Daily challenge" badge every morning. Which lens most likely explains it first?

A free app needs repeat visits to earn from ads or to convert to paid, so a daily hook is a revenue decision first. The visual and the words of the badge come after. Starting from the business model explains why the badge exists, not just how it looks.

How to do a teardown yourself

Taught by working practice, with Jakob Nielsen (heuristic review) and the cognitive walkthrough method

A teardown is a routine, not an inspiration: capture every screen of one real task, pin each decision with a number, then write decision, reason, cost and steal for each pin.

If you just browse an app looking for good ideas, you will find the ones you already agree with. A protocol fixes that. It makes you look at the boring screens (settings, errors, empty states, the paywall) where products reveal their real priorities. It also produces an artefact you can come back to, which matters more than any single insight.

The protocol, step by step

  1. Pick one task, phrased as a user would. "Pay my friend back ₹450 at a tea stall", not "test the payments flow". Write the starting state too: fresh install, or a returning user with data.
  2. Capture every screen, including the boring ones. Use the system screenshot shortcut (or a screen recording, then pull frames). Number the files in order. Include loading, empty, error and permission screens. On desktop, capture at a consistent window size.
  3. Make a strip. Put the screenshots in a row in any canvas tool (Figma, Keynote, even a slide). A strip shows the rhythm of a flow: how many steps, where it slows down, where it asks for something.
  4. Pin decisions with numbers, not arrows. Put a small numbered circle on each decision. Arrows and handwriting clutter the screenshot; numbers keep it readable and map to a list.
  5. Write the list. For each pin: decision (what), reason (why you think), cost (what it gives up), and confidence (high, medium, guess). Mark guesses as guesses.
  6. Compare with one rival. Do the same task in a competitor. The differences are where the decisions become visible.
  7. Steal one thing. End with one concrete change for your own product, small enough to ship this week.
For the solo creator

Keep one folder called teardowns/ with a subfolder per product and task. Name screenshots 01-launch.png, 02-empty.png and so on. It feels bureaucratic for one person, but after six months it becomes your private pattern library, more useful than any public gallery because every entry has your reasons attached. When you design a new screen, you search your own folder first.

Watch out

Screenshots of other products are for your study. If you publish a teardown, use your own annotations, keep the screenshots small and clearly about commentary, and never present someone else's UI as your design.

Common mistakes

The tour instead of the task

Opening every tab feels thorough.

Pick one job and follow it end to end. Tours produce feature lists; tasks produce decisions.

Fresh-install bias

Teardowns often only look at onboarding because it is easy to reproduce.

Also tear down the 100th use: an account with data, where speed and density matter more than welcome screens.

No rival

Without a comparison, every choice looks inevitable.

Do the same task in one competitor. Differences reveal decisions.

Check yourself

You want to learn how a banking app handles failure. Which teardown task is best?

A realistic task that forces the failure path shows you the error wording, retry logic and state recovery: exactly the decisions you wanted to see. Exploring screens produces a feature list, not insight into failure.
Exercise 2.1 60 minutes · on a payment or sign-up flow you use

Your first strip

  1. Choose one task in an app on your phone (for example, sign up, pay, or book).
  2. Screenshot every screen of the task, including any loading or error screens.
  3. Lay them out in a row in any canvas tool and add numbered pins (aim for 6 to 10).
  4. Write decision, reason, cost and confidence for each pin.

iA Writer

Taught by Oliver Reichenstein and Information Architects

A plain-text writing app that treats the writer's attention as the scarce resource, and so makes almost every decision on the writer's behalf: one font family, one size logic, very few settings, and tools that show you your own text more clearly rather than decorate it.

MakerInformation Architects (iA), Zurich and Tokyo
Year2010 (iPad), then Mac, iPhone, Android, Windows
CategoryMarkdown writing app
Study it forFocus, typography as interface, opinionated defaults

iA began as a design studio that wrote loudly about web typography ("Web Design is 95% Typography" is one of its well-known essays). iA Writer is that argument turned into a product. When it launched on the iPad in 2010, writing apps competed on features: styles, fonts, toolbars. iA Writer competed on absence. It opened to a blank page in a monospaced font and asked nothing of you.

  1. Focus mode dims everything except the current sentence or paragraph. The writer's working memory holds about one sentence; the rest of the page is a distraction that invites rereading and polishing too early. Cost: you lose peripheral context, so it is a mode you turn on, not the default.
  2. A typeface designed for the app. Early versions used a customized version of Nitti, a monospace by Bold Monday. Around 2017 and 2018 iA made its own family, iA Writer Mono, Duo and Quattro, derived from IBM Plex Mono and released for free. Duo is "duospaced" (most letters share one width, wide letters like m and w get more) and Quattro uses four widths, keeping a typewriter's draft feel while reading more evenly. Cost: the look is unmistakable, and people who want a proportional serif must pick from very few choices.
  3. Markdown marks stay visible, but quiet. The # of a heading sits grey in the margin, so the text stays plain and portable while structure is still legible. Cost: newcomers must learn a little syntax; there is no bold button doing the work for them.
  4. The library is your file system. Documents are plain text files in folders you own (iCloud, Dropbox, a local folder). No proprietary database, no lock-in. Cost: no rich metadata, and sync problems become the file provider's problems, which users still blame on the app.
  5. Content blocks. Typing a path like /chart.png on its own line pulls in an image, a CSV table or another text file. It keeps the document text-only while allowing rich output. Cost: it is a power feature that few users discover without docs.
  6. Authorship (iA Writer 7, late 2023). Text you paste from an AI tool can be marked as not yours and shown in a distinct style until you rewrite it; you can also attribute text to named authors. The app takes a public position: AI as a sparring partner, not a ghostwriter. Cost: extra friction for people who want the machine to write for them, which is the point.
  7. Tools that show, not change. Syntax highlighting can colour adjectives, nouns, adverbs, verbs and conjunctions, and Style Check flags filler words and clichés. They hold a mirror to your prose instead of rewriting it. Cost: they are opinionated and English-first.
  8. Very few settings, on purpose. iA has argued for years that each preference is a decision the designer pushed onto the user. The app has grown some settings over time, but each is resisted. Cost: you cannot tune it to taste, and some people leave for that reason. This is "the no-settings price".
Focus mode, live

Most apps ask for attention. A few give it back. The best tools disappear while you work, and return only when you call them. That is a design choice, not luck. Every setting you add is one more thing that can look back at you. Click any sentence to move the focus.

Without: every sentence competes. Your eyes drift back to the paragraph you already wrote and start editing it.
With: only the current sentence is at full contrast. Click another sentence (or focus it and press Enter) to move the spotlight. Notice how hard it becomes to reread.

Trade-offs and criticisms

The usual criticisms are fair and worth learning from. Monospace is not the best face for long reading, so iA added Duo and Quattro and a preview mode in proportional type. The lack of settings frustrates writers with established habits. The file-based library means features that need a database (tags across files, backlinks) arrive late or not at all, because each one has to work on plain files. And opinionated tools like Style Check can feel preachy. Each criticism is the shadow of a decision that also makes the app good.

Steal this for your software

  • Find the one resource your user is short of (attention, time, money) and make every default protect it.
  • Store user data in an open format whenever you can. It is a promise you can make that big companies often will not.
  • Before adding a setting, write down the decision you are avoiding. Make it yourself if you can.
  • Build tools that show users their own work more clearly, rather than tools that do the work for them.
  • Take a public position on a contested question (like AI text) and design it into the product.
For the solo creator

iA Writer is a small team's product, and its strategy is a solo creator's strategy: fewer features, each done carefully, defended in writing. Your essays and changelogs explaining why you refuse a feature are part of the design. They turn "missing" into "chosen".

Copy

  • Opinionated defaults explained in public.
  • One typographic system used everywhere.
  • Open file formats.

Don't copy blindly

  • Monospace for a reading app.
  • Zero settings for an expert tool where users have real, different needs.
  • Hidden power features without any discovery path.

Related products

  • UlyssesThe Soulmen, 2003; iOS later
    A Markdown-style writing app with a managed library instead of plain folders, which enables goals, sheets and publishing. Same calm, opposite bet on storage.
  • Typora2015
    Hides Markdown marks as you type and renders inline. The other answer to "should syntax be visible?"
  • WriteRoomHog Bay Software, 2006
    An early full-screen "distraction-free" Mac editor. Focus as a product idea predates iA; iA made it typographic.

Common mistakes when copying iA Writer

Minimal by deletion

You remove buttons without replacing their function, so users cannot format at all.

Move functions to a better place (text syntax, shortcuts, a command menu). Minimal means relocated, not missing.

Focus mode as decoration

It looks impressive in a demo.

Ask whether your user's task has a "current unit" (a sentence, a row, a step). If not, a focus mode just hides things.

Check yourself

You are building a journaling app and want iA Writer's calm. Which move best transfers the underlying decision, not just the look?

The transferable decision is "protect the scarce resource". Monospace and focus mode are iA's answers for drafting prose; your answers depend on your user's moment. Removing settings without making the decisions yourself just moves the problem.
Exercise 3.1 40 minutes · on your own product

Price your settings

  1. List every setting or preference in your product (or planned for it).
  2. For each, write the decision it avoids ("which date format?", "how dense?").
  3. Mark each as: keep (users truly differ), decide (you can choose well for them), or delete.
  4. Rewrite your settings screen with only the "keep" items, and write one sentence for your changelog explaining a removed setting.

Things 3

Taught by Cultured Code

A personal task manager that wins by modelling how one person thinks about a day (today, this evening, someday) and by making every interaction feel physical and unhurried, while flatly refusing to become a team tool.

MakerCultured Code, Stuttgart
Year2017 (Things 1 was 2009)
CategoryPersonal task manager, Apple platforms
Study it forMental models, restraint, motion, the Magic Plus button

Things 3 shipped in 2017 after years of quiet work, and won an Apple Design Award. Its competitors had more features: shared lists, comments, integrations. Things had a better model of a single person's day. It still sells as separate one-time purchases per platform, with free sync, and no subscription.

  1. Today is a list you curate, not a filter of due dates. You decide what goes in Today. Due dates and scheduled dates feed it, but the list is a promise to yourself, short enough to finish. Cost: people who want an automatic "everything due" view must learn the difference between "when" and "deadline".
  2. This Evening. A tiny split that matches how many people live: work things, then home things. It keeps Today from feeling like a single wall. Cost: almost none, which is why it is a lovely decision.
  3. The Magic Plus button. Tap it to add at the end; drag it to place a new to-do exactly where you want it in the list, or drop it on the left to send to Inbox, or create a heading. One control, many outcomes, all learned by direct manipulation. Cost: discoverability. People who only tap never find the drag.
  4. A small, fixed set of lists. Inbox, Today, Upcoming, Anytime, Someday, Logbook. Not a user-defined system. The model comes from the Getting Things Done tradition, simplified. Cost: people with an elaborate personal system feel boxed in.
  5. Headings inside projects. A project can be divided into sections without becoming sub-projects. Shallow structure stays shallow. Cost: no deep nesting, by design.
  6. Deadlines are rare and red. A deadline is separate from "when" and shown with a quiet countdown. Red appears only there, so it means something. Cost: you need to understand two date concepts.
  7. Tags are light and optional. Small grey lozenges, filterable, never required. Cost: tag-heavy users want more.
  8. Quick Entry everywhere, with natural dates. On the Mac a global shortcut opens a small window; you can type "tomorrow" or "next fri" in the when field. Capture must cost under five seconds or ideas are lost. Cost: another shortcut to learn.
  9. No collaboration, by design. No shared lists, no assignees, no comments. Cost: families and teams leave for Todoist, Reminders or a team tool. Benefit: none of the screens carry the weight of other people.

Trade-offs and criticisms

Things releases slowly; years can pass between visible features, and some users read that as neglect. It is Apple-only, so anyone on Android or Windows is out. Its refusal of collaboration rules out households. And some power users find the fixed model too gentle: no task dependencies, limited recurrence logic compared with OmniFocus. Every criticism points to a door Cultured Code chose to keep closed. The lesson is that saying no costs customers, and the product is better for those who stay.

Steal this for your software

  • Model the user's real day, not your database. Name lists after moments ("this evening") rather than fields ("priority 2").
  • Let one direct-manipulation gesture answer two questions at once.
  • Make the required input for capture exactly one field.
  • Reserve your loudest colour for one meaning (deadlines, errors) and use it nowhere else.
  • Decide who you are not for, and let the product show it.

Copy

  • A small fixed set of views based on a clear model.
  • Gestures with a tap fallback.
  • Calm motion that confirms state (items settle, not bounce).

Don't copy blindly

  • Hidden gestures as the only route to a feature.
  • Slow release cadence without telling users what you are working on.
  • A single-person model if your users really share work.

Related products

  • OmniFocusThe Omni Group, 2008
    A deep Getting Things Done tool with perspectives, contexts and review. The opposite bet: power over calm. Both are coherent.
  • TodoistDoist, 2007
    Cross-platform, natural-language entry ("every Monday at 9"), shared projects. Wins on reach and sharing where Things refuses.
  • Apple RemindersApple, 2011
    Free, shared lists, grocery sections. The default you are always competing with.

Common mistakes when copying Things

Copying the polish, not the model

The animations and spacing are what screenshots show.

Write your product's model first (what lists exist and why). Polish a weak model and it stays weak.

Gesture-only features

Drag-to-insert is delightful, so it feels like enough.

Always give a tap and keyboard path. Things does: tap adds at the end, and the Mac has full shortcuts.

Check yourself

Users of your habit app keep asking for "priority levels 1 to 5". What would a Things-style answer look like?

Things answers the need behind "priority" (what should I do now?) with a list the user curates. That respects the request without importing a field that becomes noise. Ignoring users is not the lesson; translating their request into your model is.

Apple Notes and Bear

Taught by Apple and Shiny Frog

Two note apps that show two kinds of good defaults: Apple Notes is approachable because it asks nothing (open, type, done), and Bear is approachable because its structure grows out of the text itself (a hashtag in a note is the organization).

MakersApple; Shiny Frog (Italy)
YearsNotes on iPhone 2007, big rework 2015; Bear 2016, Bear 2 in 2023
CategoryNote-taking
Study it forZero-setup defaults, tags as structure, typography

Notes apps live or die on the first ten seconds: an idea arrives, and the tool must catch it before it fades. Apple Notes takes the shortest path: the first line you type becomes the title, there is no "save", and the note lives in the last folder you used. Bear, which won an Apple Design Award in 2017, keeps that speed and adds a structure most people can learn in one sentence: type #work/clients anywhere in a note, and the note appears under that nested tag in the sidebar.

  1. Tags are the folders. Bear has no separate folder system. Nested tags (#work/clients) give hierarchy, and a note can live in several places at once. Cost: a folder-minded person has to unlearn the idea that a note lives in one place.
  2. Structure is typed, not filed. You never stop writing to organize. Typing the tag is the filing. Cost: renaming a tag means rewriting text in many notes (Bear does this for you, which is a necessary feature that comes with the choice).
  3. Typography is a feature. Bear ships carefully chosen themes and fonts, generous line spacing and a reading width. Notes feel like something worth rereading. Cost: design effort that does not show up in a feature list.
  4. Wiki-style links. Double-bracket links connect notes by title, a lightweight version of what Obsidian does with files. Cost: links depend on titles staying stable.
  5. One search box does most of the work. Special search terms exist, but plain search is the main tool. Cost: power filtering is less visible.
  6. The Panda editor (Bear 2, 2023). Bear rebuilt its editor to be closer to standard Markdown, so text moves more cleanly in and out. Cost: a rewrite of the core of the product, years of work for a small team, with changes that some long-time users had to adjust to.
  7. Apple Notes: zero decisions at capture. No title field, no save, no folder prompt. The first line is the title. Cost: notes pile up unsorted; Apple added tags, smart folders and Quick Note later to help.
  8. Apple Notes: the free default. Pre-installed, synced, shared. Its existence sets the bar every paid notes app must clear. Cost to competitors, not to Apple.

Trade-offs and criticisms

Bear is Apple-only for its full app (a web version arrived later), and its sync relies on iCloud, which limits who can use it. Tags-as-folders confuses people who want one canonical location. Apple Notes, for its part, has no export story most people trust, uses a closed format, and gets messy at scale. Both are good at the first ten seconds and less good at the ten-thousandth note.

Steal this for your software

  • Make capture require zero decisions. Every field at capture time is a reason not to capture.
  • Let structure emerge from content (a typed tag, a mention, a date in text) instead of from forms.
  • Treat reading typography as a feature even in a "utility".
  • Know your free, pre-installed competitor, and be clearly better at one thing it does badly.

Copy

  • First line becomes the title.
  • Inline organization (tags, mentions).
  • A renaming tool for anything users type as structure.

Don't copy blindly

  • Tags as the only structure if your users need a single home per item (invoices, contracts).
  • A closed storage format for long-lived personal data.

Related products

Linear

Taught by Karri Saarinen, Tuomas Artman and Jori Lallo

An issue tracker for software teams that bet on one claim, that work tools should feel as fast and well made as consumer apps, and backed it with an architecture (local data, instant updates), an interaction model (keyboard first, command menu) and an opinion about how teams should work.

MakerLinear (Saarinen, Artman, Lallo)
YearFounded 2019
CategoryIssue tracking and project planning
Study it forPerceived speed, keyboard UX, opinionated workflow

Before Linear, issue trackers were mostly configurable databases: custom fields, custom workflows, slow pages, many clicks. Linear's founders came from design and engineering roles at large tech companies and built the tracker they wanted. The product feels instant because the data your screen needs is already on your machine. Changes appear immediately and sync in the background. That architectural choice is a design choice: it is the only way to make every click feel like nothing happened between intention and result.

  1. A command menu for everything (Cmd+K). Every action is reachable by typing its name. A newcomer can search; an expert can type. Cost: the menu must be maintained as carefully as the visible UI, and actions need good names.
  2. Shortcuts are shown where actions are listed. The menu teaches its own shortcuts: after choosing "Assign to…" a few times you notice the A beside it and start pressing A. Cost: shortcut collisions get harder as features grow.
  3. Instant by architecture. Linear keeps workspace data in the client and applies changes locally first (optimistic updates), syncing in the background. Every action feels like it happened when you pressed the key. Cost: a complex sync engine and conflict handling, a large engineering investment for a "feeling".
  4. Cycles, not open-ended sprints. Teams work in fixed time boxes that start and end automatically, and unfinished work rolls over. Planning becomes a rhythm rather than a meeting. Cost: teams with other rhythms must bend to it.
  5. Triage as a default inbox. New issues from other teams or integrations land in Triage, so someone decides before anything enters the backlog. Cost: someone must own triage.
  6. Dense, typographic rows. Small status shapes, monospaced IDs, priority bars instead of words. Fifty issues fit on a laptop screen. Cost: newcomers need a moment to learn the symbols.
  7. Opinions, published. "The Linear Method" is a public set of practices (for example, short specs, momentum over ceremony). The product implements the opinions; the writing explains them. Cost: customers who disagree feel the tool fighting them.
  8. Craft as marketing. Linear's website and release notes are carefully made, and its dark, gradient-lit landing-page style was copied widely across the industry in the early 2020s. Cost: imitators made the style generic, which pushes Linear to keep evolving.

Superhuman

Taught by Rahul Vohra, with Sean Ellis's product-market fit question

An email client that charges a premium for one promise, that you will get through your inbox faster than anywhere else, and designs everything around it: sub-100 ms interactions, a keyboard for every action, a split inbox, and onboarding that once happened in a one-to-one call.

MakerSuperhuman (Rahul Vohra); acquired by Grammarly in 2025
YearFounded 2014; invite-only for years
CategoryEmail client (Gmail and Outlook accounts)
Study it forSpeed as positioning, teaching shortcuts, measuring fit

Email is a solved problem in the sense that Gmail is free and works. Superhuman asked a very high monthly price (around $30 a month for years) and found people who paid it gladly. The design explains why: for someone who spends hours a day in email, saving minutes per hour is worth real money. Every decision serves that one customer, and the company was unusually explicit about choosing that customer, using a survey method that has since become standard reading for founders.

  1. The 100 ms rule. The team set a target that interactions should respond within about 100 milliseconds, the threshold at which people perceive a response as instant. Speed is not polished later; it is a requirement on every feature. Cost: features that cannot be fast are delayed or refused.
  2. Keyboard for everything, taught deliberately. Every action has a shortcut, and the command menu shows them. Hints nudge you when you use the mouse for something that has a key. Cost: a learning curve the product must pay for with teaching.
  3. One-to-one onboarding. For years, new users got a live session with a Superhuman specialist who set up their inbox and drilled shortcuts. It is expensive and does not scale, which was part of the point: it guaranteed that early users reached the fast state, and it taught the company what confused them. Cost: slow growth; later the company shifted toward self-serve onboarding.
  4. Split inbox. Mail is divided into streams (important people, team, news, calendar and so on), so you triage one kind of mail at a time. Cost: some setup, and mail occasionally lands in the wrong split.
  5. Opinions about inbox zero. "Done" (archive) is the main action, reminders bring mail back later, and an empty inbox is celebrated with calm imagery. Cost: people who use email as an archive or a to-do list may find the push uncomfortable.
  6. A premium price as a filter. A high price signals seriousness and funds the onboarding and speed work. Cost: most people will never try it.
  7. Read statuses, and a correction. Superhuman originally showed whether and where a recipient opened your email, using tracking pixels, on by default. After public criticism in 2019 (notably a widely read post by designer Mike Davidson), it removed location data and turned read statuses off by default. Cost: a lesson in how a "power feature" for one user can be surveillance for another.

The product-market fit survey

In a 2018 First Round Review essay, Vohra described how Superhuman used a single question popularized by Sean Ellis: "How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?" with answers very disappointed, somewhat disappointed, not disappointed. Ellis's benchmark is that products with fit tend to have at least 40% "very disappointed". Superhuman started at about 22%. It then segmented respondents, focused on the people who loved it, studied what they valued, asked the "somewhat disappointed" what held them back, and spent its roadmap roughly half on doubling down and half on removing those blockers. Vohra reported the score rising to 58% over the following quarters.

The price excludes most people, and early invite-only access read to some as exclusivity theatre.   • For any feature that observes someone, ask how it feels to the person observed.

Raycast, Alfred and Spotlight

Taught by Apple, Running with Crayons and Raycast

The launcher pattern: one global shortcut opens one text field, and typing a few letters reaches any app, file, command or action; Raycast extends the pattern into a platform where third parties add commands that all look and behave the same.

MakersApple (Spotlight, 2005); Running with Crayons (Alfred, 2010); Raycast (founded 2020)
CategoryLauncher and command bar for macOS
Study it forCommand interfaces, fuzzy search, extension design

Menus scale badly. A menu with eight items is easy; with eighty it is a maze. A launcher replaces navigation with recall: you know the name of the thing you want, so type it. Spotlight brought the idea to the Mac in 2005 (Command-Space). Quicksilver and LaunchBar had explored it earlier, Alfred built a business on it with paid workflows, and Raycast turned it into a developer platform where anyone can write a command in TypeScript and React that renders with Raycast's own list, form and detail components.

  1. One global shortcut, one field. The entire product is behind one key combination. There is no window to manage. Cost: if users never learn the shortcut, the product does not exist for them.
  2. Fuzzy matching and learned ranking. "cl" finds Clipboard History, Calendar and "Create Linear issue". Results you choose often rise to the top. Cost: rankings shift as habits change, which occasionally surprises muscle memory.
  3. Everything is a command in one list. Apps, files, system actions, calculations and extension commands share one result list. Cost: name collisions, solved by labelling the type of each result.
  4. Primary action plus an actions menu. Enter does the obvious thing; Command-K shows every other action for the selected item. Two levels, consistent everywhere. Cost: actions must be named and ordered with care for every result type.
  5. Extensions use the host's components. Raycast extensions are written with its UI components (lists, forms, details), so every third-party command looks native and keyboard-complete. Cost: extension authors give up visual freedom; that is the point.
  6. Built-in utilities that replace small apps. Clipboard history, window management, snippets and quicklinks. Cost: Raycast competes with many small developers' products.
  7. Business model: free core, paid Pro and team features. Raycast's core is free; paid plans add AI features, sync and team sharing. Alfred sells a one-time Powerpack license for workflows. Spotlight is part of the OS. Each model shapes how much the launcher pushes you toward its extras.

    YearFounded 2010; public launch 2011
    CategoryPayments infrastructure, APIs, hosted checkout
    Study it forDX as design, documentation, honest errors, Checkout

    In 2010, accepting card payments online meant merchant accounts, gateways, forms and weeks of paperwork. Stripe's early pitch was that a developer could start accepting payments with a few lines of code. That promise moved the design work to unusual places: the docs page, the API's naming, the JSON of an error response, the test card number 4242 4242 4242 4242.     1. Three-column docs: navigate, read, copy. Prose and the code it describes sit side by side, so you never scroll between explanation and example. Cost: on narrow screens the layout must collapse carefully, and every prose change needs a matching code change.
    2. Personalized samples. When you are signed in, code samples can include your own test API key, so pasted code runs as is. Cost: docs become a logged-in product with security considerations (only test keys, never live ones).

    4. Errors written for the reader. An API error carries a machine type, a code, the parameter at fault, a human message and often a link to the docs. Errors are documentation that arrives exactly when needed. Cost: every error path needs writing and maintenance.

    Halide

    Taught by Sebastiaan de With and Ben Sandofsky (Lux Optics)

    A pro camera app that gives iPhone photographers manual control without menus, by mapping each control to a gesture or dial within thumb reach, and by borrowing visual aids from real cameras (focus peaking, histograms) so the screen tells you what the sensor sees.

    MakerLux Optics (de With, Sandofsky)
    Year2017; Halide Mark II 2020
    CategoryCamera app, iPhone
    Study it forExpert controls, gestures, reachability, honest feedback

    Apple's Camera app is built for everyone and hides almost every control. Pro camera apps before Halide often went the other way, exposing every setting as a tiny button. Halide's founders (a designer and an engineer, both photographers) asked what a physical camera does well: controls are where your fingers already are, and each control has one job. Halide maps that onto glass. It also writes publicly and in depth about iPhone camera hardware on its blog, which educates its users and markets the app at once.

    1. Gestures on the image, not buttons over it. Swiping vertically anywhere on the viewfinder adjusts exposure. The whole screen is the control, so you keep your eye on the picture. Cost: gestures must be taught, and accidental swipes must be easy to undo (a tap returns to auto).
    2. Manual focus as a dial under the thumb. Focus is adjusted by sliding along a control near the bottom corner, the way a lens ring sits under your fingers. Cost: left-handed use and large phones need care.
    3. Focus peaking. While focusing manually, edges that are in focus are outlined in colour. It borrows a decades-old aid from video and mirrorless cameras to answer the question "is it sharp?" on a small screen. Cost: visual noise when on, so it appears only during manual focus.
    4. Live histogram and readouts. Small, legible, honest data about the exposure. Pros read them; beginners can ignore them. Cost: screen space.
    5. Reachability first. Frequently touched controls cluster at the bottom, within a one-handed thumb arc. Rarely touched indicators sit at the top. Cost: the top of the screen becomes "read only", which some users find odd at first.
    6. Opinionated output options. Halide offers RAW capture and, from 2024, "Process Zero", a mode that skips Apple's heavy computational processing for a more natural, film-like file. The product takes a position on what a photo should be. Cost: images look less "finished" than the default camera's, which some people dislike.
    7. Teaching as marketing. Long blog posts analyse each new iPhone's camera in detail. Users learn the craft, and trust the app made by people who know it. Cost: time spent writing instead of building, which is a fine trade for a small team.

    Trade-offs and criticisms

    Gesture-heavy interfaces are invisible until learned, and Halide relies on a short tutorial and on users who want to learn. Pro features shift with every iPhone generation, so the app must constantly chase hardware changes. Some photographers want more manual controls, others fewer, and the app's personality (dark, precise, camera-like) suits some tastes more than others. The pricing, a subscription with a one-time purchase option, tries to serve both the casual and the committed.

    Steal this for your software

    • Rank controls by how often they are touched, and place them by reach. Read-only information can live far away.
    • Use the whole content area as a gesture surface for the most frequent continuous control.
    • Borrow feedback aids from the physical craft your users already know.
    • Show modes and aids only while they matter (focus peaking only in manual focus).
    • Teach the craft in public; it builds trust and markets the product.

    Copy

    • Controls in the thumb arc.
    • Contextual visual aids.
    • A one-tap way back to automatic.

    Don't copy blindly

    • Gestures with no visible hint at all.
    • A dark "pro" look for an app used by beginners.

    Reeder

    Taught by Silvio Rizzi

    A feed reader made by one designer-developer that set the standard for calm reading on iOS and macOS, then, in 2024, rethought itself from an inbox of unread items into a single timeline you simply scroll, with no unread counts.

    MakerSilvio Rizzi (solo, Switzerland)
    YearOriginal Reeder 2010; new Reeder September 2024
    CategoryRSS and feed reader, Apple platforms
    Study it forReading typography, gestures, rethinking a mental model

    You met Silvio Rizzi in the starter guide. This breakdown looks at a rare thing: a successful product that changed its core model. Classic Reeder treated feeds like email: every article is unread until you read it or mark it read, and the counts pile up. Many people experience that as a chore. In 2024 Rizzi released a new app called simply Reeder, which pulls in RSS, YouTube, Reddit, Mastodon, Bluesky, podcasts and more into one timeline, remembers your position across devices, and drops unread counts entirely. The previous app continues as Reeder Classic.

    1. Unread counts removed. A number on every feed turns reading into a debt. The new Reeder replaces "what have I not read?" with "where was I?". Cost: completionists who liked clearing their inbox felt the product had been taken away; hence Reeder Classic remains available.
    2. One timeline for many sources. Articles, videos, social posts and podcasts arrive in the same chronological stream, each with a light source label. Cost: mixing media types makes typography and layout harder, and some users want separation.
    3. Position sync as the core promise. Pick up exactly where you left off on any device. It is what makes a timeline without counts work at all. Cost: sync must be reliable; a lost position breaks the model.
    4. Reading typography first. Both apps open articles in a clean reader view with careful line length, spacing and fonts. Cost: original site styling is lost, which is the point.
    5. Gestures carried over. Swipes for common actions (save, star, share) and pull gestures keep the feel that made Reeder famous. Cost: discoverability, as always with gestures.
    6. Keeping the old app alive. Instead of forcing the change, Rizzi kept Reeder Classic for people who want the inbox model. Cost: two products to maintain for a solo developer.
    7. A new business model with the new app. The new Reeder is sold as a subscription with a trial, where earlier versions were one-time purchases. Cost: some loyal users resisted the change; gain: income for continuous work on many integrations.

    Trade-offs and criticisms

    The rethink split the audience, as any model change does. Reviewers noted that the timeline is great for skimming but worse for "I must read every post from this one blog". Some missed the feature of marking things read. The lesson is not that the timeline is right. It is that a mental model (inbox versus stream) is the deepest design decision in a product, and changing it is a new product, which is why Rizzi shipped it as one rather than as Reeder 6.

    Steal this for your software

    • Audit every count and badge: is it information, or is it guilt?
    • When the mental model must change, ship a new product and keep the old one available for a while.
    • Sync position, not just data. "Where was I?" is often the real question.
    • Put reading typography before chrome in any app where people read.

    Copy

    • A "you were here" marker.
    • Source labels that are quiet but always present.
    • Clean reader views.

    Don't copy blindly

    • Removing counts in a product where completeness matters (support tickets, bills).
    • Mixing media types without a strong typographic system.

    Tweetie and Letterpress

    Taught by Loren Brichter

    Two apps by one developer that show two kinds of invention: Tweetie turned a list's natural physics (scroll past the top) into a command, pull to refresh, that every platform adopted; Letterpress turned a word game into a lesson in flat colour, clear state and turn-based calm.

    MakerLoren Brichter (atebits)
    YearsTweetie 2008, Tweetie 2 in 2009 (acquired by Twitter in 2010); Letterpress 2012
    CategoryTwitter client; word game
    Study it forGesture invention, performance as design, visual language

    Tweetie was a Twitter client for iPhone that was fast, dense and elegant at a time when most apps were none of these. Tweetie 2 introduced pull to refresh: drag the timeline down past the top, an arrow flips to say "release", let go, and new tweets load. Twitter bought atebits in 2010 and Tweetie became the official Twitter app. Brichter also wrote openly about how he made scrolling fast by drawing each table cell as a single flat image rather than many layered views, a reminder that "feel" is often an engineering decision. In 2012 he released Letterpress, a two-player word game whose flat, bold colour fields looked strikingly different from the textured iOS design of the time, a year before iOS 7 went flat.

    1. Pull to refresh uses a motion users already make. People scroll to the top to see what is new. Extending that motion past the edge needs no new button and no new concept. Cost: the action is invisible until learned (the text hint solves it).
    2. A visible threshold. The arrow flips and the label changes from "pull" to "release" at the commit point, so you can cancel by not pulling far enough. Feedback makes a gesture safe. Cost: none worth naming; it is a model of good gesture design.
    3. New content arrives where you are looking. Fresh items appear at the top of the list, under your thumb. Cost: the list position must be managed so you do not lose your place.
    4. Performance as design. Fast, smooth scrolling made the dense timeline readable in motion. On 2008 iPhone hardware, that required custom drawing. Cost: a lot of low-level engineering for something users only notice when it is missing.
    5. Letterpress: a flat colour language. Tiles take your colour or your opponent's; protected tiles become a darker shade. The whole game state is readable at a glance from colour alone. Cost: colour must be paired with shade differences for colour-blind players (Letterpress offered alternative colour themes).
    6. Letterpress: turns, not real time. Built on Game Center's turn-based play, games can last days. It is a calm social game, the opposite of an arcade. Cost: games can stall when opponents vanish.

    Notion and Obsidian

    Taught by Ivan Zhao and Notion, with Shida Li, Erica Xu and Steph Ango (Obsidian)

    Two answers to "where should my knowledge live?": Notion says everything is a block in a shared, cloud-hosted workspace you can shape into anything; Obsidian says everything is a plain Markdown file on your own disk, connected by links. Each model makes some things effortless and others impossible.

    MakersNotion Labs (Ivan Zhao, Simon Last); Obsidian (Shida Li, Erica Xu)
    YearsNotion 2016, with databases from 2018; Obsidian 2020
    CategoryNotes, docs and knowledge tools
    Study it forConceptual models, flexibility vs opinion, blank canvas, ownership

    In Notion, a paragraph, a heading, an image, a to-do and an entire database are all blocks. Type / and you can turn any line into any block. Pages contain blocks; databases contain pages; views show databases as tables, boards, calendars or galleries. With a few primitives you can build a wiki, a CRM or a habit tracker. In Obsidian, a note is a .md file in a folder (a "vault"). You connect notes with [[double bracket links]], and the app shows backlinks and a graph of connections. Obsidian's CEO, Steph Ango, summed up the philosophy in a 2023 essay titled "File over app": the files should outlive the app.

    1. Everything is a block. One primitive, endlessly combined. Cost: abstraction. New users must learn what a block is before they can build anything beyond a page of text.
    2. The slash menu. Type / to insert any block by name: a command palette inside the text. Cost: none worth naming; it became a pattern across the industry.
    3. Databases with views. One set of rows can be a table, a kanban board, a calendar or a gallery. Cost: people build systems more complex than their needs, and maintaining them becomes a hobby.
    4. Templates against the blank canvas. Because the canvas can be anything, a new user facing an empty page has no idea where to start. Notion answers with template galleries, including a large community of template makers. Cost: users adopt someone else's elaborate system and abandon it.
    5. Cloud-first and collaborative. Workspaces, sharing and permissions made Notion a team tool. Cost: dependence on the network; Notion has long been criticized for speed and offline behaviour.
    6. Calm, neutral visuals. Monochrome UI, emoji page icons and cover images let users bring the colour. Cost: workspaces can become visually noisy because users decorate.
    1. Plain files in a folder you own. Notes are Markdown files; quitting Obsidian loses nothing. Cost: features that need a database are harder, and sync is your problem or a paid add-on.
    2. Links and backlinks as the main structure. Typing [[ links notes; each note shows what links to it. Ideas connect without a hierarchy. Cost: messy vaults if you never tidy, and link rot when renaming (Obsidian updates links for you).
    3. The graph view. A visual map of notes and links. Cost: often more impressive than useful; it is a motivating picture rather than a daily tool.
    4. A plugin ecosystem. Thousands of community plugins add calendars, databases, task systems. Cost: quality varies, plugins break on updates, and setups grow fragile and personal.
    5. Business model: free for personal use, pay for services. The app is free; Sync and Publish are paid. Obsidian has no venture capital, by choice. Cost: a small team serving a huge user base.
    6. "File over app" as a public value. The essay explains why durability matters more than any feature. Cost: some conveniences (real-time collaboration) are ruled out.

    Flexibility versus opinion

    Notion and Obsidian are both flexible, and both pay the flexibility tax: the user becomes the designer of their own system. That is a joy for some and a burden for most. Compare Things (chapter 4) and Bear (chapter 5), which make the system decisions for you. Neither approach is right in general. Ask who your user is. A person who enjoys building systems will love blocks and plugins. A person who wants to get on with work will abandon them after the first weekend.

    Steal this for your software

    • Write down your product's primitives (blocks? files? items?) and make sure there are as few as possible.
    • Use a slash or command menu for inserting things into text.
    • Defeat the blank canvas with two or three small, specific templates, not a gallery of elaborate systems.
    • If users' data is personal and long-lived, prefer open formats ("file over app").
    • Decide where on the opinion-flexibility spectrum you sit, on purpose.
    For the solo creator

    Do not build a Notion. A flexible platform needs years of work on primitives, performance, permissions and templates before it beats a focused tool. A solo product wins by being opinionated about one job. If you love flexibility, make it an escape hatch (export, an API, a plain-text format) rather than the core experience.

    Copy

    • Few, composable primitives.
    • Slash insertion.
    • Backlinks for anything linkable.

    Don't copy blindly

    • Unlimited flexibility for users who want answers.
    • A plugin system before your core is stable.
    • A graph view as a selling point without a daily use.

    Figma

    Taught by Dylan Field and Evan Wallace

    A design tool that put the whole file in the browser and made it multiplayer, so that designing became something several people can do on one canvas at once, with cursors, comments and links instead of exported files and version names.

    MakerFigma (Dylan Field, Evan Wallace)
    YearFounded 2012; public launch 2016
    CategoryInterface design and collaboration
    Study it forCollaboration as model, web as platform, constraints, growth through sharing

    Before Figma, interface design happened in desktop apps (Sketch was the favourite), and sharing meant exporting, syncing files and naming versions like final_v7_REAL.sketch.     6. Community and plugins. Public files, templates and plugins (from 2019) turned users into contributors. Cost: moderation and quality control.
    7. Expansion beyond design. FigJam (2021) for whiteboarding, Dev Mode (2023) for developers, and later AI and site-building features. Cost: a growing, more complex product; an attempted acquisition by Adobe, announced in 2022, was abandoned in late 2023 after regulatory scrutiny.

    Arc

    Taught by The Browser Company (Josh Miller and team)

    A browser that reimagined tabs as a workspace (a vertical sidebar, separate Spaces, tabs that tidy themselves away) and won passionate fans, then showed the limit of ambitious redesign when its makers concluded it asked too much of ordinary people and moved on to a simpler, AI-centred browser.

    MakerThe Browser Company (New York); acquired by Atlassian in 2025
    YearsMac public release 2023; Windows 2024; new feature work largely stopped late 2024; Dia browser from 2025
    CategoryWeb browser
    Study it forRethinking a mature category, auto-tidying, the cost of novelty

    Browsers had barely changed in a decade: a row of tabs that shrink until unreadable. Arc moved tabs into a vertical sidebar, separated work and personal browsing into Spaces with their own colours, split tabs into pinned (kept) and today (temporary), and archived temporary tabs automatically after a period of inactivity (12 hours by default). It was beautifully made and deeply loved by a minority. In October 2024 the company told users it would stop major feature development on Arc to focus on a new product, and in 2025 it released Dia, a simpler browser built around AI. In a public letter, CEO Josh Miller reflected that Arc had been too different, too many new things to learn, for most people. In September 2025 Atlassian announced it would acquire the company.

    1. Vertical tabs in a sidebar. Titles stay readable however many tabs you open. Cost: horizontal space, and a break with twenty years of habit.
    2. Pinned versus today. Two kinds of tab for two intentions: things you return to, and things you are just looking at. Cost: a new concept to learn on day one.
    3. Auto-archive. Unpinned tabs you have not viewed for a while (12 hours by default, adjustable) slide into an archive. Tab hoarding is solved by forgetting on your behalf. Cost: the occasional panic of "where did that tab go?", softened by a searchable archive.
    4. Spaces with their own colour and profile. Work and home are separated visually and by login. Cost: more structure to set up.
    5. The browser as a creative object. Boosts (restyle any site), Easels (collage boards), split view and Little Arc windows. Cost: feature sprawl; each delight is another thing to learn.
    6. Strong visual identity. Coloured gradients, playful motion and a carefully crafted onboarding. Cost: high expectations and a look that ages quickly.
    7. The decision to stop. The team concluded that Arc's learning curve limited it to enthusiasts, and redirected effort to a simpler product. Cost: disappointed loyal users. Gain: a lesson, stated in public, that most products never admit.

    Trade-offs and criticisms

    Arc is the clearest case in this guide of the novelty tax: every new concept (Spaces, pinned versus today, archive, Boosts) was good on its own, and together they were too much for people who just wanted to open a website. Fans loved it precisely because of that richness. The business never found a revenue model before the pivot. And the turn to an AI browser raised questions about whether the company's attention had followed the product or the hype. Whatever you think of the pivot, the honest public reflection is rare and worth reading.

    Steal this for your software

    • Count the new concepts a first-time user must learn. Keep it to one or two.
    • Let the product tidy itself (archive, expire, collapse) with a clear, searchable way back.
    • Split one ambiguous thing into two clear intentions when it helps (keep versus glance).
    • Offer a few presets instead of a free slider for settings with sensible ranges.
    • When something is not working, say so in public and explain what you learned.

    • Auto-tidying with an archive.
    • Clear separation of contexts.
    • Honest retrospectives.

    Don't copy blindly

    • Many novel concepts at once.
    • Delight features before a business model.
    • Changing deep habits (tabs, keyboard shortcuts) without a familiar fallback.

    Google Search and Craigslist

    Taught by Google's early search team and Craig Newmark

    Two of the web's oldest surviving pages: Google's homepage stayed almost empty for decades because the job is one question, and Craigslist stayed dense and plain because the job is scanning many listings fast. Both are lessons in appropriateness, the match between form and job, rather than in style.

    MakersGoogle (Larry Page, Sergey Brin); Craig Newmark
    YearsGoogle 1998; Craigslist began as an email list in 1995, website 1996
    CategorySearch; classifieds
    Study it forFit over fashion, density, longevity, speed

    In the late 1990s, rival search sites became "portals" stuffed with news, weather, stock prices and ads. Google's homepage offered a logo, a box and two buttons. The emptiness made the job obvious and the page fast on dial-up modems. Craigslist, meanwhile, looks like a page from 1999 because, in spirit, it is one: a city, a grid of category links, then lists of text listings. Designers love to mock it, and it has served huge numbers of people for decades. Both pages teach the same lesson from opposite ends: the right amount of design depends on the job.

    1. Google: one field, centred. The page has one job and shows one control. Cost: Google had to resist years of pressure to use the most visited page on the web for promotion.
    2. Google: the results page carries the density. The homepage is empty because the results page is full: titles, links and snippets you scan in seconds. Simplicity at the entry, density where the work happens. Cost: as results pages filled with ads, cards and, from 2024, AI-generated answers, the clarity of the ten blue links eroded.
    3. Google: play, carefully placed. Doodles change the logo for holidays and anniversaries without touching the box. Personality lives in one controlled spot. Cost: almost none.
    4. Google: speed as a value. Results arrive fast and the page is light. Every extra kilobyte was treated as a cost. Cost: constant engineering discipline.
    5. Craigslist: everything on one screen. The home page lists every category as a link, so you navigate by scanning, not clicking through menus. Cost: overwhelming at first glance; rewarding after one visit.
    6. Craigslist: text over images. Listings are rows of text with price and place, so you scan dozens per screen. Images appear inside listings. Cost: less appealing for visual goods; competitors with photo grids took some categories.
    7. Craigslist: change almost nothing. Regular users know exactly where everything is. Stability is a feature for a tool used for years. Cost: missed improvements (mobile ergonomics, safety features) that rivals exploited.
    8. Craigslist: business model shapes design. Most listings are free; fees apply to a few categories in some cities. Without an ad business, there is no reason to add engagement features. Cost: a smaller company that innovates slowly.

    Trade-offs and criticisms

    Google's homepage is still minimal, but the results page has become one of the most contested designs on the web: ads that look like results, then panels, then AI summaries that answer without sending traffic to the sites they draw on. Minimal entry and maximal results are now in tension. Craigslist's refusal to change left openings for competitors: Facebook Marketplace, OLX and Quikr in India, and many category-specific apps took share with photos, profiles and safety features. Appropriateness is not permanent. The job changes, the devices change, and so must the form.

    Steal this for your software

    • Match density to the job: sparse for one decision, dense for scanning many.
    • Keep the entry point simple and put density where the work happens.
    • Give personality one controlled place (a logo, an empty state) rather than spreading it everywhere.
    • Value stability for tools people use for years. Change only what clearly helps.
    • Re-check fit when devices or user habits change.

    Copy

    • One primary action on entry screens.
    • Text-dense lists for scanning.
    • Speed budgets for pages.

    Don't copy blindly

    • "Ugly works" as an excuse for not caring.
    • Minimalism that hides needed information on working screens.

    Duolingo

    Taught by Luis von Ahn and Duolingo's growth and design teams

    A free language-learning app that may be the most effective habit machine in consumer software: streaks, a mascot with a personality, short lessons, leagues and relentless testing. Study it for how well those mechanics work and for the honest question of whether they serve learning or engagement.

    MakerDuolingo (Luis von Ahn, Severin Hacker)
    YearPublic launch 2012
    CategoryLanguage learning, freemium
    Study it forHabit design, gamification, A/B testing, ethics of engagement

    Learning a language takes hundreds of hours. Most people quit within weeks. Duolingo's design bet is that the hard part is not teaching, it is coming back. So the product makes coming back easy (lessons of a few minutes), rewarding (XP, sound, animation) and costly to skip (a streak you do not want to lose, an owl who reminds you). The company tests constantly and has written publicly about how the streak became one of its most important features. It also uses spaced-repetition research to schedule review of words you are likely to forget.

    1. The streak. A count of consecutive days with at least one lesson. It turns a vague goal (learn Spanish) into a concrete daily one (keep the number going), and loss aversion does the rest. Cost: people do the minimum to protect the number, and a broken long streak can make someone quit entirely.
    2. Streak protection. Streak freezes (and similar features) let a day be missed without loss. They reduce the cliff of a broken streak. Cost: they also make the streak something you can buy, which blurs the line between motivation and monetization.
    3. Lessons of a few minutes. The unit of commitment is tiny, so starting is easy. Cost: depth; real conversation needs longer, harder practice.
    4. One path, one next step. The home screen is a single path; the next lesson is obvious. No choice paralysis. Cost: less learner control.
    5. A mascot with a voice. Duo the owl delivers reminders, jokes and guilt. Personality makes nagging tolerable, even shareable; the "menacing owl" became an internet meme that the company embraced in marketing. Cost: the joke depends on a relationship built on mild pressure.
    6. Leagues and social comparison. Weekly leaderboards with promotion and demotion. Cost: extrinsic motivation can crowd out intrinsic interest; some users grind easy lessons for points.
    7. Hearts and freemium limits. Mistakes cost hearts in the free tier; the subscription removes limits and ads. Cost: the learning mechanic (mistakes) is tied to a monetization mechanic (limits), which can punish exactly the behaviour learning needs.
    8. Testing everything. Many changes ship as experiments measured on retention. Cost: what is measured (daily activity) can drift from what matters (actual language ability).

    The ethical debate

    Duolingo's mechanics clearly increase daily practice, and daily practice is good for learning. Critics argue that the mechanics optimize for the streak and the app, not for fluency: people complete easy lessons to keep a number alive, and guilt-based reminders train anxiety. Defenders reply that without the mechanics, almost nobody would practise at all. Both are partly right. The useful question for you is not "is gamification bad?" but "does each mechanic reward the behaviour that actually achieves the user's goal?" A streak for a daily habit the user chose is a tool. A streak that keeps someone tapping after the habit stopped serving them is a trap. Read Honest & Calm alongside this chapter.

    • Make the smallest unit of progress tiny, so starting is easy.
    • Show one obvious next step on the home screen.
    • If you use streaks, add a safety net, and celebrate real progress more than the count.
    • Give reminders a consistent, kind voice, and stop sending them when they are ignored.
    • Pair every engagement metric with a value metric (words learned, tasks finished) and watch both.

    WhatsApp and UPI payments

    Taught by WhatsApp (Jan Koum, Brian Acton) and India's UPI apps (Google Pay, PhonePe and others, on NPCI's rails)

    Two flows that hundreds of millions of Indians use daily, many on inexpensive phones and patchy networks: a messenger whose ticks make an invisible network visible, and a scan-and-pay flow with one decision per screen. Both are lessons in designing for everyone, not for designers.

    MakersWhatsApp (Meta since 2014); UPI by NPCI, apps by Google, PhonePe, Paytm and banks
    YearsWhatsApp 2009; UPI 2016; Google Pay India launched as Tez in 2017
    CategoryMessaging; real-time payments
    Study it forFeedback, robustness, inclusion, flows for billions

    WhatsApp became the default way India communicates, not because it was beautiful, but because it worked: small, fast on cheap Android phones, tolerant of bad networks, identity by phone number (no username to invent), and voice notes for people more comfortable speaking than typing. UPI did the same for money. You open any UPI app, scan a QR code at a tea stall, type ₹20, enter your UPI PIN and see a big tick. The shopkeeper's speaker box announces the amount out loud. In a few years, this became how India pays, with billions of transactions a month.

    1. Ticks make the network visible. One grey tick: sent to WhatsApp's server. Two grey ticks: delivered to the phone. Two blue ticks (added in 2014): read. In places where networks drop and phones die, knowing whether a message arrived is half the value. Cost: read receipts create social pressure, so they can be turned off (which also hides others' receipts from you).
    2. Phone number as identity. No username, no password at sign-up; your contacts are already there. Cost: privacy, since your number is exposed to every group you join.
    3. Voice notes as first-class. Hold to record, slide to cancel, lock to record hands-free. Voice works across literacy levels, scripts and languages. Cost: long voice notes are hard to skim (WhatsApp later added faster playback and transcripts).
    4. Built for cheap phones and bad networks. Small app, efficient data use, messages queue and send when the network returns. Cost: engineering constraints on features.
    5. Friction against misinformation. After viral rumours spread through forwarded messages, especially in India, WhatsApp labelled forwarded messages (2018) and limited how many chats a message can be forwarded to at once. Deliberate friction as a safety feature. Cost: inconvenience for legitimate sharing.
    6. UPI: one decision per screen. Scan, confirm amount, enter PIN, see result. Each screen asks one question with one large button. Cost: little room for extras, which is correct.
    7. UPI: a standard PIN step. PIN entry is a standardized, bank-backed step that looks much the same across UPI apps, which builds familiarity and trust whichever app you use. Cost: apps cannot customize it; that is the point.
    8. UPI: success that is unmissable, for both sides. A full-screen tick and sound for the payer, and a voice announcement on the merchant's speaker box, so a shopkeeper does not need to look at a phone. Cost: hardware and audio in a noisy shop; and fake "success" screens became a known scam, which is why the merchant's independent confirmation matters.

    Read receipts gave people a new source of anxiety ("they read it and didn't reply"). Phone numbers as identity expose users in large groups.     • Confirm important outcomes in more than one channel (visual and audio, payer and payee).
    • Use deliberate friction where speed causes harm (mass forwarding, irreversible actions).
    • Support voice and low-literacy use where your audience needs it.
    For the solo creator

    Buy or borrow an inexpensive Android phone and keep it on your desk. Run every release on it, on a throttled network (Chrome DevTools can simulate slow 3G). If you serve Indian users, this one habit will catch more real problems than any design review. Also try your product in Hindi or another language your users speak; see Accessible & Inclusive.

    Copy

    • Visible delivery states.
    • Huge, unambiguous success screens.
    • Offline queues that send later.

    Don't copy blindly

    • Read receipts on by default without a way to turn them off.
    • Promotions inside a payment flow.
    • Phone numbers as public identity where users need privacy.

      Similar ticks and simplicity with a stronger privacy stance and usernames. The same interaction model with different values.

    Common mistakes

    Designing on a flagship phone with fast Wi-Fi

    It is the device on your desk.

    Test on a low-end phone with a throttled network before every release.

    Silent sending

    Background sync feels clean.

    Show queued and failed states clearly, with a retry, for anything users send.

    Check yourself

    Your field-survey app is used by health workers in rural areas. Forms are submitted from places with no signal. What WhatsApp-style decision matters most?

    An offline queue with visible delivery states is exactly what the clock and ticks do. Errors and disabled buttons lose work or force users to remember to retry, which fails in the field.

    Wrap-up

    Ten things to remember

    1. You cannot steal a feeling. Name the decision, its reason and its cost, then decide whether to borrow it.
    2. Read software top to bottom (job, model, structure, interaction, visuals, feel, words), and remember the business model underneath and the omissions around it.
    3. Great products are coherent: one bet, every layer serving it, refusals stated openly.
    4. Protect the user's scarcest resource, usually attention or time, and make your defaults defend it (iA Writer, Superhuman).
    5. Model the user's real life, not your database (Things, Reeder, Day One).
    6. Speed is architecture plus interaction plus words, not a dark theme (Linear, Stripe).
    7. Gestures need thresholds, feedback and button fallbacks (Tweetie, Halide).
    8. Density should match the job: sparse for one decision, dense for scanning many (Google, Craigslist).
    9. Engagement mechanics are powerful; reward real progress, not the metric (Duolingo, Day One).
    10. For a solo creator, depth in one experience, public writing about decisions and honest business models beat feature parity (Overcast, Reeder, Halide).
