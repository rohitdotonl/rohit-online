Identity & Launch. Giving a small product a face, a voice and a first day.

You can build a good product and still have nobody find it, understand it, or trust it enough to pay. This guide covers the parts of product design that face outward: the name, the icon, the identity kit, the landing page, the pricing page, the app store page, and the launch itself. Every one of them is a design problem, and every one of them is small enough for one person to do well.

Warm-up: what do you already believe?

Most builders carry two quiet beliefs about marketing. The first is that it is a separate, slightly grubby activity that starts after the "real" design work. The second is that it is mostly about being loud. This guide argues against both. The outward-facing parts of a product are designed artefacts with users, jobs and failure modes, just like a settings screen. And for small, calm products, the winning move is almost never loudness. It is clarity, specificity and consistency.

Answer the two questions below before you read on. Do not look anything up. The point is to notice what you believe now, so you can see what changes.

Diagnostic 1

You have ₹0 for marketing and two weekends before launch. Which single piece of work will most improve how many visitors become users?

Visitors decide in seconds whether a page is for them. A specific headline plus a real picture of the product answers "what is it and is it for me?" faster than anything else. A logo matters far less than builders think at launch (see chapter 5), animation rarely clarifies, and more features usually add noise.
Diagnostic 2

Which of these is the best description of a product's "brand"?

Logos, colors and taglines are tools for expressing a brand. The brand itself lives in people's heads: the expectation they form from using the product, reading its emails, getting help, paying for it. You can influence it, not declare it. Chapter 1 builds on exactly this.

How to study this guide

  1. Pick your product. Everything works best applied to a real thing you are building or have shipped. If you have nothing yet, use the worked example that runs through the guide: Margin, a fictional calm app for saving book highlights and writing notes about them, made by one person.
  2. Read a part, then do its exercises. Part one (identity) takes about a weekend of exercises. Part two (the marketing site and pricing) takes another. Part three (launch) runs alongside your release.
  3. Keep a "launch folder". A single folder with your brand sheet, headline drafts, screenshots, press kit and changelog. By the end of the guide it will hold everything you need on launch day.
  4. Use the demos. Four labs let you test a headline, run a five-second test, see an icon shrink to 16 px, and flip a pricing page between honest and manipulative. Interaction teaches these better than prose.
Part one

Identity: who your product is

Before anyone sees a landing page they meet a name, an icon and a feeling. These chapters cover the brand as a promise, where it lives inside the product, how to name a product, how to make a wordmark and an app icon without being an illustrator, and how to write it all down on one page so you stay consistent for years.

1

A brand is a promise you keep

Taught by Marty Neumeier, with April Dunford and Kathy Sierra

For a small product, a brand is three things working together: a promise (what you will reliably do for someone), a personality (how it feels to deal with you), and consistency (keeping both, everywhere, for years).

Marty Neumeier's short book The Brand Gap (2003) popularized a useful definition: a brand is not what you say it is, it is the gut feeling people have about your product. That definition is liberating for a solo creator, because it means a brand is not something you buy from an agency. It is something that accumulates every time someone touches your product. Each experience either confirms the expectation they had or breaks it.

That gives you the mechanism. People form a prediction ("this app is fast and quiet and won't nag me"), then they test it at every touchpoint. Confirmed predictions build trust, and trust is what lets someone recommend you, pay you, and forgive your bugs. A logo is just a shortcut that lets people recall the prediction quickly. Without the experiences behind it, the logo recalls nothing.

Brand starts with positioning, not with pictures

Positioning, which you met in Problems Worth Solving, decides what your product is, for whom, compared to what, and why it is better for them. April Dunford's Obviously Awesome (2019) breaks it into competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, target customers and market category. Brand sits on top of positioning: it is how that position feels and sounds. If your positioning is fuzzy, no amount of visual polish will fix your brand, because there is no clear promise for the visuals to express.

So the order is: positioning, then promise, then personality, then expression (name, icon, colors, type, words). Most builders do it backwards. They pick a font and a gradient first, then try to reverse-engineer what the product stands for.

Brand attributes: "this, not that"

Personality is easiest to define as a short list of attributes, each paired with its opposite that you reject. "Friendly" alone means nothing, because every product claims it. "Friendly, not chummy" or "precise, not cold" draws a line you can actually design to. Mailchimp's public voice and tone guide is a well-known example of this "this, not that" style. Three to five pairs are enough. More than that and you will not remember them while you work.

Kathy Sierra's Badass: Making Users Awesome (2015) adds the most important correction: the brand people love is rarely about the product. It is about who they become by using it. People do not want to be better Margin users, they want to be better readers. Write your promise about their result, not your features.

Key idea

For the solo creator

Your advantage is that one person writes every word and designs every screen, so consistency is cheap for you and expensive for big companies. Your risk is that you write the error messages at 2 a.m. in a different mood from the homepage. Write your attributes on a sticky note next to your editor and read them before you write any user-facing string.

Do

  • Write the promise as a result for a specific person ("Your highlights, kept and findable").
  • Define 3–5 attributes as "this, not that" pairs.
  • Derive visual and verbal choices from the attributes, and be able to say which attribute each choice serves.
  • Audit the "boring" touchpoints (receipts, errors, password resets) first.

Don't

  • Pick colors and fonts before you can say what you promise.
  • Use attributes everyone claims: "simple", "powerful", "innovative".
  • Let each surface (app, site, email) drift into its own personality.
  • Confuse a rebrand with fixing a product people do not trust.

Common mistakes

Treating the brand as the logo

The logo is the visible, discussable part, and making it feels like progress.

Write the promise and attributes first. Delay the logo until the product exists; use a plain wordmark meanwhile.

Generic attributes

"Simple, modern, powerful" feels safe because nobody can disagree with it.

For each attribute, write the opposite you reject and a screen where you would choose it over a competing value.

Promising what the product does not yet do

Aspirational copy is tempting before launch.

Promise only what the current build keeps. Every broken promise costs more trust than the claim earned.

Check yourself

Margin's attributes are "calm, not sleepy" and "precise, not pedantic". A sync fails. Which error message fits?

D is calm (no alarm, no exclamation), precise (says what happened and what will happen) and not pedantic (no error codes). A is chummy, B is cold and technical, C is corporate and says nothing.

Where a product brand actually lives

Taught by Don Norman, with Aarron Walter and the Mailchimp content team

For software, most brand impressions are made inside the product and around its edges: the details of the interface, the words, the motion and sound, and the emails and support replies people get when something goes wrong.

Big consumer brands spend on advertising because most of their customers rarely touch the product. Software is the opposite. Your users touch the product dozens of times a week. The interface is the brand's main channel, and the marketing site is a small entrance hall. That changes where you should invest.

Don Norman's three levels of design (from Emotional Design, 2004) are a helpful map. The visceral level is the first impression of the icon and the screen. The behavioral level is how it feels to use: speed, feedback, whether it does what you expected. The reflective level is the story people tell themselves about using it ("I'm the kind of person who uses a careful notes app"). A brand that only works at the visceral level is a coat of paint.

Six places to put your brand

1. Interface details
Corner radius, spacing, the accent color, how a selected row looks, the shape of the cursor in your editor. Users will not name these, but they recognize them. See Visual Design.
2. Copy
Button labels, empty states, confirmations, settings descriptions. This is where personality is loudest. Write it with your attribute pairs in front of you. See Words.
3. Motion
A calm product moves calmly: short, eased transitions, no bounces unless playfulness is an attribute. Durations of roughly 150–250 ms feel quick without feeling abrupt. See Motion & Feel.
4. Sound and haptics
Optional, and powerful when used once. Slack's short notification sound became so recognizable that people react to it outside the app. If you use sound, use one, keep it under a second, and let people turn it off.
5. Emails
Welcome, receipts, password resets, trial ending, export ready. Many are sent by default templates from your payment or auth provider. Customize every one you can.
6. Support and docs
A reply from the person who built the product is a brand moment no large company can match. Chapter 17 covers this.

For the solo creator

Make a list of every automated message your stack sends on your behalf: auth provider (magic link, password reset), payment provider (receipt, failed payment, renewal), app store (none you control, which is why your in-app copy matters more there), hosting (status page). Most can be customized in an afternoon. This is the highest-leverage brand work you can do, and almost nobody does it.

Do

  • Send from a named person and allow replies.
  • Use the same accent, type and sentence style in emails as in the app.
  • Design the 404, offline, and "something went wrong" screens.
  • Keep sound to one short, optional cue, if any.

Don't

  • Leave provider-default emails with "Dear Customer" and "noreply".
  • Add personality only to happy paths. Errors are where trust is decided.
  • Use jokes in moments of loss (failed payment, lost data).
  • Add a mascot as a substitute for a voice.

In the wild

  • Mailchimpthe "high five"
    After you send a campaign, a cartoon hand gives you a high five. It marks the moment of greatest anxiety (pressing send to thousands of people) with relief. Put the personality where the emotion already is.
  • Asanacelebrations
    Completing tasks can occasionally trigger a creature flying across the screen, and users can turn it off. Delight should be rare, earned and optional.
  • Slacknotification sound
    A short, distinctive sound that became part of how people recognize the product. One sound, used consistently, becomes brand; many sounds become noise.
  • Basecamp / HEY37signals
    Support replies, help pages and product announcements read like they were written by the same opinionated people who built the product. Voice consistency across support is a competitive advantage for small teams.

Common mistakes

Personality in the hero, nothing in the product

The landing page is designed in a burst of energy; the app's edges are designed under deadline.

Spend your brand budget in the order users meet touchpoints most often: in-app copy, errors, emails, then the site.

Whimsy in the wrong moment

A playful voice applied uniformly, including to "your card was declined".

Keep the voice, drop the jokes when stakes are high. Mailchimp calls this adjusting tone to the reader's state.

"noreply@" everywhere

It is the default and feels professional.

Use a real address you read. For a small product, replies are free research.

Check yourself

You have one free evening for "brand work" on a shipped app with 300 users. Which task does the most for the brand?

Your 300 users meet emails and error messages far more often than the logo or About page. Fixing the touchpoints people actually experience confirms the promise where it is tested.

Naming: the four kinds of names and how to judge them

Taught by Al Ries and Jack Trout, with Paul Graham and the naming practice of Indian startups

A good product name is easy to say, easy to spell after hearing it once, available where you need it, legally usable, and harmless in the languages your users speak. Meaning matters less than you think; memorability and availability matter more.

Names feel enormously important because they are permanent and public. In practice, a name is a container. People pour the experience of your product into it. "Apple" and "Amazon" meant nothing about computers or shopping. What made them work was that they were short, concrete, easy to say and easy to remember, and the companies then filled them with meaning. So the job of a name is mostly to not get in the way: not be misheard, misspelled, confused with a competitor, or unavailable.

Four kinds of names

Indian products show every type. Zerodha is a compound of "zero" and the Sanskrit "rodha" (barrier). Razorpay and PhonePe are compounds with a clear category word. Zomato and Swiggy are invented. Jio is a real Hindi word ("live!") used as a metaphor. Paytm started as a compression of "pay through mobile". Notice that the invented names needed large budgets to fill with meaning. A solo creator usually cannot afford that, which is one reason compound and metaphor names are a good default for you.

Domains, stores and trademarks, briefly

You do not need the .com. Many respected products use other domains: Linear lives at linear.app, and plenty of tools use .so, .io or a country domain. A common pattern is a prefix: get[name].com, use[name].com, [name]app.com. What matters is that the domain is short, unambiguous when spoken, and that you own the name in the places your users search. The .app domain requires HTTPS, which is a sensible default anyway.

Trademarks are a legal topic and this is not legal advice, but the basics are simple. Trademarks are registered per class of goods and services under the Nice Classification; software products typically fall in class 9 (downloadable software) and class 42 (software as a service). Search the free databases before you fall in love: the USPTO's trademark search for the US, the IP India public search for India, and WIPO's Global Brand Database across many countries. An identical or very similar mark in the same class, for a similar product, is a red flag. If your product earns real money, pay a trademark lawyer for a proper clearance once.

Myth

"The Chevy Nova failed in Latin America because no va means 'doesn't go'." This story is repeated in marketing books, but it is generally considered a myth: Spanish speakers hear "nova" as one word, and the car reportedly sold fine. The real lesson is narrower: check for genuine slang and offensive meanings with native speakers, but don't reject a name because of a far-fetched split of syllables.

Watch out

Names that are near-misspellings of real words ("Kwik", "Fotos", "Notz") fail the say-it-once test badly. Every person who hears the name has to be told how to spell it, forever. Dropped vowels had a fashion moment around 2005–2010 (Flickr, Tumblr) when the real words were taken; today they mostly read as dated.

Do

  • Prefer one or two syllables and under 10 letters.
  • Choose a real word or clear compound you can say on a phone call.
  • Check meaning with native speakers in every language your users speak.
  • Search trademark databases in classes 9 and 42 before you commit.

Don't

  • Pick a name that only works when seen written down.
  • Use a competitor's name plus a twist ("Notionly").
  • Spend weeks on the .com; a clean alternative domain is fine.
  • Put the version or platform in the name ("Margin Pro for Mac 2").

In the wild

  • Linearlinear.app, 2019
    A real word with a strong association (straight lines, steady progress) on a non-.com domain. Nobody doubts it is a serious product. A metaphor name on a clean alternative domain beats a contorted .com.
  • ThingsCultured Code, 2009
    A plain, almost generic word for a to-do app. It works because the product filled it with a strong identity over many years. Plain words become distinctive through consistency.
  • InstapaperMarco Arment, 2008
    "Instant" plus "paper": you understand it once you know it saves articles to read later. Compounds explain themselves with one sentence of context.
  • ZerodhaIndia, 2010
    A compound with an English half and a Sanskrit half, meaning roughly "zero barriers". It sounds distinctive to everyone and means something to people who know the word. Indian languages are a rich source of distinctive, meaningful names.

Common mistakes

Naming by committee of one, at midnight

A name feels perfect when you have been staring at a list for hours.

Sleep on your shortlist, then run the seven tests with other people. A name you have to defend is a name that fails.

Descriptive and generic

"Book Notes" is clear, so it feels safe.

You cannot own or be found by a generic phrase. Add a distinctive half ("Margin Notes") or move toward metaphor.

Skipping the trademark search

It feels like a big-company concern.

A renamed product loses its reviews, links and recognition. Twenty minutes of searching now is cheap insurance.

Check yourself

Your shortlist is "Kwotz", "Margin" and "ReadingHighlightsManager". Margin has an app with the same name in the finance category, a different trademark class. What do you do?

Kwotz fails say-it-once. The descriptive name is too long and unownable. A same-name product in a different category and class is often not a conflict, but it deserves a careful check (and a lawyer if money is at stake). Starting over throws away a strong candidate.

A naming process you can run in a weekend

Taught by naming practitioners, with Sahil Lavingia and Paul Graham

Good names come from volume and filtering: generate a hundred or more candidates from several directions, cut fast with hard tests, then live with three finalists for a few days before choosing.

The mistake most people make is looking for "the" name in their head. Names come out of a process with a wide mouth and a narrow exit. The wide mouth is word lists from many sources: your product's job, its metaphor, the feeling it should evoke, words from other languages, and plain objects. The narrow exit is a set of tests that are cheap to run and unforgiving.

Six directions to generate from

Verbs and nouns from what the product does. For Margin: keep, mark, underline, note, quote, gather, collect, file, recall, annotate, excerpt, commonplace. Most will be too generic alone, but they feed combinations.

What physical thing or practice is this product like? A reading-notes app is like a margin, a commonplace book, a bookmark, a card index, a scrapbook, a slip box (Zettelkasten), a pencil. Metaphors give you names with built-in stories.

Words for the attribute pairs from Exercise 1.1. Calm: still, hush, low, lamp, dusk. Precise: fine, nib, rule, grain. These rarely work alone but often combine well ("Nib", "Lamplight").

Words from languages you know, chosen for sound as well as meaning. Hindi and Sanskrit offer words like smriti (memory) or pustak (book); Japanese offers shiori (bookmark). Check that the word is easy for your other users to say, and that you are using it respectfully and correctly.

Plain, concrete objects with a nice sound, even if unrelated: Bear, Things, Stripe, Notion, Slack. Concrete nouns are easy to picture, which helps both memory and the icon. A word you can draw is a gift to your future icon.

Combine across lists: job + object (Dropbox), feeling + job, metaphor + suffix (-ly, -ify, -io are tired; avoid). Try prefixes and compounds in both orders: "Notemark", "Markleaf", "Quoteshelf".

    Wordmarks and simple marks for non-illustrators

    Taught by Paul Rand, with Sagi Haviv (Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv) and Massimo Vignelli

    Start with a wordmark: your name, set carefully in one good typeface. Add a simple mark only when you need a symbol for small, square places, and test both at 16 pixels and in a single color.

    Paul Rand argued in his 1991 essay "Logos, Flags, and Escutcheons" that a logo gets its meaning from the quality of the thing it represents, not the other way round. Sagi Haviv, whose firm designed marks for organizations like Chase and the Smithsonian, makes the same point in practice: a logo should be simple, appropriate and distinctive; it does not need to explain what the company does. That is great news for a non-illustrator. Your logo's job is recognition, not description, and recognition comes from simplicity and repetition.

    A wordmark is the name itself, set in type with care. It is the right default for a new product because it does two jobs at once: it is the logo and it teaches the name. Many respected software brands are wordmarks or lean heavily on one (Stripe, Linear's name in its site header, iA's simple lettering). A mark (symbol) is something you add for places where the full name will not fit: the app icon, the favicon, the avatar on social sites.

    Making a wordmark without being a type designer

    1. Pick a typeface that matches your attributes. A calm, bookish product suits a well-made serif or a humanist sans; a technical tool suits a crisp grotesque or a monospace. Use a typeface whose license allows logo use (most open-source fonts on Google Fonts do, under the SIL Open Font License). See Typography for choosing faces.
    2. Set it at a large size and choose a weight. Medium or semibold usually reproduces best at small sizes. Very thin weights disappear.
    3. Adjust the spacing by eye. Fonts are spaced for running text. At logo size, letter pairs often look uneven. Tighten slightly overall, then fix individual pairs (like "r" next to "g") until the gaps look even. This is called kerning.
    4. Decide on case. Lowercase feels friendlier and more informal; title case feels like a proper name; all caps feels formal and needs extra letter spacing.
    5. Convert to outlines (paths) so it renders the same everywhere, and export as SVG.
    6. Optional single tweak. One small custom detail (a shortened descender, a joined pair) can make it ownable. One. Not three.

    The two tests every mark must pass

    The 16 px test. Shrink the mark to 16 by 16 pixels, the size of a browser favicon, and look at it at arm's length. Fine lines merge, small gaps close, and detail turns to mud. What survives is the silhouette and one or two big shapes. If you cannot recognize it, simplify until you can. The monochrome test. Render the mark in pure black on white and pure white on black. A mark that depends on a gradient or on two close colors to be readable will fail on invoices, embossed stickers, notification icons, and the iOS tinted icon mode. Good marks work as a single flat shape first and get color second.

    Key idea

    A logo identifies; it does not explain. Simple, bold and repeated beats clever and detailed.

    For the solo creator

    Build the mark in code. A good small mark is usually a few rectangles, circles and one path: you can write it as an SVG by hand in fifteen minutes, keep it in your repo, and export every size from it. If you later hire an identity designer, you will hand them a clear brief (promise, attributes, current wordmark) instead of a blank page, and you will get a better result.

    Do

    • Start with a wordmark in your product's main typeface.
    • Draw a mark from one idea you could sketch in five seconds.
    • Test at 16 px and in one color before showing anyone.
    • Keep a master SVG and generate sizes from it.

    Don't

    • Put a tagline inside the logo.
    • Use a font whose license forbids logo use.
    • Rely on gradients, shadows or fine lines to be recognizable.
    • Buy a generic "letter in a circle" mark from a template site that others also use.

    • Notionmark

    You want it to say "books" and "notes" and "calm" and "sync".

    One idea. The rest is the product's job.

    Endless logo tinkering before launch

    App icons and favicons

    Taught by Apple's Human Interface Guidelines, with Material Design and icon designers like Michael Flarup

    An app icon has one job: to be found and recognized in a grid of other icons, at sizes from 1024 px down to 16 px. One idea, a strong silhouette, no text, and a color that stands apart from its neighbors.

    Your icon is the most-viewed piece of brand you will ever make. People see it every time they unlock their phone, look at their dock or switch tabs. But they rarely look at it. They scan for it, among 20 to 30 others, in a fraction of a second. That is the mechanism to design for: visual search. Visual search runs on preattentive features (color, shape, size, orientation; see The Human Mind). An icon that differs from its neighbors on one of those features pops out. An icon that blends in on all of them must be read, and nobody reads icons.

    Platform shapes and sizes

    You supply a square 1024×1024 px image with no transparency and no rounded corners; the system applies the rounded "squircle" mask (a continuous-curvature corner, not a simple circle arc). The icon is shown at many sizes: the home screen uses 60 pt (180 px on 3× phones), Spotlight 40 pt, Settings 29 pt. Since iOS 18, people can choose dark and tinted icon appearances, so Apple asks for dark and tinted variants; with iOS 26, Apple introduced layered "Liquid Glass" icons built in its Icon Composer tool. The safe practice holds across all of these: a simple foreground shape on a simple background, so the system can re-render it.

    Since macOS Big Sur (2020), Mac icons share the same rounded-square outline as iOS, with a gentle drop shadow and room for a little more depth and detail, because they are seen larger in the dock and Finder. Apple's template places the icon body at about 824 px inside a 1024 px canvas, leaving room for the shadow. Recent macOS versions push harder toward the shared shape, so an icon that ignores it now looks out of place. Provide sizes from 16 px to 1024 px; the 16 and 32 px versions appear in Finder lists and should be simplified by hand if needed.

    Android adaptive icons (introduced in Android 8.0) are two layers, a foreground and a background, each 108×108 dp. Device makers mask them into circles, squircles or rounded squares, and the system can move the layers slightly for motion effects. Only the inner 72×72 dp is guaranteed to show, and your key shape should sit inside a 66 dp diameter safe circle. Since Android 13, people can turn on themed icons, which use a monochrome version of your foreground layer tinted to their wallpaper, so supply one. The Play Store listing uses a 512×512 px icon.

    A browser shows your favicon at 16×16 css px in tabs (32 px on high-density screens). Provide a 32 px PNG or a favicon.ico, an SVG favicon for modern browsers (an SVG favicon can even include a prefers-color-scheme media query to switch for dark tab bars), an apple-touch-icon at 180×180 px for iOS home screen bookmarks, and, for an installable web app, 192 and 512 px icons in the web app manifest, including a "maskable" version with padding for Android's masks.

    Five icon principles

    1. One idea. A single object or symbol. A camera lens, a checkbox, a bear's head, a page. If you need two, you are explaining, not identifying.
    2. Recognizable silhouette. Fill the shape with black: can you still tell what it is? Silhouette is what survives at 29 pt and in the tinted mode.
    3. No text. Words in icons are unreadable at small sizes and repeat the label underneath. A single letter can work if it is drawn as a shape, not typed.
    4. Distinct in a grid. Look at the most common icons on your users' screens. If they are mostly white and blue, a dark or warm icon will be found faster.
    5. Consistent with the product. The icon's color and shape should appear in the app (accent color, the same glyph in the empty state), so the icon promises what opening the app delivers.
    Lab: one icon, five sizes
    The same drawing rendered at 180 px (the 1024 master scaled down, as on a 3× phone home screen), 60, 29 and 16 px, at true pixel size on your screen. Switch concepts and watch what survives at 29 and 16. Then switch to monochrome: the text and thin-line concepts lose almost everything.
    For the solo creator

    Draw your icon as an SVG with named layers (background, foreground, highlight) and write a tiny script that exports every platform size, the monochrome Android layer, and the favicons from that one file. When you tweak the icon later, every size updates together. If you want a richer, illustrated icon later, this simple version is still your 16 px and themed fallback.

    • Test the icon on real home screens (yours, friends') with dark and light wallpapers.
    • Provide dark, tinted or monochrome variants where the platform asks.
    • Hand-tune the 16 and 32 px favicons; do not just scale down.

    Don't

    • Bake rounded corners or transparency into an iOS icon.
    • Put the key shape near the edges where masks cut it.
    • Use a photo, a screenshot, or your logo plus tagline as an icon.
    • Copy the category's dominant look (every notes app in yellow).

    • HalideLux, 2017

    Common mistakes

    Judging the icon on a big artboard

    That is where you draw it, so that is where you see it.

    Put it on your phone through a test build or a home-screen web bookmark on day one and live with it for a week.

    The identity kit and the one-page brand sheet

    Taught by Massimo Vignelli, with Kenya Hara and Mailchimp's content style guide

    A small product needs a small identity kit: one accent color plus neutrals, one or two typefaces, an imagery rule, an illustration rule (often "none"), and a voice. Write it on one page so every future decision can be checked against it in a minute.

    Massimo Vignelli's Canon (2010) argued for a disciplined, small set of typefaces and a grid, applied with rigor. The point was never minimalism for its own sake. It was that a small vocabulary used consistently is more recognizable than a large one used loosely. For you, the identity kit is also a time-saver: when you design a new screen, an email or a launch image at midnight, you are choosing from five options, not five hundred.

    The five parts

    Color
    One accent that is yours, used sparingly (for the primary action, selection and the icon), plus a neutral scale for text and surfaces. Define it as tokens (see Systems & Platforms) with light and dark values. Check contrast for text uses (see Accessible & Inclusive).
    Type
    One family for interface and marketing, or a pair: a reading face and an interface face. Name the sizes you use for headlines on the site. Using the same type in the app and on the site is one of the strongest consistency signals you have.
    Imagery
    A rule for pictures. For software, the best rule is usually "real product UI, cropped close, on a plain background". No stock photos of people pointing at laptops.
    Illustration
    Decide whether you use illustration at all. If yes, one style (line weight, color use, level of detail), drawn by one hand. If you cannot draw, "none, use the product" is a strong and honest rule.
    Voice
    Your attribute pairs from Chapter 1, three example sentences that sound like you, and a short list of words you never use. See Words.

    The anatomy of a landing page

    Taught by Steve Krug, with Joanna Wiebe and Julian Shapiro

    A landing page answers a visitor's questions in the order they ask them: What is it? Is it for me? Why should I believe it? What does it cost me (in money, time, risk)? What do I do now? Sections exist to answer those questions, not to fill a template.

    Steve Krug's Don't Make Me Think (2000) observed that people do not read web pages, they scan them, pick the first reasonable option, and muddle through. On a landing page that means most visitors read the headline, glance at the picture, maybe read a subheading, and decide. A few will scroll and read everything; they are often your most serious prospects. A good page serves both: the scanner gets the answer in the top screen, and the reader finds every objection handled further down.

    The hero: what, who, why

    The hero is the top screen: headline, a one- or two-sentence subheadline, a primary call to action, and a product visual. Together they must say what the product is (category, in plain words), who it is for (or what situation), and why it is better for them (the outcome or the difference). The headline can carry one or two of those; the subheadline carries the rest. The visual proves it.

    Notice that "what" usually needs a category word. "A notes app", "an issue tracker", "a podcast player". Builders often avoid the category because it feels unoriginal. But a category word lets the visitor load a whole mental model instantly; your differentiator then says how you differ from that model. Without it, the visitor has to build a model from scratch, and most will not bother. Dunford makes this point forcefully: the category is a frame of reference, and choosing it is a positioning decision.

    Benefits over features (with features as proof)

    A feature is what the product has. A benefit is what the user gets. "Full-text search across all highlights" is a feature. "Find that quote you half-remember in two seconds" is a benefit. Lead with the benefit, then give the feature as proof, because benefits without features sound like empty promises and features without benefits make the visitor do the translation.

    Social proof, objections and the call to action

    Social proof works because people use others' behavior as evidence when they are uncertain. It only works if it is believable: specific testimonials with a real name and context ("Found a quote from 2019 in seconds", Priya, editor) beat vague praise ("Amazing app!!"). Before you have users, skip proof rather than fake it. A short note from you about why you built it is more credible than borrowed logos.

    Objections are the reasons a willing visitor still hesitates. For most small software they are the same handful: price and how billing works, privacy and where data lives, lock-in and export, platform support, and "will this be around next year?" (a real worry with solo products). Answer them plainly in an FAQ. Answering "what if you stop working on this?" honestly (export formats, a promise of notice) is a strong trust signal precisely because few companies do it.

    The call to action should be one primary action, worded as what happens ("Download for Mac", "Start free trial") rather than a vague "Get started". Put the risk-reducers right next to it: "Free for 14 days, no card", "One-time purchase". Repeat it at the end of the page for readers who scrolled.

    For the solo creator

    Build your landing page as a single static HTML page in the same repo as your product, using your product's tokens. It will load fast, you can change copy in a minute, and it will look like your product because it is made of it. A heavy site builder template invites you to fill sections you do not need.

    Do

    • Say the category in plain words in the hero.
    • Show the real product in the first screen.
    • Write three benefits, each backed by a visible feature.
    • Answer price, privacy, export and longevity in an FAQ.
    • Use one primary call to action, repeated at the end.

    Don't

    • Lead with a slogan that would fit any product.
    • Use stock photos or abstract 3D art instead of your UI.
    • Show a wall of twelve equal feature tiles.
    • Invent urgency (fake countdowns, "3 spots left").
    • Show logos of companies whose employees merely signed up.

    • Thingsculturedcode.com

    • Plausible Analytics2019

    Common mistakes

    Template-driven sections

    Templates come with "Features", "Testimonials", "Integrations", "Team" blocks, and empty blocks feel unfinished.

    Start from the visitor's questions and delete every block that does not answer one.

    Hiding the price

    Fear that the price will scare people off.

    Visitors assume a hidden price is high or a trap. Show it, or at least the starting price and model.

    Clever over clear

    The headline is the most visible thing you write, so you want it to be impressive.

    Write the clear version first. Only keep a clever version if it is clearer still (Chapter 9).

    Check yourself

    Your page has a strong hero, but heatmaps show most visitors leave after the first screen. Many people who do sign up came via a long FAQ link from a forum. What does this suggest?

    Most visitors are not your audience and leave fast; that is fine. The ones who read deeply are often the ones who convert. A good page serves both behaviors rather than optimizing for one.

    Writing the headline

    Taught by Joanna Wiebe (Copyhackers), with David Ogilvy and Harry Dry

    A landing page headline should make the right visitor think "that's for me" in one reading. It does that by being specific about the outcome, the audience or the category, and by sounding like the words customers already use.

    David Ogilvy wrote in Ogilvy on Advertising (1983) that far more people read the headline than the body copy, so the headline is most of the ad's value. On the web the ratio is even more extreme. The headline is also the hardest thing to write because it has to be short and specific at once, and specificity feels risky: it excludes people. But exclusion is the point. A headline that tries to include everyone speaks to nobody.

    Joanna Wiebe's method at Copyhackers is to steal the words from your customers: read reviews, support emails, forum posts and interviews, and collect the exact phrases people use to describe their problem and their wished-for outcome. Customers' words beat your words because visitors recognize their own thoughts. "I forget everything I read" is a better seed than "knowledge retention".

        Trying to say everything

        Every feature feels essential.

        The headline says one thing. The subhead says two more. The page says the rest.

        Never revisiting it

        It was written once at launch.

        The five-second test and the fold

        Taught by usability practice (Christine Perfetti and Jared Spool's popularization), with Nielsen Norman Group's scrolling research

        Show your hero to someone in your audience for five seconds, hide it, and ask what the product is, who it is for and what they would do next. If they cannot answer, the page does not work, however good it looks.

        The five-second test works because it simulates the real decision. Visitors land from a link, glance, and judge whether to stay. In five seconds a person can take in the headline, the general type of image, and the main button. They cannot read paragraphs. So the test isolates exactly what the top of your page communicates at a glance. It costs nothing, and five people are usually enough to show the big problems (testing with small groups is covered in Testing & Iteration).

        Lab: five-second test simulator
        Press start. The hero appears for five seconds, then disappears, and you answer three questions from memory.

        1. What is this product?

        2. Who is it for?

        3. What would you do next?

        Run it on Version A, then B. Honestly answer from memory. Notice how B's answers come without effort while A's require guessing. Real tests use someone who has never seen the page; you can use this lab's structure with a friend and your own hero.

        How to run it for real

        1. Screenshot the top screen of your page at a common laptop size and at phone size.
        2. Find five people in or near your audience. Friends of friends, a community you belong to, or a remote testing service. Not other founders who know your product.
        3. Show for five seconds, then hide. Ask: "What is this product? Who is it for? What would you do next? What do you remember?"
        4. Write down their exact words. Count how many got "what" and "who" right. Under four of five means rewrite.
        5. Change one thing (usually the headline or the image) and test again with new people.

        The fold: myths and what is true

        Two opposite myths circulate. One says "everything important must be above the fold, nobody scrolls". The other says "the fold is dead, everyone scrolls". Both are wrong. The top screen does get the most attention, so it must answer what and who. But people scroll happily when the top earns it, so you do not need to cram the whole page into the hero. And remember the fold is not a fixed line: it moves with every screen size. Test the hero on a phone, where the fold is very high and the product visual often lands below it.

        Do

        • Test the hero with five people from your audience before launch.
        • Record exact words, not impressions.
        • Test phone and laptop separately.
        • Change one thing per round.

        Don't

        • Test on people who already know your product.
        • Ask "do you like it?"
        • Cram everything above the fold.
        • Put the only CTA below a tall, abstract hero image.

        In the wild

        • Lyssnaformerly UsabilityHub

        • Mobile-first heroescommon pattern

        Friends and other founders are easy to reach.

        They will fill gaps from what they know about you. Find people who match your audience and know nothing about the product.

        Hero image that is a mood

        An atmospheric image feels premium.

        If the image does not show the product or its result, it is using your most valuable space to say nothing.

        Check yourself

        Five testers saw your hero for five seconds. Three said "some kind of AI writing tool", two said "not sure". Your product is a highlights app. What do you change first?

        Nobody understood "what". That is a headline and visual problem. Five people are enough to see a failure this clear; more testers would only confirm it. Fix the message, then re-test with new people.

        Screenshots, demos and showing the product

        Taught by Edward Tufte (show the data), with Bret Victor and the Dropbox demo video story

        The most persuasive thing on a software marketing page is the software. Show it real, cropped close to the part that matters, annotated with a few words, and moving only when motion explains something a still cannot.

        Tufte's advice for charts, to show the data rather than decoration, applies directly to product marketing: show the product, not a metaphor for it. A real screenshot does three jobs. It answers "what is it" faster than words. It proves the product exists (a surprisingly real worry with new products). And it lets the visitor imagine using it, which is the moment they start to want it.

        The classic story here is Dropbox. Before its public launch, Drew Houston made a short screencast showing files syncing between computers, and its waitlist reportedly jumped from about 5,000 to 75,000 people overnight, as Eric Ries recounted in The Lean Startup (2011). Syncing is invisible and hard to describe; a few minutes of seeing it work did what paragraphs could not.

        Four rules for screenshots

        1. Real UI, real-looking data. Use your actual app with realistic content (real book titles, plausible notes), not "Lorem ipsum" or obviously fake "Task 1, Task 2".
        2. Crop to the point. A full-window screenshot at landing-page size makes every element tiny. Crop to the panel that proves the benefit, at a size where its text is readable.
        3. Annotate sparingly. One or two short labels with thin leader lines, saying the benefit, not the control name ("Finds it even with a typo", not "Search field").
        4. Keep it current. Out-of-date screenshots are a broken promise. Automate them if you can, or put "update screenshots" on your release checklist.

        When to use motion

        Use video or animation when the benefit is a change over time: syncing, importing, a fast keyboard flow, an undo, a transition that shows where something went. Keep loops short (about 5–15 seconds) and focused on one action. Autoplay only if muted, without sound by default, with a still poster frame, and respect prefers-reduced-motion by showing the still instead. A longer demo (one to three minutes) belongs behind a play button, for the visitors who want it. Add captions; many people watch with sound off.

        The product visual in the hero
        Without: the full app window, tilted in fake 3D, with decorative blobs. At hero size every label is a grey line. It signals "there is an app" but shows nothing you can want.
        With: one panel, flat, at a readable size, with believable content. You can read the highlight and the note and imagine your own. That is the moment of desire.
        For the solo creator

        Add a "marketing" seed dataset to your app (a demo library with real, public-domain quotes and realistic notes) and a script that opens the app in that state at fixed window sizes and captures screenshots. Every release, run it. Your screenshots will always be current, and you will never fill a demo account by hand again.

        Do

        • Use real UI with realistic, public-domain or invented-but-plausible content.
        • Crop each image to one benefit.
        • Use short, muted, looped clips for change-over-time benefits.
        • Offer a still image when reduced motion is on.

        Don't

        • Fake the UI with mockups of features that do not exist.
        • Tilt screenshots into 3D so they look "dynamic".
        • Autoplay video with sound.
        • Use a two-minute video as the only explanation.

        In the wild

          A simple screen recording of syncing made an invisible benefit visible and built a waitlist before launch. If your benefit is invisible, show it happening.
        • Linearlinear.app
          The homepage relies on real, crisp product UI, often in short animated sequences that show keyboard-driven flows. Speed is a benefit you can only prove with motion.

        Designing an honest pricing page

        Taught by Patrick McKenzie, with Dan Ariely and Madhavan Ramanujam

        A pricing page should make it easy to pick the right plan, understand exactly what you will pay and when, and know how to leave. Use anchoring and plan structure to help people choose, never to trick them.

        Pricing pages are where design meets money, so they attract the most manipulation. That is also why honesty there is so visible. A visitor on your pricing page is already interested. Their job is to answer: which plan fits me, what will I be charged (total, including tax, and how often), what happens at the end of a trial, and can I get out? Every element should help answer one of those.

        Patrick McKenzie (patio11) has argued for years that independent software makers tend to charge too little, and that plans should be differentiated by the value different customers get, not by arbitrary limits. Madhavan Ramanujam's Monetizing Innovation (2016) adds that you should design the product around what people will pay for, rather than pricing it at the end. For you, that means pricing is a design decision you make early, not a number you add at launch.

        Plans: good, better, best

        Three plans is a common structure because it maps to three kinds of customers (trying, committed, heavy or team) and because the middle option is easy to choose. Each plan should be defined by who it is for, not by a random cut of features. "Free: for trying Margin with one book", "Pro: for readers", "Pro + Sync: for readers on several devices" is clearer than "Basic, Plus, Premium".

        Anchoring and decoys, honestly

        Anchoring means the first number people see shapes how they judge the next. A decoy is an option designed mainly to make another look better. Both are real effects, and you cannot avoid them, since any page with more than one price has anchors. The ethical line is simple: every plan you show must be a plan someone should rationally buy, and every number must be the real one. Showing a team plan first so the personal plan feels reasonable is fine if teams really buy it. Inventing a "Premium" tier at ten times the price that nobody can use is a trick. For the broader ethics, see Honest & Calm.

        Many calm, local-first apps sell a one-time license, sometimes with paid major upgrades. It is honest and well liked; it needs a plan for ongoing revenue (paid upgrades, an optional sync subscription).
        Cancellation
        Say how to cancel on the pricing page and make it as easy as signing up. Do it regardless: a hard cancellation is a dark pattern, and it turns customers into critics.

        Regional pricing and India

        A $10 monthly price is a coffee in San Francisco and a large share of a student's monthly spending money in many countries. Purchasing power parity (PPP) pricing adjusts prices by country so the product is similarly affordable everywhere. App stores make this partly automatic: Apple and Google set local prices per storefront, and you can adjust them. On the web, payment providers and merchant-of-record services let you set prices by currency or offer country-based discounts.

        For India specifically: show prices in rupees, not converted dollars; consider UPI and local card support (your payment provider choice matters); remember that GST applies to digital services and consumers expect to see the final price; and know that RBI rules since 2021 require additional authentication for recurring card payments (e-mandates), which can make subscription renewals fail silently if your provider does not support them well. Many global services price far lower in India: Netflix, for example, offers a mobile-only plan there. Test your India price by asking your target users what they pay for comparable apps, not by converting your US price.

        App store pages

        Taught by Apple's App Store product page guidance, with Google Play's store listing guidance

        An app store page is a landing page with strict limits. Your icon, name, subtitle and first two or three screenshots do almost all the work, because that is what shows in search results. Make the screenshots tell a story, one benefit per frame.

        In App Store search results, people see your icon, name, subtitle, rating and the first screenshots (or a preview video). Many never open the full page. So the first screenshots are your hero. They must answer what and why at a glance, at the size of a thumbnail. A grid of plain UI captures without captions rarely does that; a sequence with a short benefit caption above a cropped, readable UI usually does.

        The limits you design within

        • Name: up to 30 characters. Subtitle: up to 30 characters, shown under the name; use it for the category and benefit ("Book highlights & notes").
        • Keywords field: 100 characters, comma-separated, not visible to users; do not repeat words already in your name.
        • Promotional text: up to 170 characters at the top of the description, which you can change without submitting a new version. Use it for news.
        • Screenshots: up to 10 per device size. App previews: up to 3 short videos, 15–30 seconds each.
        • What's New: release notes for each version, shown on the page.
        • Title: up to 30 characters. Short description: up to 80 characters, shown prominently.
        • Full description: up to 4,000 characters, and it is indexed for search, so write it for people first and include the words they search for naturally.
        • Feature graphic: 1024×500 px, shown in some placements and above a promo video.
        • Screenshots: up to 8 per device type.
        • Release notes per version.

        Ratings prompts: timing is the design

        On iOS, the system review prompt (requested through requestReview) is shown at most three times in a 365-day period per user, and the system decides whether to show it at all. So each request is precious. Ask right after a moment of success (the user finished their first daily review, or exported notes), never at launch, never in the middle of a task, and never after an error. Do not put your own "Do you love Margin?" gate before the system prompt to filter out unhappy users; Apple's guidelines discourage custom review prompts, and it is a manipulative pattern anyway. Make it easy for unhappy users to reach you directly from Settings instead.

        For the solo creator

        Generate store screenshots with the same seed-data script as your website images (Chapter 11), then add captions in a simple HTML template rendered at each required size. One command, every device size, every release. Write the release notes as you merge each change, not on submission day.

        Do

        • Put the category and benefit in the subtitle.
        • Make the first three screenshots work as thumbnails.
        • Ask for ratings right after a success moment.
        • Write release notes for humans, every version.

        Don't

        • Stuff keywords into the name.
        • Use screenshots of empty states or settings.
        • Ask for a rating on first launch or after an error.
        • Ship "Bug fixes and performance improvements" every time.

        Raw UI screenshots

        Easy to capture and "honest".

        Wasting the review prompt

        Asking on launch feels like the moment of most attention.

        Tie it to a success event and add a minimum usage threshold (for example, after the third successful review session).

        Before launch: waitlists and building in public

        Taught by Pieter Levels, with Arvid Kahl and Rahul Vohra

        The weeks before launch are for finding the first people who care, learning their words, and giving them a reason to come back on launch day. A waitlist and honest public progress notes do this cheaply, if you treat the people on them as collaborators rather than numbers.

        A launch day with no one waiting is a launch into silence. The mechanism you need is simple: people who have already raised their hand (by joining a list, following your progress, or trying a beta) are many times more likely to show up, try the product and talk about it than strangers. So pre-launch work is about collecting and nurturing those hands. It is also research: every reply to a waitlist email is a customer telling you, in their words, what they want.

        A waitlist page that is also research

        A waitlist page is a landing page with a smaller ask: an email address. Keep the hero as clear as any landing page (Chapter 8), show whatever real UI you have, and say honestly when access is expected. Then add one optional question after sign-up: "What do you hope this will help you with?" or "What do you use now?" The answers are gold. They tell you who is signing up, in their words, and they start a conversation you can continue by email.

        Some waitlists add referral mechanics ("move up the list by inviting friends"). Robinhood famously grew a large pre-launch waitlist this way, showing people their position in line. These mechanics can work, but they turn your early community into a scoreboard and can attract people who want the prize, not the product. For a calm product, a plain list and personal replies usually fit the brand better.

        Building in public

        Building in public means sharing your progress, decisions, numbers and mistakes openly while you build. Pieter Levels popularized a version of it with public revenue dashboards for products like Nomad List; Buffer published salaries and revenue in the 2010s. The benefits are real: an audience that knows your story before launch, feedback on decisions while they are cheap to change, and accountability. The costs are also real: time, distraction, the temptation to optimize for followers rather than users, and competitors who can watch.

        A calm version works well: one short post a week about a real decision you made and why, with a screenshot. "This week I removed tags and replaced them with books as the only organizer. Here's why." That is useful to readers, honest about your process, and doubles as a design journal you will learn from yourself.

        For the solo creator

        Write your weekly note in the same place every week (a blog, a newsletter, one social account) and reuse it: the same text becomes a changelog entry, a waitlist update and, months later, raw material for your founder story. Pick one channel where your users already are. You do not need to be everywhere.

        Do

        • Ask one research question on the waitlist.
        • Reply personally to every answer.
        • Run a small private beta and fix what it reveals.
        • Post weekly about real decisions, with real screenshots.

        Don't

        • Hide the product behind mystery.
        • Inflate waitlist numbers.
        • Promise a date you cannot keep.
        • Spend more time posting about building than building.

        In the wild

        • Nomad ListPieter Levels, 2014
          Built and grown in public, with revenue and decisions shared openly, by a solo maker. Openness can be a marketing channel and a brand trait at once.
        • Superhumanwaitlist with onboarding
          Used its waitlist to survey and select users, then onboarded many personally. A waitlist is a research instrument, not only a hype device.
        • Robinhoodpre-launch, 2013–2014
          Showed sign-ups their place in line and let them move up by referring friends, building a very large waitlist. Referral mechanics grow lists fast; decide whether the game fits your brand.

        Common mistakes

        Collecting emails and never writing

        You plan to email "at launch".

        An email list cools fast. Send a short, real update every two to four weeks so people remember why they signed up.

        Beta without questions

        You invite people and wait for feedback that never comes.

        Give beta users one specific task and one question each week. Watch two of them use it live (see Testing & Iteration).

        Check yourself

        Your waitlist has 400 people. Launch is in three weeks. What is the best use of this week?

        The 400 are warm. A real update keeps them warm, the question teaches you their words, and a small beta finds launch-day bugs. Growing the list matters less than activating the people already on it.

        Launch day: Product Hunt, Show HN and communities

        Taught by Hacker News guidelines, with Product Hunt's maker guidance and Rami Ismail's presskit()

        On launch day you are a guest in other people's communities. Bring something people can try immediately, describe it plainly, be present to answer every question, and never ask for votes. Then have everything ready that a curious person or journalist might need.

        Launch platforms reward the same thing communities always reward: a useful contribution from a person who shows up. They punish what communities always punish: self-promotion that takes more than it gives. Think of launch day as a conversation, not a broadcast. The ranking is a side effect of how well that conversation goes.

        Where to launch

        Product Hunt ranks launches within a day that starts at 12:01 a.m. Pacific Time, so many makers post right then to get the whole day. You can post your own product as its maker. Prepare a short tagline (it has a character limit), a gallery of images (reuse your store frames), an optional video, and a "first comment" from you that tells the story: who you are, why you built it, what is special, and what feedback you want. Product Hunt's guidance discourages asking people to upvote; ask your list to "come check it out and leave feedback" instead. Results vary a lot by category; developer tools and productivity apps tend to find an audience there.

        Show HN on Hacker News is for things you have made that people can try. Its guidelines ask that the thing be usable right now (not a landing page, waitlist or fundraiser) and, ideally, that people can try it without signing up. Title it plainly: "Show HN: Margin, a calm app for book highlights and notes". Post a first comment explaining what it is, how it works (technical detail is welcome there), and what you would like feedback on. Never ask for upvotes or organize votes; vote rings are detected and penalized. Expect blunt feedback; answer every reasonable comment calmly and thank people for criticism. The famous early Dropbox thread, where a commenter argued users could build the same thing with existing tools, is a reminder that skeptical comments are normal and not a verdict.

        Smaller communities often matter more: subreddits about your topic, forums, Discord or Slack groups, newsletters in your niche, Indian maker communities. Read each community's rules first; many ban self-promotion except in specific threads. Participate before you post. When you do post, lead with the problem and your story, not a link dump, and stay to answer. One thoughtful post in a community of your exact users beats ten generic launches.

        The press kit

        Journalists, newsletter writers and bloggers will not ask you for assets; they will use whatever they find or skip you. A press kit is one page with everything they need: a one-paragraph description, a short and a long version, key facts (price, platforms, release date, founder name, location), high-resolution screenshots, the icon and logo in several formats, a short video, a founder photo if you are comfortable, and a contact email. Rami Ismail's free presskit() format, made for indie game developers, popularized this simple, one-page structure.

        The Show HN first comment

        🚀 Super excited to launch Margin!!! The ultimate AI-powered reading platform that will revolutionize how you read. Please upvote and share with everyone! Sign up at our website to join the waitlist. 🙏

        Hi HN, I'm Rohit. I made Margin because I had thousands of Kindle highlights and could never find any of them again.

        It imports Kindle and PDF highlights, lets you write a note next to each, and shows you one a day. It's local-first: notes live in a SQLite file on your device and export to Markdown. Sync is optional.

        You can try it without an account (Mac and web demo). I'd especially like feedback on the import from PDFs, which is the hardest part. Happy to answer questions about how it works.

        Without: hype words, emoji, a request for votes (against the rules) and a waitlist (not something people can try). This post will be flagged or ignored.
        With: a person, a real problem, what it does, a technical detail the audience will appreciate, try-without-signup, and a specific request for feedback.
        Watch out

        Do not measure your launch by its rank. A good launch is one where real people in your audience tried the product, some came back the next week, and you learned three things you did not know. A top ranking with no retained users is a spike, not a launch.

        For the solo creator

        Launch day is exhausting alone. Prepare everything a week early: posts written, images exported, press kit live, emails scheduled, a status page and a canned "we're on it" reply. Then the day itself is just showing up and replying. Do not launch on the same day as a big release on your platform's own calendar if you can avoid it, and do not ship risky code that morning.

        Do

        • Make the product tryable without sign-up where possible.
        • Write a personal, plain first comment.
        • Answer every comment, including harsh ones.
        • Have a press kit page ready before launch.

        Don't

        • Ask for upvotes or organize voting.
        • Post a waitlist as a Show HN.
        • Argue defensively with critics.
        • Launch everywhere on the same day and answer nowhere well.

        In the wild

        • Dropbox on HN2007
          Its early HN post drew a now-famous skeptical comment about building it yourself with existing tools; the product went on to great success. Criticism on launch day is data, not destiny.
        • presskit()Rami Ismail, Vlambeer
          A free, simple press kit template widely used by indie game developers. Make it effortless for others to write about you.

        Common mistakes

        Launching once, loudly

        A launch feels like a single shot.

        You can launch major updates again later, and small communities one at a time. Plan a series of smaller launches.

        No time to reply

        You scheduled the launch around work or sleep.

        Block the whole day. Replies in the first hours shape the whole thread.

        Check yourself

        Your Show HN gets a top comment: "This is just a fancy Markdown folder. Why would I pay?" What is the best reply?

        A calm, honest reply that names the real difference and respects the alternative wins over readers, not just the commenter. Ignoring it leaves the strongest objection unanswered; arguing or vote manipulation damages trust and breaks the rules.

        Docs, support, changelogs and onboarding emails

        Taught by Daniele Procida (Diátaxis), with Keep a Changelog and Kathy Sierra

        After launch, your brand lives in the places people go when they are stuck, curious or deciding whether to stay: the help pages, your support replies, the changelog and the first emails. Design each as carefully as a screen.

        The mechanism is trust under stress. People form their deepest opinion of a product when something goes wrong or when they are unsure. A fast, warm, specific support reply from the person who made the product turns a problem into loyalty. A clear help page lets someone succeed without asking. A living changelog shows the product is cared for, which addresses the biggest worry about solo products: "will this be abandoned?"

        Documentation: four kinds

        Daniele Procida's Diátaxis framework separates documentation into four kinds, because each serves a different need. Mixing them is why many help centers feel confusing.

        Support replies are design

        1. Answer fast, even if partially. "Got it, looking now" within hours beats a perfect answer in three days.
        2. Repeat the problem in your own words. It proves you read it and catches misunderstandings.
        3. Give the fix in steps, with a screenshot or a link to a how-to page. If the fix is a bug on your side, say so plainly.
        4. Close the loop. When you ship a fix, email the person. This single habit creates more fans than any feature.
        5. Turn repeats into pages and design changes. If three people ask the same thing, write a how-to; if ten do, change the product.

        The changelog

        A public changelog shows momentum and care. The Keep a Changelog convention groups changes under Added, Changed, Deprecated, Removed, Fixed and Security, with dates and newest entries first. For users, add a human layer: one sentence on why the change matters, a screenshot for anything visible, and thanks to people who reported issues. Offer an RSS feed or an email digest. Linear's public changelog, with visuals and regular entries, is often cited as a model.

        Onboarding emails

        The first emails after sign-up are part of onboarding (activation is covered in Problems Worth Solving). Their job is to help the person reach the first moment of value, not to sell. Behavior-based emails beat a fixed drip: send the "here's how to import" tip only to people who have not imported yet.

        Telling the designer-founder story

        Taught by Oliver Reichenstein (iA), with Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson (37signals)

        People trust small products more when they can see the person behind them and understand what that person believes. A short, honest founder story and a manifesto-like statement of principles turn your opinions into a reason to choose you.

        A big company competes on scale and features. You compete on point of view and care. The founder story makes both visible. It answers questions a careful buyer has about a small product: Who is behind this? Why do they care? Will they still care next year? What will they never do? iA's essays, written by Oliver Reichenstein and his team, explain why the company makes the decisions it does (one font, few settings, focus). 37signals has written books (Getting Real, 2006; Rework, 2010) and manifestos for products like HEY that are as much about beliefs as features. Both turned opinions into a brand people sign up for.

        What goes in a small-product manifesto

        • What we believe: two or three convictions about the problem ("Reading is wasted if you can't find what you read").
        • What we will always do: promises you can keep ("Your notes export to plain Markdown, always").
        • What we will never do: the most credible part ("No ads. No selling data. No streaks or guilt notifications").
        • Who is behind it: your name, where you work from, how to reach you.
        • How it is paid for: people trust what they understand. "You pay, so you are the customer."
        The most credible part of any manifesto is the list of things you will never do, because it costs you something to promise.A principle for this course, drawn from the practice of iA and 37signals
        For the solo creator

        Do not pretend to be a team. Being one person is a strength for a calm product: it means one point of view, direct contact and care. Address the risk openly instead ("If I ever stop working on Margin, I'll give at least six months' notice and keep export working"). That sentence does more for trust than any "we".

        Do

        • Write in first person, with your name.
        • Tell the lived problem in specific terms.
        • List what you will never do.
        • Say how the product is funded.

        Don't

        • Say "we" when you mean "I".
        • Make promises you cannot keep for years.
        • Attack competitors by name; state your belief instead.
        • Turn the manifesto into a sales page.

        In the wild

        • iAessays, 2010s onward
          Long essays on writing, typography and focus that explain the product's choices. Explaining your decisions recruits people who share your values.
        • HEY37signals, 2020
          Launched with a long, opinionated page about what was wrong with email and what HEY would do differently, written in the founders' voice. A strong point of view can be the main marketing.
        • Getting Real37signals, 2006
          A book of principles about building smaller, simpler software, which also explained the company's products. Publishing your method builds an audience before and beyond any one product.

        Common mistakes

        Manifesto without a product to back it

        Writing beliefs is easier than shipping them.

        Every belief should be visible in a product decision. If not, cut the belief or change the product.

        Too long, too early

        You have a lot to say.

        Start with 200 words. Add essays later, one decision at a time.

        Check yourself

        Which manifesto line is most credible for a solo calm-notes product?

        D is specific, checkable and costs something to promise (it rules out common engagement tactics). The others are claims anyone can make.

        Measuring the marketing site without obsessing

        Taught by conversion basics, with privacy-first analytics makers and Tufte on honest numbers

        Track a few numbers that describe a simple funnel (visit, sign-up or download, first value, paid), look at them weekly rather than hourly, and pair them with words from real people. At small traffic, qualitative evidence beats split tests.

        Analytics can turn a calm maker into an anxious one. The cure is to decide in advance which few numbers you care about and why. A conversion rate is the share of people at one step who reach the next. For a small product the useful ones are: visitors to sign-ups (or downloads), sign-ups to activated (reached first value), and activated to paid. Each tells you where to look. A low first rate points at the landing page; a low second at onboarding; a low third at value or pricing.

        Why split tests rarely work at small scale

        A/B tests need a lot of traffic to tell a real difference from noise. As a rough worked example: if 2% of visitors sign up now and you want to detect an improvement to 3% (a big 50% relative lift) with the usual statistical standards (95% confidence, 80% power), you need roughly 3,800 visitors per variant. Detecting a smaller lift, say 2.0% to 2.2%, needs tens of thousands per variant. Most small products do not have that traffic, so tests run for months or end with false winners. Use five-second tests, interviews and the "how did you hear about us?" question instead, and save A/B tests for when you have the traffic.

        Wrap-up

        Everything in this guide follows from one idea: the outward-facing parts of your product are designed artefacts with users and jobs. A name must be said and spelled. An icon must be found in a grid. A headline must be understood in five seconds. A pricing page must be understood and trusted. A launch post must be useful to a community. A support reply must solve a problem. Treat each as a design problem, keep the same promise and voice across all of them, and your small product will feel larger and more trustworthy than its size.

        Ten things to remember

        1. A brand is a promise kept at every touchpoint: promise, personality, consistency. Positioning comes first, visuals last.
        2. Most brand impressions in software happen inside the product and its emails, errors and support replies. Fix those first.
        3. Good names are easy to say, spell, find and own. Generate a hundred, cut hard, check stores, trademarks and languages.
        4. Start with a wordmark. Add a mark with one idea that passes the 16 px and monochrome tests.
        5. An app icon is found by visual search: one idea, strong silhouette, no text, different from its neighbors.
        6. Fit your identity on one page: one accent, one or two typefaces, real UI as imagery, a voice with a never-say list.
        7. A landing page answers what, who, why, proof, risk and next, in that order; the hero must pass a five-second test.
        8. Show the real product, cropped to one benefit, moving only when motion explains.
        9. Honest pricing shows real charges, real plans, trial terms and cancellation in plain sight; price locally where it matters.
        10. Launch as a guest: be tryable, be present, never ask for votes. Then measure weekly, with words beside numbers.
