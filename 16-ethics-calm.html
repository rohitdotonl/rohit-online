Warm-up: what do you already notice?

Before we name anything, test your instincts. You have spent years on the receiving end of these designs: the subscription that would not die, the cookie banner with one big green button, the app that pings you at 9:47 pm with "You have new activity!". You already know a lot. The goal of this guide is to turn that vague irritation into precise vocabulary and, more importantly, into design decisions you make on purpose in your own product.

How to study this guide: read one Part per sitting. Do the quizzes before reading the explanation. Do at least the exercises marked "on your own product", because ethics you have not applied to your own work is just opinion. Diagnostic 1

Your reading app's paid plan converts poorly. A friend suggests making the "Start free trial" button big and green and changing the dismiss link to "No thanks, I don't care about reading more". What is the real problem with this suggestion?

This is confirmshaming. It works on some people in the short term because nobody likes to agree with an insulting sentence about themselves. Part one

Honesty: don't trick people

We start with the clearest line in product ethics: do not deceive. Dieter Rams made honesty one of his ten principles in the 1970s; Harry Brignull gave the violations names in 2010; regulators on three continents have since turned many of those names into law. This Part teaches you the mechanism behind deception (it rides on predictable human biases), the main families of deceptive patterns, an honest redesign for each, and what the law now says. You will finish by playing a spotter game on a mock checkout.

1

Good design is honest

Taught by Dieter Rams, with Kenya Hara and Don Norman

An honest product shows what it is, does what it says, and costs what it claims. Every gap between what the interface implies and what is true is a lie, even if no sentence in it is false.

Rams wrote his ten principles of good design while he was head of design at Braun. Two of them frame this whole guide. The sixth: good design is honest. In the version Vitsœ publishes, it does not make a product appear more innovative, powerful or valuable than it really is, and it does not try to manipulate the consumer with promises that cannot be kept. The seventh: good design is long-lasting. It avoids being fashionable, and therefore never appears antiquated. We come back to longevity in Part 3. Here, honesty.

For a physical object, honesty is mostly about form. A radio should not pretend to be a piece of furniture; a plastic part should not be painted to look like metal. Software has no material, so its honesty lives somewhere else: in the relationship between what the interface signals and what the system actually does. Norman would call this the gap between the conceptual model the user forms and the real model underneath. Normally we try to close that gap so people are not confused. A deceptive pattern opens the gap on purpose, so that people act on a false model in a way that benefits the business.

Four places software can lie

When you audit your product for honesty, you are looking in four places. Each has its own common lies.

  1. Signals. What visual weight, color and position imply. A green, huge "Accept" next to a gray, tiny "Settings" says "this is the normal choice" even if nobody wrote that.
  2. Words. What labels, headlines and microcopy state or imply. "Free" that turns into a charge, "Only 2 left" that is always 2, "Continue" that actually means "subscribe".
  3. Defaults. What happens if the person does nothing. Pre-ticked boxes, opt-outs buried in settings, auto-renewal with no reminder.
  4. Effort. How hard each path is. Sign-up in one tap, cancel by phone during office hours. The asymmetry is itself a message: we will make leaving painful.

Why honesty is also the better business for you

Deception works in a narrow, measurable way: it raises a short-term number (sign-ups, add-ons, retention this month). It costs in ways that are slower and harder to measure: refunds, chargebacks (payment processors penalize merchants with high chargeback rates), support load, angry reviews, app store rejection, legal risk, and, worst for a small product, the death of word of mouth. A large company can absorb that. A one-person product lives on recommendations from people who trust it. Your honesty is your marketing budget.

There is also a quieter argument. Kenya Hara writes about design as a way of making people perceive things clearly, of "awakening" them to what is already there. Deception is the precise opposite: it designs for a person's inattention. If you want to be the kind of designer who makes things people see clearly, you cannot also be the kind who relies on them not seeing.

Key idea

Ask "what will a busy person believe after two seconds?" and design so that belief is true.

Do

  • Put the real cost, duration and renewal terms at the same visual level as the offer.
  • Make the honest path the easy path: equal effort to say yes and no.
  • Describe features as they are today, not as the roadmap hopes.
  • Let defaults favor the user when in doubt (off for marketing, off for sharing).

Don't

  • Rely on "it's technically in the terms".
  • Use visual weight to hide the option you would rather people did not pick.
  • Claim "AI-powered", "secure", "private" or "unlimited" without being precise about what that means.
  • Test deceptive variants "just to see"; the variant that wins is often the one that tricks best.

For the solo creator

You are designer, engineer and the person who reads the refund emails. That is your advantage: you feel the cost of a dishonest pattern directly. Keep a "surprise log": every support email that starts with "I didn't realize…" goes into one note. Each entry marks a gap between what your interface implied and what happened. Common mistakes

Confusing "persuasive" with "honest"

Good copy persuades by showing real value. Founders slide from "show value clearly" to "make the alternative look bad" without noticing.

Persuasion is fine when it would survive the user knowing exactly what you are doing. If the technique only works when hidden, it is manipulation.

Treating legal text as disclosure

Terms of service feel like they protect you, so it seems fine to put important facts there.

Anything that changes what a person pays, shares, or can undo belongs in the interface at the moment of decision, in plain words.

Copying a competitor's flow wholesale

Large products have spent years on growth experiments, so their flows look "proven".

Proven for which metric? Copy their structure only after auditing it with the four places to lie: signals, words, defaults, effort.

Check yourself

Your note-taking app's sync is "end-to-end encrypted" for note bodies, but titles and tags are stored in plain text on your server so search works. The marketing page says "Your notes are end-to-end encrypted." Which fix is most honest?

Precision is honesty. A reasonable reader of "your notes are end-to-end encrypted" assumes titles too, and titles often contain the most sensitive words. (b) throws away a real benefit; (c) is the legal-text mistake. Being exact also signals competence, which security-minded users notice.

How deceptive patterns work

Taught by Harry Brignull, with Daniel Kahneman and Arunesh Mathur

A deceptive pattern is an interface that exploits a predictable shortcut in human thinking to make people do something they did not intend. Learn the shortcuts and you can recognize any pattern, including new ones nobody has named yet.

Harry Brignull, a UX designer with a PhD in cognitive science, coined the term "dark patterns" in 2010 and started a website cataloguing them. He later renamed the site deceptive.design and, in 2023, published the book Deceptive Patterns. The names changed over the years (for example "roach motel" became "hard to cancel", "sneak into basket" became part of "sneaking", "privacy zuckering" was retired as a name), so you will see both old and new labels. This guide uses whichever name is clearest and mentions the other.

In 2019 a Princeton team led by Arunesh Mathur crawled around 11,000 shopping websites and found dark patterns on roughly one in ten of them, including fake countdown timers and fake "someone just bought this" messages, some generated by third-party plug-ins sold for that purpose. The point for you: these patterns are not rare and not only in big companies. They often arrive in your product through a template, a plug-in or a growth tutorial.

The engine underneath: shortcuts in thinking

Kahneman's distinction between fast, automatic thinking (System 1) and slow, deliberate thinking (System 2) explains why these patterns work. People using an app are almost always in System 1: skimming, tapping the obvious button, trusting defaults. Deceptive patterns are built to be correct for System 2 and misleading for System 1. The full information is there for a careful reader. Almost nobody is a careful reader at a checkout. (See The Human Mind for the full treatment of attention and bias.)

A test you can run on any design: the four questions

When a pattern does not have a name, run these four questions. If any answer is "yes", you are probably looking at a deceptive pattern.

  1. Would it stop working if users understood it? Honest persuasion survives being explained. "Annual plan saves 30%" still works when you know why it is there. A fake countdown does not.
  2. Is the effort asymmetric? Count the taps and the reading needed for each option. Yes in one tap and no in six is a tell.
  3. Would you be embarrassed to show it on stage? Imagine a slide at a design conference titled "How we got 12% more add-ons". Would you show the screenshot?
  4. Would the user be glad, a week later? This is the test we will reuse for habits and notifications in Part 2.
Deceptive patterns are not a design problem a designer can always fix alone; they are usually a business decision expressed in pixels.Paraphrasing a theme of Harry Brignull, Deceptive Patterns, 2023

Do

  • Learn the six shortcuts; they generalize to patterns that have no name yet.
  • Audit third-party plug-ins, checkout templates and "conversion" widgets before you install them.
  • Use the four questions as a review step for every flow involving money, data or commitment.

Don't

  • Assume a pattern is fine because a famous product uses it.
  • Argue about labels; argue about the gap and the effort.
  • Treat "users can read the details" as a defense; design for how people actually read.

It would become deceptive if the loss were exaggerated ("you'll lose all your progress" when it is archived anyway) or if "Delete" were hidden. Effort here is equal, and the statement survives being understood.

Patterns of pressure

Taught by Harry Brignull, with Robert Cialdini

Pressure patterns push a decision by manufacturing emotion: shame, fear of missing out, herd instinct, or simple fatigue. The honest version gives the same true information and lets the person decide at their own pace.

Cialdini's Influence (1984) described the levers that make people say yes: reciprocity, commitment, social proof, liking, authority and scarcity. Every one of them is real and every one can be used honestly. A genuinely limited batch is scarce. A genuinely popular plan has social proof. Pressure patterns are what you get when the lever is pulled without the fact behind it, or with the fact exaggerated. Below are the five you will meet most, each with a drawn honest alternative.

Confirmshaming

The decline option is worded to make the person feel stupid, selfish or lazy for declining: "No thanks, I prefer to stay uninformed." It works because agreeing with an insulting sentence about yourself is uncomfortable, so a few people click yes to avoid the discomfort. The honest version names both options neutrally. If your offer only works when "no" is humiliating, the offer is weak.

Fake scarcity and fake urgency

Scarcity ("only 2 left") and urgency ("offer ends in 09:59") both trigger loss aversion: we weigh a loss more heavily than an equal gain, so the fear of missing out short-circuits comparison shopping. They are honest when true and specific. They become deceptive when the number is invented, the timer resets on reload, or "only 2 left" means "only 2 left at this price on this site in this minute". In 2019 the UK's Competition and Markets Authority secured commitments from several large hotel booking sites to stop misleading pressure selling such as false claims about how many rooms were left or how many people were looking.

Fake social proof

"Priya from Pune just bought this", "1,203 people are viewing", "4.9 from 10,000 reviews". Social proof works because, under uncertainty, we copy what others do. Faking it is now explicitly targeted by regulators: the US FTC finalized a rule in 2024 banning fake reviews and testimonials, including reviews by people who do not exist and buying positive reviews. For a small product the temptation is different: you have few users, so the numbers look thin. The honest response is to show the proof you really have (one detailed quote from a named user, with permission) rather than inflate it.

Nagging

The same request, repeated until the person gives in: "Turn on notifications?" with only "OK" and "Not now", asked every launch. "Not now" is not "no"; it is a promise to ask again. Nagging exploits fatigue: eventually, saying yes costs less than one more dismissal. The honest version accepts a "no" and remembers it, and asks again only when something meaningful changes (the user creates their first reminder, for example), at most once.

Key idea

Every persuasion lever is honest when the fact behind it is true, specific and the same for everyone.

Do

  • Word "no" options neutrally ("Not now", "No, thanks", "Skip").
  • Use deadlines only with a public date and time you keep.
  • Show real counts with how they are measured, or show none.
  • Ask for a permission once, in context, and remember the answer.

Don't

  • Put fake stock limits on digital goods.
  • Reset or "extend" deadlines you said were final.
  • Install "recent purchase" pop-ups fed by anything other than real, consented data.
  • Offer only "OK" and "Later" for a question you will keep asking.

Patterns of sneaking

Taught by Harry Brignull, with Richard Thaler & Cass Sunstein

Sneaking patterns change the deal while the person is not looking: an item appears in the basket, a fee appears at the last step, an ad dresses up as a button, a question is worded to be misread, a box is already ticked. The honest version shows the whole deal, up front, in the words a skimmer will actually read.

Thaler and Sunstein's Nudge (2008) made "choice architecture" famous: the way options are arranged changes what people choose, and there is no neutral arrangement. They argued for nudges that are transparent and easy to opt out of, aimed at choices people would endorse. Sneaking patterns are the inverse, sometimes called "sludge" (a word Thaler later popularized for friction that works against people's interests). They rely on the fact that defaults stick and that skimmers read the first two words of a sentence.

Sneak into basket and preselection

An add-on ("Priority support, ₹199") is added to the cart or ticked by default, so the person has to notice and remove it. The default effect is powerful: most people keep whatever is preselected, partly from inertia and partly because a default looks like a recommendation. The EU's Consumer Rights Directive (2011) already bans pre-ticked boxes for extra payments, and the Court of Justice of the EU ruled in the Planet49 case (2019) that a pre-ticked box is not valid consent for cookies. The honest version: add-ons start unticked, and the total updates visibly when someone ticks one.

Hidden costs (drip pricing)

The advertised price is lower than the price you pay: a "convenience fee", "platform fee", tax or delivery charge appears only at the last step. By then, the person has invested time (sunk cost) and committed mentally to the purchase, so most continue. India's 2023 dark pattern guidelines call this drip pricing. In the US, the FTC finalized a rule in late 2024 requiring all-in prices for live-event tickets and short-term lodging. The honest version shows the total, including mandatory fees, from the first price the person sees.

Disguised ads

An advertisement styled to look like content or like part of the interface: a big green "Download" button on a file site that is actually an ad, a "recommended article" that is paid placement without a label. It exploits the learned meaning of interface elements. For you, the risk is subtler: cross-promotion of your own other app styled as a native feature, or affiliate links in a "recommendations" list without disclosure. The honest version labels ads and sponsorship clearly, in words ("Ad", "Sponsored", "Affiliate link"), and never styles them as controls.

Trick questions

A question worded so that a skimmer gives the opposite answer to the one they intend: double negatives, a switch between "tick to opt in" and "tick to opt out" in the same form, or a dropdown where the "no" option is hidden among normal ones. Brignull's best-known example is an airline checkout where the way to decline travel insurance was the option "Don't insure me", placed alphabetically inside a list of countries of residence. The honest version: one question, one meaning, phrased positively, with the same polarity for every checkbox.

Key idea

The whole deal, up front, in words a skimmer reads correctly. Anything added later or worded to be misread is sneaking.

Do

  • Start every optional add-on and marketing choice unticked.
  • Show all-in prices from the first view; offer a breakdown on demand.
  • Label ads, sponsorships and affiliate links in plain words.
  • Phrase every checkbox so that "checked means yes".

Don't

  • Add anything to a cart that the person did not add.
  • Introduce a fee on the last screen.
  • Style promotions as buttons, system alerts or content.
  • Mix opt-in and opt-out wording in one form.

Patterns of obstruction

Taught by Harry Brignull, with the US FTC and the Norwegian Consumer Council

Obstruction makes the choice the business dislikes harder than the one it likes: one tap to join, a maze to leave; one tap to share, five screens to keep things private. The honest rule is symmetry: leaving, refusing and deleting should take no more effort than joining, accepting and creating.

Effort is a cost people pay in time and attention. When one option is cheap and the other is expensive, most people take the cheap one, even when they wanted the other. That is not a character flaw; it is how a limited attention budget works. Obstruction patterns turn that fact into revenue. They are also, today, the patterns most likely to get a company sued.

Roach motel (hard to cancel) and forced continuity

The name comes from an old US insect-trap advertising slogan: easy to get in, impossible to get out. Signing up takes a minute online; cancelling requires a phone call, a chat with a "retention specialist", a buried link under Help, a survey, three offers and a final confirmation email. Its partner is forced continuity: a free trial that silently becomes a paid subscription, with no reminder before the first charge.

In 2018 the Norwegian Consumer Council published "Deceived by Design", analyzing how large platforms steered users through privacy settings. In 2021 it followed with "You can log out, but you can never leave", documenting the many steps needed to cancel Amazon Prime. The US Federal Trade Commission sued Amazon in 2023 over Prime sign-up and cancellation flows; in September 2025 Amazon agreed to a settlement reported at $2.5 billion, including refunds to customers. Separately, the FTC adopted a "click-to-cancel" rule in 2024 requiring cancellation to be as easy as sign-up, but a federal appeals court vacated it in July 2025 on procedural grounds before it took effect, and the FTC has since reopened rulemaking on subscriptions with an advance notice of proposed rulemaking. Check the current status before you rely on it. Existing laws (like the US ROSCA act and several state laws, such as California's automatic renewal law) still require simple cancellation. Treat symmetry as the standard regardless of what any single rule says this year.

Obstruction in general

Cancellation is the famous case, but obstruction appears anywhere a business prefers one answer: deleting an account (no button, "email support"), exporting your data (only one export per 30 days, in a proprietary format), turning off personalization (hidden four levels deep), comparing plans (prices only shown after a demo call; this is sometimes called comparison prevention). The tell is always the same asymmetry.

Interface interference

Visual design used to hide or demote the option the business dislikes: the "Reject" button in light gray on white, the "Decline" link at 11 px under a 48 px green "Accept", the "Delete account" text styled as plain body copy. It works because people navigate by salience (see Visual Design on hierarchy). Hierarchy itself is not wrong: there is usually a recommended action. The line is crossed when the demoted option is a legitimate choice the person came to make, and when it is made hard to see, not just secondary.

Privacy zuckering

Brignull's old name (now retired on his site) for tricking people into sharing more personal data than they meant to: confusing privacy settings, "find your friends" flows that upload the whole address book and email every contact, defaults that make a profile public. In 2015 LinkedIn agreed to a $13 million settlement in a US class action over its "Add Connections" feature sending repeated invitation emails to users' contacts. The honest version: share nothing by default, ask for the narrowest data needed, and show exactly who will see or receive what before it happens. Part 3 goes deeper into privacy.

Key idea

Symmetry: no is as easy as yes, leaving as easy as joining, private as easy as public.

Do

  • Put "Cancel subscription" in the same place a person would look to manage it, reachable in two or three taps.
  • Email a reminder before a trial converts or an annual plan renews.
  • Allow one honest save attempt at most (a pause option or a plan change), shown alongside "Cancel", not instead of it.
  • Make delete account and export data self-serve.

Don't

  • Require a phone call, chat or email to cancel something bought online.
  • Put "Cancel" behind a mandatory survey or several offers.
  • Use visual weight to make a legitimate option invisible.
  • Share or upload anything about other people (contacts) without explicit, specific action.

The law, in one page

You are not a lawyer and this is not legal advice. But a designer who ships to the world should know the shape of the rules, because they increasingly name specific interface patterns. The direction of travel is clear and global: regulators now judge the design, not only the terms.

GDPR (applies since 2018) requires consent to be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, and says withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving it. Pre-ticked boxes and "by continuing you agree" do not count. Data protection by design and by default (Article 25 GDPR) requires you to build privacy in, not bolt it on. The Digital Services Act (Article 25) prohibits online platforms from designing interfaces that deceive or manipulate users or otherwise materially distort their ability to make free and informed decisions; it applies to online platforms since February 2024 (micro and small enterprises are exempt from this particular article, but general consumer law still applies to them). The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive also covers misleading and aggressive practices like fake urgency. French regulator CNIL fined Google and Facebook in January 2022 (€150M and €60M) because refusing cookies took more clicks than accepting them.

The FTC Act (Section 5) prohibits unfair or deceptive practices, and the FTC has used it against dark patterns directly; its 2022 staff report "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" lays out its view. ROSCA (2010) requires clear disclosure, express consent and a simple cancellation method for online subscriptions. In the Epic Games case (orders finalized 2023), Epic agreed to pay $245 million in refunds over Fortnite purchase flows the FTC described as dark patterns. The 2024 click-to-cancel amendments were vacated by a federal appeals court in July 2025; the FTC has since reopened rulemaking. The 2024 fake reviews rule is in effect. States add their own: California's privacy law (CPRA) says agreement obtained through dark patterns is not consent, and its automatic renewal law requires online cancellation.

India's Central Consumer Protection Authority notified the Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023 under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, in November 2023. They list 13 specified dark patterns: false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm shaming, forced action, subscription trap, interface interference, bait and switch, drip pricing, disguised advertisement, nagging, trick question, SaaS billing, and rogue malwares. They apply to platforms offering goods or services in India, as well as advertisers and sellers. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 adds consent requirements for personal data: consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, with a clear affirmative action, and withdrawing must be as easy as giving it.

Calm technology: center and periphery

Taught by Mark Weiser & John Seely Brown, with Natalie Jeremijenko

Good technology lives mostly in the periphery of attention, where you can sense it without thinking about it, and moves to the center only when it matters, then back again. Calm is not the absence of information; it is information that does not demand.

Mark Weiser was chief technologist at Xerox PARC and coined "ubiquitous computing" (his 1991 Scientific American article "The Computer for the 21st Century" opens with the idea that the most profound technologies are those that disappear into everyday life). In 1995 he and John Seely Brown wrote a short paper, "Designing Calm Technology", arguing that if computers were going to be everywhere, they had better not shout. Their central point was a distinction between the center of attention (what you are consciously focused on) and the periphery (what you are aware of without attending to it, like the sound of traffic or the weather out of the corner of your eye).

They argued that calm technology does two things. It uses the periphery, so we can be aware of many things without being overburdened; and it lets things move easily between periphery and center, so we can take control when we need to. They wrote that a calm technology will move easily from the periphery of our attention, to the center, and back. The feeling this creates, they said, is of being "at home": attuned to what is around you, in control.

The Dangling String

Weiser and Brown's favorite example was an artwork by Natalie Jeremijenko at PARC: a length of plastic spaghetti-like string hanging from a small motor in the ceiling, wired to the office Ethernet. Each packet of network traffic made the motor twitch. A quiet network meant a gentle twitch every few seconds; a busy network made the string whirl and make a soft noise. Nobody needed to look at it. You heard and saw it at the edge of awareness, and you noticed when something was unusual. Information about the network was present, continuous and undemanding. That is the model: ambient, continuous, glanceable, and silent until it matters.

Translating it to screens

On a screen, the periphery is the edges: the status bar, the corner of a window, the color of a small dot, the length of a scroll bar, a gentle change in a title's weight. The center is anything that interrupts: modals, alerts, sounds, toasts that block content, badges demanding to be cleared. Calm software puts status in the edges and keeps the center for the person's own work.

  • Escalate in steps: dot, then inline message, then (only for real risk) a modal.

Don't

  • Use red badges for counts that do not represent obligations.
  • Animate peripheral items; motion pulls them into the center.

For the solo creator

Common mistakes

Designers remove status entirely to look minimal.

Define levels (periphery, inline, interrupt) and map each event to one.

Amber Case's eight principles

Taught by Amber Case, with Mark Weiser

Amber Case turned Weiser and Brown's idea into eight working principles. Each one is a question you can ask of any feature: how little attention can this need, and what happens when it fails?

Amber Case is a designer and researcher who wrote Calm Technology: Principles and Patterns for Non-Intrusive Design (O'Reilly, 2015). She frames technology as something that should inform without overburdening, and her principles are deliberately practical. Here they are, each with a software example drawn below. Read the list, then study the drawings: each shows one concrete way to apply the principle in a small app.

  1. Technology should require the smallest possible amount of attention. It should create ambient awareness through different senses and communicate without taking you out of your world.
  2. Technology should inform and create calm. Its job is to give you what you need to know so you can stop worrying, not to give you something new to worry about.
  3. Technology should make use of the periphery. It should move easily from the periphery to the center and back.
  4. Technology should amplify the best of technology and the best of humanity. Machines should not act like humans, and humans should not act like machines; give each the work it is good at.
  5. Technology can communicate, but doesn't need to speak. A tone, a light, a vibration can carry status without words or a voice.
  6. Technology should work even when it fails. A broken escalator becomes stairs. Design a sensible default state for failure.
  7. The right amount of technology is the minimum needed to solve the problem. Every added feature, sensor or connection adds a way to fail and a demand on attention.
  8. Technology should respect social norms. Introduce new behavior gradually, and consider the people around the user, not only the user.

How to use the eight as a review

The principles overlap, which is fine: use them as eight lenses during a review, not as a scoring rubric. In practice, three of them do most of the work for a small software product. Principle 6 (work when it fails) forces you to design offline, error and empty states, which most apps skip. Principle 7 (minimum technology) is your best defense against feature creep. Principle 8 (social norms) is the one most often forgotten: the person next to your user (on a call, on a train, in a shared document) also experiences your product.

Emptiness: leaving room

Taught by Kenya Hara, with Naoto Fukasawa and Oliver Reichenstein

Emptiness is not a lack. An empty vessel is useful because it can receive anything. A calm product leaves room for the person's own content, intention and pace, instead of filling every gap with suggestions, prompts and promotion.

Kenya Hara, art director of MUJI since 2002, writes about emptiness as a design idea in Designing Design (2007) and White (2010). He distinguishes simplicity in the Western modernist sense (reducing to essentials for efficiency) from emptiness in a Japanese sense: a space that invites the viewer to bring their own meaning. His image is a vessel: its value comes from the empty space inside. MUJI's posters with a single horizon line and a tiny figure are, in his framing, empty containers that viewers fill with their own feelings.

Hara also describes MUJI's aim with a contrast between two Japanese phrases. "Kore ga ii" means roughly "this is what I want", the feeling of a product that excites a specific desire. "Kore de ii" means roughly "this will do", a quieter satisfaction: the product is good enough that you do not need to think about it further. MUJI aims for a confident version of the second. For software this is a surprisingly radical idea: build a product that people can stop thinking about.

Emptiness in software

The baseline guide covered whitespace as a visual tool (see also Visual Design). Here we care about emptiness as an ethical stance. An attention-maximizing product treats every empty moment as wasted inventory: an empty feed gets "suggested posts", an empty search gets "trending", a finished task gets "what's next?". A calm product treats empty moments as the user's to keep.

The empty vessel is useful precisely because it can hold anything; design that leaves room lets people bring their own meaning.Paraphrasing Kenya Hara, Designing Design, 2007

Four kinds of room to leave

  1. Room in space. Empty canvas, generous margins, no promotional slot "because there was space".
  2. Room in time. No autoplay, no "next" timer, no immediate follow-up prompt after completion.
  3. Room in meaning. Let people name, organize and use things their own way; avoid forcing your categories (Fukasawa calls designs that fit unconscious behavior "without thought").
  4. Room in commitment. Allow light use. Someone who opens your app once a month should not be punished with guilt, lost streaks or "we miss you" emails.

Do

  • Design the "done" state as a real ending.
  • Let empty states be quiet invitations with one hint at most.
  • Support occasional use without penalties.

Don't

  • Fill every empty slot with suggestions or upsells.
  • Treat a user's free moment as an opportunity to increase engagement.
  • Confuse emptiness with missing information; the essential status still shows, quietly.

  • MUJIRyohin Keikaku, art direction by Kenya Hara since 2002

  • iA WriterInformation Architects
    Opens to a blank page and a cursor; no templates gallery, no onboarding tour. The empty page is the feature.
  • Things 3 "Today"Cultured Code
    When you clear today's list, the view is simply empty and quiet, not filled with suggestions. Done means done.
For the solo creator

Re-engagement emails and streak resets feel like retention tools.

Let people leave and return without penalty. A light user who comes back gladly is worth more than a guilt-driven daily user.

The attention economy and stopping cues

Taught by Tristan Harris and the Center for Humane Technology, with Adam Alter and Natasha Dow Schüll

When a business earns more the longer people stay, the product slowly learns to remove the moments when people would naturally stop. Calm design puts those stopping points back.

In 2013 Tristan Harris, then a design ethicist at Google, circulated an internal slide deck titled "A Call to Minimize Distraction & Respect Users' Attention". He argued that a handful of designers at a few companies were shaping how billions of people spend their time, mostly by optimizing for time-on-site. The idea grew into the "Time Well Spent" movement and, in 2018, the Center for Humane Technology, which he co-founded with Aza Raskin and Randima Fernando. Its argument is not that technology is bad, but that a business model built on capturing attention creates pressure to exploit human weaknesses.

How products remove stopping cues

Adam Alter, in Irresistible (2017), uses the phrase stopping cues for the natural signals that tell us we are done: the end of a chapter, the last page of a newspaper, the end of an episode. Many attention-optimized designs work by removing them. Natasha Dow Schüll's study of slot machines, Addiction by Design (2012), shows the same thing in gambling: machines are designed to keep players in a continuous "zone" with no natural pause.

Habits that serve: the "glad they did it" test

Taught by Nir Eyal and his critics, with BJ Fogg and James Clear

Habit-forming design is a tool, not a sin. The question is whose goal the habit serves. The test: a week later, would the person be glad they did it, and would they still choose it if they saw exactly how you designed it?

Nir Eyal's Hooked (2014) describes a four-step loop that many popular products share: a trigger (external, like a notification, then internal, like boredom or loneliness), an action (the simplest behavior done in anticipation of reward), a variable reward (of the tribe, the hunt, or the self), and an investment (the user puts something in, like data, content or followers, that makes the next loop more likely). The model drew on BJ Fogg's behavior model, which says a behavior happens when motivation, ability and a prompt converge at the same moment.

The book was widely used by startups, and widely criticized. Critics argued that it gave product teams a recipe for compulsion, especially the variable reward step, which borrows from the reinforcement schedules that make slot machines compelling. Eyal himself included an ethics section, the Manipulation Matrix, and later wrote Indistractable (2019), about how individuals can control their attention. That second book drew its own criticism: some argued it shifts responsibility for distraction from companies to users. You do not need to settle the debate to use its best part, which is the matrix.

The Manipulation Matrix

Eyal asks two questions of any habit-forming product: Would I use it myself? and Will it help users materially improve their lives? The four answers give four roles.

The streak debate, as a worked example

Duolingo is the best-known example of streaks in a useful product. A streak counts consecutive days of practice; the app reminds you to keep it and lets you buy or earn "streak freezes" to skip a day without losing it. Duolingo has said publicly that streaks are one of its most effective features for retention. For a language learner, daily practice genuinely helps, so the habit serves a real goal. Critics point at the edge cases: people doing a 30-second lesson at 11:58 pm just to save the number, anxiety about losing a 400-day streak, notifications with guilt-laden copy. The number, at some point, becomes the goal instead of the learning.

Work through it with the glad test. Would the person be glad, a week later, that they did today's lesson? Mostly yes. Would they be glad they did a token lesson at midnight purely to save the count? Probably not; that is the streak serving itself. So an ethical streak design would: reward real practice (not the minimum tap), forgive misses (rest days, weekly goals), keep reminder copy factual rather than guilt-based, and let people turn streaks off without losing their learning history.

Four questions for any habit feature

  1. Whose goal is it? Did the user state this goal, or did you choose it for them?
  2. Is the reward the value itself? Learning, a finished draft, a cleaner inbox, or a random hit of novelty?
  3. Can they skip without punishment? Rest days, pause, forgiveness.
  4. Can they leave with their investment? Export of history, notes, data.
Key idea

A good habit feature helps people do what they already wanted to do, more easily. A bad one makes them want what you wanted them to want.

Do

  • Let users set their own goals, times and frequency.
  • Use weekly or flexible targets instead of unbroken daily chains.
  • Reward meaningful completion, not the minimum tap.
  • Write reminders as facts, not threats.

Don't

  • Use loss framing ("your streak will die") in notifications.
  • Randomize rewards to make checking compulsive.
  • Lock a person's history behind continued use.

In the wild

  • Duolingo streaksDuolingo
    Daily streak with freezes, reminders, and a famously persistent owl mascot. Habit design that supports a real goal, with edges worth debating.
  • Apple Fitness ringsApple Watch, 2015–
    Daily ring goals the user can adjust; in later versions Apple added the ability to pause rings for rest or illness. A forgiving mechanism added after years of rigid streaks.
  • Tiny HabitsBJ Fogg, 2019
    Fogg's method anchors tiny behaviors to existing routines and celebrates them; it is aimed at the person designing their own habits. Habit design can be done with the user, not to them.
For the solo creator

Write your product's "glad sentence": "A week later, my user is glad they ___." Every habit feature (streaks, reminders, badges, daily goals) must make that sentence more true. If a feature raises your retention chart but does not touch the sentence, cut it.

Retention case studies are everywhere.

Streaks fit products where daily practice is the value (language, exercise). For a notes or reading tool, a streak often adds guilt and nothing else.

No exit from the habit

You never designed "turn this off".

Every habit mechanic needs an off switch that keeps the user's history intact.

A notification policy for your app

Taught by Amber Case, with Apple's and Google's platform guidelines

A notification spends someone else's attention. Decide in writing which events deserve that spend, at what level, at what time, and how often, before you write a single push. Then let the user tighten it further.

Every notification is an interruption, and interruptions have a cost beyond the seconds it takes to read them: the person loses their place in whatever they were doing and has to rebuild it. A notification that is useful pays that cost back. One that is promotional, vague or badly timed does not, and the person learns a lesson: this app's pings are noise. After enough of those they turn notifications off entirely, and now your genuinely important messages cannot reach them either. Notification abuse destroys the channel.

Step 1: classify every event

Before designing any push, list every event your system could notify about and put each in one of three classes. Be strict.

Batching combines several useful events into one ("3 comments on 'Draft 2'") instead of three pings. A digest delivers all useful events at a predictable time the user chooses. Privacy by design

Taught by Ann Cavoukian, with the GDPR and Apple's and Signal's practice

The most private data is data you never collect. Collect the minimum, keep it for the shortest time, process it on the device when you can, explain it in plain words, and let people take it with them or delete it themselves.

Ann Cavoukian, then Information and Privacy Commissioner of Ontario, developed "Privacy by Design" in the 1990s and published its seven foundational principles in 2009. Among them: be proactive, not reactive; make privacy the default setting; embed it into the design; aim for full functionality (positive-sum, not privacy versus features); protect data through its whole life cycle; be visible and transparent; and keep it user-centric. The EU's GDPR made a version of this law in 2018 as "data protection by design and by default", alongside the principle of data minimization: collect only what is adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary for the purpose.

Why should a solo creator care beyond compliance? Because every field of personal data you store is a liability: something to secure, back up, disclose in a breach, answer requests about, and explain. Data you never collect cannot leak. Minimization is the rare principle that is both more ethical and less work.

The data ladder

Clear permissions and honest sign-in

Ask for system permissions (contacts, location, photos, camera) only when a feature needs them, and ask for the narrowest version: "selected photos" rather than the whole library, "while using the app" rather than "always", approximate rather than precise location when that is enough. Both iOS and Android now offer these narrower choices; design your feature so it works with them. For sign-in, offer options that do not force more data than needed: email with a magic link, Sign in with Apple (which lets people hide their real email behind a relay address), or no account at all for a local app. Do not demand a phone number or social login to use a notes app.

Consent banners: the classic battleground

Cookie banners are where privacy and deceptive design meet most visibly. The honest move for most small products is to not need one: use privacy-friendly, cookieless analytics (or none), no ad trackers, and no third-party embeds that set cookies. Local-first: you own your data

Taught by Ink & Switch (Martin Kleppmann, Adam Wiggins, Peter van Hardenberg, Mark McGranaghan)

In local-first software the primary copy of your data lives on your own device, and the cloud is a helper for sync and backup, not the owner. It is the strongest structural form of respect: the app stays fast, works offline, and keeps working even if the company disappears.

In 2019 the research lab Ink & Switch published an essay, "Local-first software: You own your data, in spite of the cloud". The authors observed that cloud apps (like Google Docs) made collaboration easy but took away ownership: if the service goes down, changes its terms or shuts down, your work goes with it. Old-fashioned desktop apps with files gave you ownership but made collaboration hard. They proposed a goal that combines both, and described it with seven ideals.

#Ideal (Ink & Switch, 2019)What it means for your design
1No spinners: your work at your fingertipsReads and writes hit local storage; the UI never waits on the network to show your own data.
2Your work is not trapped on one deviceSync across devices is a feature, not an afterthought.
3The network is optionalFull editing offline; sync when connected.
4Seamless collaboration with your colleaguesConcurrent edits merge (for example with CRDTs) instead of producing conflicts.
5The Long NowData stays readable for decades, independent of your company.
6Security and privacy by defaultEnd-to-end encryption is natural when servers only relay.
7You retain ultimate ownership and controlUsers can copy, export, modify and delete without asking permission.

You don't have to go all the way

Full local-first with real-time collaboration is still hard engineering (libraries such as Automerge, from Ink & Switch, and Yjs help). But you can take most of the benefit with simpler moves: store user documents as plain files the user can see (Markdown, JSON, SQLite); cache everything locally and render from cache; queue writes offline; sync files through a service the user already owns (iCloud Drive, Dropbox); and make export a first-class feature. Each move climbs toward ownership.

Do

  • Render user data from local storage first, always.
  • Prefer formats users can open without you: Markdown, plain text, CSV, JSON, SQLite, standard image and audio formats.
  • Design an explicit offline experience (Chapter 8, principle 6).
  • Write down what happens to user data if you stop working on the product.

Don't

  • Gate reading your own data behind a network request.
  • Invent a proprietary format when an open one fits.
  • Promise "you own your data" while offering no export.

In the wild

  • ObsidianDynalist Inc., 2020–
    Notes are a folder of Markdown files on your disk; sync is an optional paid service, and you can use your own. The file system as a promise: you can leave any day with everything.
  • iA WriterInformation Architects
    Works on plain text Markdown files in folders you choose, synced through services like iCloud or Dropbox. A writing app that does not own your writing.
  • Actual BudgetOpen source, local-first
    A personal budgeting app built local-first, with optional sync and the option to self-host. Local-first works for serious, everyday tools, not only research demos.
For the solo creator

Local-first is also operationally kind to you. Fewer server round-trips means fewer outages to wake up for, lower hosting bills, and a product that keeps working while you are on holiday. If you are starting a new tool for individuals, start from files or SQLite on the device and add sync later.

Common mistakes

"Offline mode" as a read-only cache

Write sync is harder than read caching.

Notes are Markdown, but tags, links and attachments live only in your database.

Store metadata in the files (front matter) or include it in export.

Honesty in everyday interface

Taught by Dieter Rams, with Brad Myers and indie practice

Trust is built in small, repeated moments: a progress bar that tells the truth, a price that does not change, a change log that admits what broke, a feature retired with notice and an export. None of these are hard. All of them are noticed.

Honest progress

Brad Myers showed in 1985 that people prefer having a progress indicator during long waits. Progress bars are among the most common "small lies" in software: a bar that races to 90% and then sits there, a fake multi-step "Analyzing your profile…" animation for something that took 50 ms, a spinner with no end. Some deliberate slowdowns are defended as making a result feel more valuable ("labor illusion"). The honest line: a waiting indicator should reflect real work. If you know the steps, show them. If you do not know the duration, say what is happening instead of faking a percentage. If the work is instant, show the result instantly.

Fair pricing and easy cancellation

Chapter 5 covered cancellation. Fair pricing is its twin: prices that are easy to compare, renewal terms visible before purchase, reminders before renewal, no surprise price rises (announce them in advance and honor the old price until renewal at least), and a plain refund policy. Many indie products also offer a lifetime or one-time purchase option, or "pay once for this version" models, which remove subscription anxiety entirely (see Identity & Launch for pricing pages).

Transparent changes: changelogs

A changelog is a trust device. It shows the product is alive, admits fixes ("Fixed a bug where search skipped notes with emoji titles"), and warns of changes before they surprise people. Good changelogs are dated, specific, written for users not developers, and honest about removals and regressions. Bad ones say "Bug fixes and performance improvements" every time, which tells the user nothing and, over the years, reads as concealment.

Sunsetting features (and products) responsibly

Every product eventually removes things. How you do it says everything about you. Google Reader's shutdown in 2013 is the classic case: announced in March, closed on 1 July, with an export through Google Takeout. People were upset, but they could leave with their subscriptions in a standard format (OPML), which is why a new generation of RSS readers could import them. A responsible sunset has five parts.

  1. Notice. Announce early (months, not days), in the app and by email, with a date.
  2. Reason. Say why, briefly and truthfully.
  3. Export. Provide a full export in an open format, and keep it working until after the shutdown date.
  4. Alternatives. Point to replacements, including competitors if needed.
  5. Money. Refund unused prepaid time, pro rata.

Do

  • Drive progress indicators from real work, and name the step.
  • Announce price rises in advance; honor the old price until renewal.
  • Write specific, dated changelogs, including removals and regressions.
  • Plan sunsets with notice, reason, export, alternatives, refunds.

Don't

  • Fake progress or add theatrical delays.
  • Quietly remove or paywall features.
  • Write "bug fixes and improvements" for a release that changes behavior.
  • Shut down without an export.

Longevity: software that lasts

Taught by Dieter Rams, with Donald Knuth, Low-tech Magazine and the Sustainable Web Design community

Long-lasting design avoids fashion, keeps people's work readable for decades, runs on the devices they already own, and wastes neither energy nor attention. Maintenance is a form of respect: it says "I am still here, and your trust is still safe."

Rams's seventh principle says good design is long-lasting: it avoids being fashionable and therefore never appears antiquated. His ninth says good design is environmentally friendly: it conserves resources and minimizes physical and visual pollution through the product's life. Braun products he designed in the 1960s still look current and many still work. Software decays faster, but not by necessity. Most software rot comes from three choices: trendy styling, closed formats, and growing weight that pushes older devices out.

1 · Files that outlive the app

The single most important longevity decision is the format of the user's work. A plain text file from 1975 opens today on any computer. A document in a proprietary format from a dead app from 2010 may not open anywhere. Open, documented, widely implemented formats outlive companies.

2 · Style that does not date

Trends in UI come in waves: heavy skeuomorphism, then extreme flat, then neumorphism, glassmorphism, gradient blobs, and whatever is fashionable this year. Each looks fresh for a year or two and then marks the product as "from that year". Timeless interfaces lean on typography, spacing, clear hierarchy and system conventions rather than surface effects. You can still have personality, but put it where it ages well: in words, in a well-chosen typeface, in a single accent color, in thoughtful details.

3 · Light pages and the energy of software

Digital products use energy: in data centers, networks and especially in the devices that download and run them. Heavier pages and apps cost energy, data plans (a real expense for many users in India and elsewhere) and battery, and they push older devices out sooner. The HTTP Archive's measurements show the median web page has grown to over 2 MB. A text-centric product rarely needs more than a fraction of that. Practical moves: compress and size images, avoid autoplaying video, use system fonts or a small number of subsetted webfonts, ship less JavaScript, cache well, and lazy-load what is below the fold.

Myth

"Dark mode saves lots of battery." On OLED screens dark pixels do use less power, but a 2021 Purdue University study found the savings at typical indoor brightness were only a few percent, and much larger only at high brightness. Offer dark mode because people like it and it suits low light, not as a big green win. Page weight and CPU work usually matter more.

4 · Support older devices

Every time an app drops support for an older OS or a slower phone, some people are pushed to buy new hardware or lose access. Hardware manufacturing is where much of a device's lifetime carbon footprint comes from, so keeping old devices useful is one of the most effective environmental moves software can make. Check your analytics for the oldest OS versions and slowest devices in real use, test on a cheap or old phone regularly, and drop support only with notice and a reason.

5 · Finished software and maintenance as respect

There is a quiet tradition of software that is allowed to be finished. Donald Knuth froze TeX's features decades ago; new versions only fix bugs, and the version number converges toward π (3.14159265…), a joke that is also a philosophy. Many small Unix tools have done one job unchanged for decades. "Finished" does not mean abandoned: it means feature-complete, with ongoing maintenance (security fixes, OS compatibility, bug fixes). For a solo creator, deciding that your product's core is finished can be the most respectful thing you do: users get stability instead of churn, and you get time to maintain it well.

Key idea

Open formats, timeless style, light weight, old devices welcome, and a product allowed to be finished. That is long-lasting design in software.

Do

  • Store and export user work in open, simple formats.
  • Set a page-weight and bundle-size budget and check it in CI.
  • Test on an old, cheap device every release.
  • Decide what "finished" means for your core and protect it from churn.

Don't

  • Redesign for fashion every year.
  • Ship autoplay video, huge hero images or several webfonts on reading pages.
  • Drop older OS versions just to use a new API you could do without.
  • Rewrite stable features because they are boring to you.

  • Low-tech MagazineSolar-powered website, 2018–
    A site served from a small solar-powered server, with dithered images and a static design to keep pages tiny; it can go offline in long cloudy spells. A vivid demonstration of how small a good reading page can be.
  • TeXDonald Knuth, 1978–
    Feature-frozen for decades, still maintained, with documents from the 1980s still compiling. Finished software, maintained, is a gift to users.
  • CraigslistSince 1995

Safety by design: abuse, moderation and kids

Taught by Eva PenzeyMoog, with Australia's eSafety Commissioner and the UK ICO

Every feature that connects people, shares data or shows location can be used to hurt someone. Design for the abuser as a user persona, make the safe choice the default, and give the person at risk the tools to see and stop what is happening.

Eva PenzeyMoog's Design for Safety (2021) starts from a hard fact: technology is routinely used in domestic abuse, through shared accounts, location sharing, smart home devices, shared calendars, and access to someone's phone. Most product teams design only for well-meaning users and treat abuse as an edge case. PenzeyMoog's method is to add an abuser persona and a survivor persona to your design process and walk every feature through their eyes. Australia's eSafety Commissioner promotes a similar "Safety by Design" approach built on three principles: service provider responsibility, user empowerment and autonomy, and transparency and accountability.

Abuse vectors in ordinary features

FeatureHow it can be abusedSafer design

Invites and shared documentsSpam, or regaining access after a breakup.Owner can see and revoke all access; access logs; notify when someone is added.

Moderation for small apps

If your product lets people send anything to other people (comments, messages, shared links, public profiles), you are running a small moderation operation whether you planned to or not. Big platforms have teams; you have yourself. So design to reduce how much moderation you need. Prefer private and invite-only spaces over public ones. Slow down new accounts (rate limits on messages and invites). Make block and report one tap away, and make block mean the blocked person cannot see or contact the blocker. Write short, plain community rules. Keep a simple review queue, and decide in advance how you will handle illegal content (in many countries you must act on reports and, for some content like child sexual abuse material, report it to authorities). Consider not building the social feature at all: a sharing link that the user sends through their own messaging app avoids an entire category of harm.

Children

Children are in your audience if your product is likely to be used by them, even if it is "not for kids". The UK's Age Appropriate Design Code (the "Children's code", in force since 2021) sets 15 standards for online services likely to be accessed by children, including high-privacy defaults, location off by default, no nudge techniques that encourage children to weaken privacy, and minimal data collection. In the US, COPPA requires verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information from children under 13. India's DPDP Act, 2023 treats anyone under 18 as a child and requires verifiable parental consent for processing their data, and bars tracking and targeted advertising directed at children. The design response is the same everywhere: high-privacy defaults for everyone, no dark patterns, no public profiles by default, and no engagement mechanics designed to keep young people hooked.

Do

  • Add abuser and person-at-risk personas to every feature that shares, connects or locates.
  • Make sharing visible, time-limited and revocable.
  • Ship block, mute and report with any feature that lets people reach each other.
  • Default to high privacy for everyone, which also protects children.

Don't

  • Treat abuse as an edge case to handle later.
  • Build public social features you cannot moderate.
  • Let one account holder silently see another member's private data.
  • Use engagement mechanics in products likely to be used by children.

For the solo creator

Your best safety tool is scope. Every social, public or location feature you skip is a moderation and safety burden you never carry. When you do add one, add the safety controls in the same release, not "later". And write down, now, what you will do the first time someone reports abuse: who you contact, what you remove, how fast.

Common mistakes

Block that doesn't really block

It hides the blocked person's content from you but still lets them see and contact you.

Make blocking two-way for contact and visibility, and do not notify the blocked person.

Public by default

It helps growth and discovery.

Private by default; let people choose to publish specific things.

Account recovery as an attack

Recovery by "answering a question" or a shared email lets a former partner take over.

Notify on recovery attempts, show active sessions, let people sign out other devices.

Ethics for one: your oath and your checklist

Taught by Mike Monteiro, with Dieter Rams and everything in this guide

When you are the designer, the engineer and the business, nobody else will stand up for the user in the room, because the room is your head. Write your principles down while you are calm, turn them into a checklist, and run it before every release.

Mike Monteiro, a designer and co-founder of the studio Mule Design, has argued for years that designers are responsible for the work they put into the world, and that "I was just doing my job" is not a defense. His 2013 talk "How Designers Destroyed the World" and his 2019 book Ruined by Design make the case that design decisions have consequences at scale, and he published "A Designer's Code of Ethics" in 2017, which begins from the idea that a designer is first a human being, and only then a professional. In a team, the designer often plays the user's advocate against business pressure. Alone, you play both roles. That is harder than it sounds, because the business side of you always has a number to point at and the user side only has a feeling.

Write your oath

A design oath is a short list of commitments specific enough to decide real cases. "Be ethical" decides nothing. "Cancelling takes no more taps than subscribing" decides a lot. Write seven to ten lines, each a rule you could check. Here is an example drawn from this guide; adapt the wording, keep the specificity.

MY DESIGN OATH (Rohit, v1)
1. The first price a person sees is the price they pay.
2. Saying no, leaving and deleting take no more effort than
   saying yes, joining and creating.
3. Nothing optional is pre-selected.
4. I never show numbers, deadlines or reviews that are not true.
5. Pushes are for things a person asked for. Promotion never is.
6. Every list has an end. Every session can finish.
7. I collect the minimum, keep it short, and let people export
   and delete it themselves.
8. People's work lives in open formats they can open without me.
9. I walk every sharing feature through an abuser's eyes.
10. If I shut down, I give notice, export and refunds.

Saying no to features that harm

Some requests will come from users ("let me see when my partner reads my messages"), some from your own growth instincts ("import contacts to grow"), some from partners ("add our SDK"). For each, look for the legitimate need under the request, and design for that need without the harm. Often there is a version that gets most of the business value honestly.

Three habits that keep you honest

  1. The pre-ship checklist. Run the checklist below before every release that touches money, data, notifications or sharing. It takes ten minutes.
  2. The decision log. When you choose between business and user interests, write one paragraph: the options, what you chose and why. Re-read it after a month. It is surprisingly good at revealing rationalizations.
  3. The stranger test. Before shipping a persuasive element, show it to someone outside tech and ask "what do you think happens if you tap this?". If their answer differs from reality, you have a gap.

Wrap-up

Ten things to remember

  1. Honesty is the size of the gap between what a busy person believes and what really happens.
  2. Deceptive patterns exploit six shortcuts: defaults, scarcity, social proof, shame, salience and effort. Neutralize the shortcut, not just the name.
  3. Persuasion is honest when the fact behind it is true, specific and the same for everyone.
  4. Symmetry: no as easy as yes, leaving as easy as joining, private as easy as public.
  5. The law in the EU, US and India now names many of these patterns. Design to the honest version and you mostly comply.
  6. Calm technology keeps status in the periphery and brings things to the center only when a decision is needed.
  7. Leave room: empty moments and endings belong to the user. Every list needs a stopping cue.
  8. A habit feature is good when, a week later, the person is glad they did it.
  9. Collect the minimum, keep data on the device and in open formats, and let people export and delete it themselves.
  10. Write your oath while calm, walk features through an abuser's eyes, and run the pre-ship checklist every release.
