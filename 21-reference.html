Part one

People and lineage

Design is a conversation that has been running for a century. Knowing who said what, and who they were answering, keeps you from rediscovering old ideas badly and helps you read new ones critically. These four chapters give you a timeline, a family tree, a directory of about a hundred people, and the ten ideas they keep repeating.

1

A timeline of product design, 1945–2026

Drawn from the history taught across the course

Most of what you use daily was invented decades before you met it: the mouse in the 1960s, windows in the 1970s, the grid in the 1950s, the core usability rules by the early 1990s.

The timeline has three lanes. The top lane is objects, graphics and type: the physical and visual tradition that gave software its sense of order. The middle lane is interaction and software: the machines and products themselves. The bottom lane is ideas, books and standards: the moments when someone wrote down what the field had learned.

Read it in three ways. First, look for clusters. The years 1968 to 1984 hold almost the whole personal computer, from Engelbart's demo to the Macintosh. The years 2007 to 2014 hold almost the whole mobile era, from the iPhone to Material Design. Second, look for gaps between lanes: ideas often arrive long before products (Bush described a linked personal library in 1945; the web arrived in the 1990s). Third, look at the bottom lane and notice how few of the books are recent. The fundamentals settled early. What changes is the material.

Five turning points worth knowing in detail

  1. 1968, the Mother of All Demos. Douglas Engelbart showed the mouse, hypertext links, shared screens and live collaborative editing in one 90-minute presentation in San Francisco. Much of the next fifty years was spent shipping what he showed.
  2. 1973 to 1981, Xerox PARC. The Alto and then the Star brought windows, icons, menus, a pointer and WYSIWYG editing together. Larry Tesler's work on modeless editing and cut, copy and paste came from here.
  3. 1984, the Macintosh. The graphical interface became a product people could buy. Susan Kare's icons and Bill Atkinson's QuickDraw and MacPaint set the visual and interaction vocabulary. Apple's Human Interface Guidelines were published as a book in 1987.
  4. 2007, the iPhone. Direct manipulation with a finger, inertial scrolling and rubber-band bounce made the physics of a list part of its meaning. A year later the App Store opened, and a solo developer could reach the world.
  5. 2022, ChatGPT. A conversational interface to a large language model reached a mass audience within weeks. Designers now work with systems whose output is probabilistic, which reopened old questions of feedback, trust and control. See Designing with AI.
Myth

"Flat design began with iOS 7." Swiss graphic design, Otl Aicher's pictograms and Microsoft's Metro design language (Windows Phone 7, 2010) were all flat before iOS 7 in 2013. What iOS 7 did was make flatness the default for a huge software platform, and it was followed by Material Design in 2014, which added depth back as a system of elevation.

Do

  • Read an old source when a new trend cites it. The original is usually more careful.
  • Ask of every new interface idea: which earlier idea is this a version of?
  • Notice which ideas have survived forty years. Those are the safe bets.

Don't

  • Assume the latest platform style is the best one. Styles cycle: skeuomorphic, flat, glassy.
  • Treat history as trivia. It is a library of solved problems.
  • Confuse a launch date with an invention date.

In the wild

  • Apple Liquid GlassApple, 2025
    A translucent material across Apple's platforms, introduced at WWDC 2025. It echoes Aqua (2001) and the depth debates of 2013. Visual styles return in cycles; the principles of legibility and hierarchy do not change.
  • Command palettesSublime Text, VS Code, Linear, Raycast
    Type to find any action. A modern form of the command line and of Raskin's argument for fast, modeless commands. Many "new" patterns are old ideas meeting a new audience.
  • Local-first appsInk & Switch essay, 2019
    Software that keeps your data on your device and syncs, rather than living only on a server. A reaction to cloud-only software, built on decades of work on personal computing.

2

Who learned from whom

A map of seven threads

The field is a few long threads, not a crowd of separate geniuses: functional industrial design, quiet Japanese design, typography and the grid, augmenting human intellect, information graphics, usability science, and calm technology.

Each row of the map is one thread. Solid lines mean a direct link: someone worked with, studied under or took over from someone else. Dashed lines mean a link of ideas: someone cited, admired or extended the earlier work. The map is deliberately incomplete. Its job is to show you that when you study one person, you are joining a conversation, and to point you at the hubs.

Reading the threads

Function and reduction. The Bauhaus taught that form should come from purpose and industrial production. The Ulm School of Design (HfG Ulm) made that systematic, and Braun's collaboration with Ulm teachers shaped Dieter Rams. Jony Ive has spoken openly of admiring Rams. In software, this thread shows up as restraint: fewer controls, honest materials, nothing decorative that does not help. Study it in Breakdowns: Objects.

Quiet Japan. The mingei (folk craft) movement of Soetsu Yanagi valued anonymous, everyday objects. His son Sori Yanagi designed the Butterfly Stool and plain kitchen tools. MUJI, founded in 1980 with Ikko Tanaka as art director, sold "no-brand quality goods"; Kenya Hara took over as art director in 2002 and Naoto Fukasawa joined its advisory board. The lesson for software is emptiness as a receptive space, not as a lack. See MUJI as a system.

Type and grid. Jan Tschichold's New Typography argued for asymmetric, functional layout; Swiss designers such as Josef Müller-Brockmann turned the grid into a discipline; Massimo Vignelli carried it into corporate and transit design. Robert Bringhurst wrote the modern bible of book typography, and Matthew Butterick and Oliver Reichenstein brought that care to screens. See Typography.

Augmenting intellect. Vannevar Bush imagined the memex. Douglas Engelbart, who read Bush, built the first working system for augmenting human intellect. Alan Kay and colleagues at PARC designed personal, graphical computing. The Mac team shipped it. The iPhone team made it physical. Bret Victor keeps asking what this thread forgot.

Information. William Playfair invented the line and bar chart; Charles Minard drew Napoleon's march to Moscow in 1869. Jacques Bertin named the visual variables. Edward Tufte turned this into a design ethic for data. See Information & Data.

Usability science. Gestalt psychologists described how we group what we see. Experimental psychologists measured pointing (Fitts), choosing (Hick) and remembering (Miller). Card, Moran and Newell applied this to computers; Norman and Nielsen turned it into a profession; Krug and Yablonski made it short enough to use daily. See The Human Mind.

Calm technology. Mark Weiser and John Seely Brown at PARC argued that technology should move between the center and periphery of attention. Amber Case turned that into eight practical principles; Ink & Switch argue for local-first software that respects ownership. See Honest & Calm.

Key idea

Study one hub per thread, not twenty names. Rams, Hara, Bringhurst, Engelbart, Tufte, Norman and Weiser will carry you through almost everything else.

For the solo creator

Choose the two threads closest to your product and go deep on them. If you make a reading app, that is type and grid plus calm technology. If you make a data tool, it is information plus usability science. Depth in two threads beats a shallow tour of seven.

Do

  • When you read someone, look up who they cite. Read one of those next.
  • Notice when two threads meet in a product: iA Writer is type and grid plus calm.
  • Keep your own map. Add people as you meet them.

Don't

  • Treat any one person as the source of truth. Every hub had teachers.
  • Copy a thread's visual style without its reasons.
  • Assume influence where there is only resemblance.
Check yourself

You are designing a quiet, text-first journaling app. Which two threads should you study most closely?

A text-first, quiet product lives or dies on typography and on how it treats attention. Usability science always matters, but the specific taste of this product comes from the type and calm threads, and MUJI-style emptiness.

Cognition and usability

The people who turned psychology into rules you can use on a screen.

Don Norman

Cognitive scientist; Apple, then co-founder of Nielsen Norman Group

Gave designers their working vocabulary: affordances, signifiers, mapping, constraints, feedback and conceptual models. His core claim is that when people make errors with a device, the design is usually at fault, not the person.

Study firstThe Design of Everyday Things (1988, revised 2013), chapters 1 and 2.
In this course: Foundations: affordances

Jakob Nielsen

Usability engineer; co-founder of Nielsen Norman Group

Made usability cheap and routine. His ten usability heuristics (first published with Rolf Molich in 1990, refined in 1994) remain the most used checklist in the field, and his discount usability argued for small, frequent tests over big studies.

Study firstThe ten heuristics article on nngroup.com, then Usability Engineering (1993).
In this course: Testing: heuristics

Steve Krug

Usability consultant and author

People do not read, they scan; they do not optimise, they satisfice; and they muddle through. So make every page self-evident, and if it cannot be, self-explanatory. He also made a monthly do-it-yourself usability test feel achievable for anyone.

Study firstDon't Make Me Think (2000; Revisited, 2014). A short afternoon.
In this course: The Human Mind: satisficing

Alan Cooper

Software designer; the "father of Visual Basic"

Argued that software should be designed around users' goals rather than tasks or features, and introduced personas as a design tool in The Inmates Are Running the Asylum (1999). About Face is his long manual of goal-directed design.

Scientists behind the laws

The researchers whose experiments the laws of UX quote, often loosely. Read the originals once.

Paul Fitts

Psychologist, US Air Force and Ohio State

In 1954 showed that the time to move to a target grows with distance and shrinks with target width, in a predictable logarithmic way. This is the reason for big touch targets, edge and corner menus, and context menus at the pointer.

Study firstRead a plain summary first; his 1954 paper on the information capacity of the human motor system is short.
In this course: The Human Mind: Fitts

William Hick and Ray Hyman

Psychologists

Measured in 1952 and 1953 that decision time grows with the logarithm of the number of equally likely choices. It applies to unfamiliar, comparable options; it does not mean fewer menu items is always faster.

Study firstThe Hick's law entry in Laws of UX, then the chapter in The Human Mind.
In this course: The Human Mind: Hick

George A. Miller

Cognitive psychologist, Harvard and Princeton

His 1956 paper "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two" showed limits on how much we can hold and judge at once, and introduced chunking. It is often misquoted as a rule for menu length; it is not one.

Study firstThe paper itself, which is readable, then Cowan below.
In this course: The Human Mind: working memory

Nelson Cowan

Cognitive psychologist, University of Missouri

Argued in 2001 that working memory holds about four chunks, not seven. Design consequence: do not ask people to remember anything across screens if you can show it instead.

Study firstHis 2001 paper "The magical number 4 in short-term memory".
In this course: The Human Mind: working memory

Card, Moran and Newell

Researchers at Xerox PARC and Carnegie Mellon

Stuart Card, Thomas Moran and Allen Newell's The Psychology of Human-Computer Interaction (1983) modelled people as information processors, with the GOMS and keystroke-level models for predicting task time. The book that made HCI a science.

Study firstRead about the keystroke-level model; use it to compare two flows by counting steps.
In this course: The Human Mind: time

Max Wertheimer and the Gestalt school

Psychologists, Berlin, 1910s–1930s

Wertheimer, Koffka and Köhler described how perception organises parts into wholes: proximity, similarity, closure, continuity, figure and ground. Every layout decision about grouping relies on these.

Study firstAny good summary of the Gestalt principles, then test them on your own screen.
In this course: The Human Mind: Gestalt

Anne Treisman

Cognitive psychologist

Her feature integration theory (1980) explained why some things pop out instantly (a single red item among grey) while others need serial search. The basis of using one accent colour for one important thing.

Study firstA summary of feature integration theory and preattentive features.
In this course: The Human Mind: pop-out

Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris

Psychologists

Their 1999 "invisible gorilla" experiment showed that people counting basketball passes often miss a person in a gorilla suit walking through. Attention is narrow; a change you do not draw attention to may be missed.

Study firstThe Invisible Gorilla (2010).
In this course: The Human Mind: blindness

Daniel Kahneman

Psychologist; Nobel laureate in economics, 2002

Showed with Amos Tversky how judgement departs from rational models: framing, anchoring, loss aversion. With colleagues he described the peak-end rule: we remember an experience by its most intense moment and its end.

Study firstThinking, Fast and Slow (2011), parts one and five.
In this course: The Human Mind: biases · peak-end

Herbert A. Simon

Polymath; Nobel laureate in economics, 1978

Coined "satisficing": people choose the first option that is good enough. In The Sciences of the Artificial (1969) he defined design broadly as devising courses of action to change existing situations into preferred ones.

Study firstThe Sciences of the Artificial, chapter five on design.
In this course: Foundations: what design is

BJ Fogg

Behaviour scientist, Stanford

His behaviour model says a behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt meet at the same moment (B = MAP). Useful for designing helpful habits, and a warning about how easily it is misused.

Study firstTiny Habits (2019).
In this course: The Human Mind: motivation · Ethics: habits

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi

Psychologist

Described flow: deep, absorbed engagement that happens when challenge matches skill and feedback is clear. A design goal for tools of thought, and a reason to protect people from interruption.

Study firstFlow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience (1990).

A note on accuracy

Entries state only well-documented facts: published works, products and dates. Where a date is approximate it says so. Many famous design "quotes" online are misattributed, so this directory paraphrases ideas instead of quoting. If you want the exact words, read the source named in "Study first".

For the solo creator

The independent makers in the software group (Brichter, Rizzi, Reichenstein, Arment, Panic) are your closest peers: small teams or single people who design and build. Study their products more than their talks. Open the app, use it for a week, and write down three decisions you would steal.

Do

  • Pick one person a week and do their "study first" item.
  • Write one paragraph after each: the idea, and where it applies in your product.
  • Prefer primary sources (their book, talk or product) over summaries of them.

Don't

  • Collect quotes for slides. Use ideas for decisions.
  • Follow only people from one group. The interesting work is where groups meet.
  • Take any one person's rules as universal; each wrote for a context.
Exercise 3.1 4 weeks · 1 hour a week

A four-person apprenticeship

  1. Pick four people from four different groups whose work relates to your product.
  2. Each week, do one person's "study first" item. Take notes by hand.
  3. Write a one-page memo: the idea, a picture of where it shows up in a real product, and one change it suggests for your own product.
  4. Make the change, or write down precisely why you will not.
You're done when: you have four one-page memos and at least two shipped changes traced back to a named person's idea.

What the ten look like on one screen

Ideas only matter when they change a pixel. Here is the same simple save flow drawn twice. The first ignores most of the ten; the second applies them. Count how many you can name in the second.

Key idea

When two designers seem to disagree, check whether they are applying the same idea to different contexts. Usually they are.

Do

  • Use the ten as a lens in reviews: which idea does this screen break?
  • Write your product's own principles as local versions of the ten.
  • Notice when two ideas conflict (less, but better against make state visible) and decide on purpose.

Don't

  • Treat the ten as a style. They produce very different looks in different products.
  • Use one idea to win every argument. Each has a cost.
  • Stop at the slogan. Each idea has a mechanism; learn it in the linked guide.

In the wild

  • iA WriteriA, 2010 onward
    Content first, less but better, respect attention: one typeface family, a focus mode, almost no settings. Three of the ten, applied without compromise, make a product's identity.
  • Google Docs version historyGoogle
    Autosave plus a browsable history make state visible and every action recoverable. Undo and history are honesty about mistakes.
  • Kindle e-readersAmazon, 2007 onward
    E-ink with no backlight glare in early models, no notifications while reading, progress shown quietly. Respecting attention can be the main feature.
Check yourself

Your minimal app hides sync status entirely "to keep it clean". A user loses an hour of work believing it had synced. Which two ideas are in conflict, and which should have won?

Minimalism removes what does not help. Sync status helps exactly when it matters. A small, quiet indicator respects both ideas; hiding it entirely breaks the more important one. Blaming the user (d) is what Norman warns against.

A law of UX is a prediction about people, borrowed from research, with a limited range where it holds. Use each one as a question to ask of a design, not as a rule that wins arguments.

How to use a checklist, and the problem brief

Taught by Atul Gawande, with the aviation tradition he drew on

A checklist does not make you a good designer. It stops a good designer from forgetting the things they already know, especially when tired, rushed or proud.

Gawande's The Checklist Manifesto (2009) describes how short checklists cut complications in surgery. The mechanism is simple: experts skip steps they consider obvious, and under pressure, obvious steps are exactly the ones that get skipped. A checklist moves those steps out of memory and into the world.

He distinguishes two kinds. A read-do checklist is followed step by step, like a recipe. A do-confirm checklist is used after you have worked from memory: you pause and confirm nothing was missed. Most of the checklists here are do-confirm. Design the screen your way, then run the list. The exception is the problem brief, which is read-do: answer each item before you start.

Five rules for checklists that work

  1. Short beats complete. Gawande suggests five to nine items per pause point. Long lists here are split into groups; run one group at a time.
  2. Check the product, not your memory of it. Open the real screen on a real device. Most false ticks come from checking from memory.
  3. Write each item as a statement that can be false. "Errors are friendly" cannot fail. "Error messages say what went wrong and how to fix it" can.
  4. An unticked item is a decision, not a failure. If you choose to skip an item, write why. That note is design rationale.
  5. Edit the list. When you make the same mistake twice, add it. When an item never catches anything, cut it.

Inspiration, tools and communities

Where to look, what to use, who to talk to

Look at real products more than at design galleries, and keep what you collect with a note on why it works. Inspiration without annotation is just scrolling.

Galleries show you surfaces, often polished concepts that never shipped. Products show you decisions under real constraints: real data, real edge cases, real users. Use galleries to widen your visual range; use products to learn design. For both, the habit that matters is the swipe file: save it, then write one line on the decision it shows and where it would apply in your work. See Practice: the swipe file.

Communities

Working alone, you need a few people who will tell you the truth. Look for small, specific groups over large general ones: a forum for your platform, a group of indie developers, a local design meetup, two or three peers you swap critiques with monthly. Large feeds are good for news and bad for feedback. When you post work, ask a specific question ("Is the primary action clear on this screen?") rather than "Thoughts?". See Practice: getting feedback as a soloist.

Wrap-up

This library ends the course, but it is meant to be the guide you open most after finishing it. The other guides teach the reasons; this one keeps them within reach while you work.

Ten things to remember

  1. The field is a few long threads. Study one hub per thread: Rams, Hara, Bringhurst, Engelbart, Tufte, Norman, Weiser.
  2. Ten ideas recur everywhere: visible state, fit the person, less but better, respect attention, direct action, structure first, content first, problem first, test early, honesty.
  3. A precise word lets you notice and fix a problem. Learn the glossary by teaching it back.
  4. Laws are predictions with boundaries. Ask them as questions, never use them to overrule users.
  5. A few numbers settle most arguments: 44 pt and 48 dp, 4.5:1, 45–75 characters, 16 px, 0.1/1/10 s, 150–300 ms, 3 flashes, 5 users.
  6. Put the numbers into tokens and components so you decide them once.
  7. Checklists stop experts forgetting what they know. Run do-confirm lists after designing, on the real product.
  8. Most mistakes come from four defaults: your knowledge, your setup, your order of work, your incentives. Fix the source.
  9. Read fewer books, apply each one, and prefer free originals for the deep ideas.
  10. Collect inspiration with a note on why, or do not collect it.
