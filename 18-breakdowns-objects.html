Breakdowns: Objects. Seventeen things you can hold, and what they teach about things you can't.

A radio with a clear lid, a calculator with round keys, a CD player you switch on by pulling a cord, a clock whose second hand waits for the train. Physical products are design under pressure: every part costs money, every control takes space, every mistake ships in a box. This guide takes them apart one decision at a time and carries each decision across into software you can build alone.

Warm-up: how to study objects

You have already met Rams's ten principles, Fukasawa's "without thought" and Kenya Hara's emptiness in the starter guide. Here you stop quoting them and start reading them in the objects themselves. Principles are compressed lessons; objects are the uncompressed source. When you read the source you see the trade-offs the principles hide.

Each product chapter has the same shape, so you can skim or study. A facts box. An annotated drawing with numbered pins. A list of numbered decisions that match the pins, each with why and what it cost. What it left out. Where it falls short. Then "Steal this for your software", a quiz and a short exercise. The drawings are schematic recreations, simplified on purpose: they show structure, not exact proportions. Look up photographs of the real thing as you read; the gap between the drawing and the photo is itself a lesson in what matters.

Study plan: one part per sitting. Keep a notebook (paper or a text file) titled "Steal list". Every time you hit a "Steal this" item that applies to your product, copy it in with today's date. By the end you'll have twenty to forty candidate changes, and the final chapter helps you pick three.

1

How to break down a product

Taught by Dieter Rams, with Don Norman and Naoto Fukasawa

A breakdown is not a review. You are not judging whether you like the object; you are reverse-engineering the decisions that made it, and the costs those decisions paid, through seven fixed lenses.

Most people "study" good design by looking at it and feeling something. That feeling is real but it doesn't transfer. You can't open your editor and type "make it feel like a Braun". What transfers is a decision: this radio puts its only frequently-used control under the thumb, and hides the rest. Decisions are portable. Feelings are not.

The trouble is that decisions are invisible once they're made. A finished object looks inevitable, as if it could not have been any other way. Your job in a breakdown is to make it look chosen again. For every feature ask: what were the alternatives, why was this one picked, and what did it cost? If you can't name a cost, you haven't understood the decision yet. Every real decision costs something.

The seven lenses

Look through each lens in order. The order matters: it goes from why the thing exists, to how it looks, to how it's used, to what it's made of, to the small stuff, to its relatives, and finally to the absences, which are the hardest to see.

1 Purpose
What job does it do, for whom, in what situation? What is the one sentence that, if false, would make the object pointless? (The Kindle: "read books for hours without your eyes or the battery giving up.")
2 Form
Overall shape, proportions, hierarchy of parts. What is biggest? What do you see first? Does the form explain the function, or hide it?
3 Interaction
Every control: where it is, how it moves, what feedback it gives, and whether its current state is visible without touching it. Map the most common task, step by step.
4 Material and medium honesty
Is each material used for what it's good at? Does anything imitate something else (plastic pretending to be wood)? How does it age: does wear make it worse or more personal?
5 Details
Radii, gaps, the click of a switch, the label typeface, the colour of a single key. Details are where care becomes visible, and where cheap shortcuts show.
6 System and family
What is it part of? Siblings in the same line, modules that combine, accessories, refills, the shop it's sold in. Great objects are rarely alone.
7 What was left out
Features competitors had that this lacks. Controls you expected and didn't find. Labels, logos, colours, settings removed. The absences are where the courage is.
Key idea

If you can't name what a decision cost, you haven't understood it yet.

A worked example: the humble wall light switch

Take an object you touch daily and never think about. A good rocker light switch, the large flat kind common in European homes.

  1. Purpose. Turn a room's light on or off, in the dark, with a full hand of shopping, without looking.
  2. Form. A large flat rectangle. The whole face is the control, so the target is huge. (Fitts's law in plastic; see The Human Mind.)
  3. Interaction. Press top or bottom. The rocker's tilt shows state at a glance, and the snap gives sound and touch feedback in under 50 ms.
  4. Material honesty. Matte plastic that hides fingerprints. No fake brass.
  5. Details. Rounded edges, no sharp seam to catch a fingernail. Sometimes a tiny glowing dot so you can find it at night.
  6. System. Same frame for sockets, dimmers and blanks, so a wall of them reads as one family.
  7. Left out. No label, no screen, no app, no "are you sure?". Costs: no scheduling, no remote control. Most people never miss them.

Now translate: the most-used action in your product should be a large target, show its state at rest, respond instantly, and ask nothing. That's one sentence of design direction, earned from a light switch.

For the solo creator

You don't have a design team to critique with, so breakdowns are your critique partner. One object a week, one page each, twenty minutes. Common mistakes

Admiring instead of analysing

Beautiful objects trigger a pleasant feeling, and it's easy to write that feeling down as if it were insight.

Ban adjectives in the first draft. Every sentence must name a part, a decision, or a cost.

Skipping lens 7

You can't see what isn't there, so you list only what's present.

Put a competitor next to the object (an ordinary calculator next to the ET 66) and list the differences. Most of them will be absences.

Translating literally

The easiest translation is visual: copy the colour, the radius, the dial.

Translate the mechanism. The next chapter is all about this.

Translating atoms to bits

Taught by Dieter Rams, with Bret Victor and Kenya Hara

Physical products get their discipline for free, from cost, space, weight and physics. Software has none of those limits, so you must translate the reasons a physical decision was made, and then impose the discipline yourself.

A Braun radio couldn't have forty buttons: each one meant a hole in the case, a switch, wiring, assembly time, and a few more pfennigs on every unit sold. A pocket radio couldn't be bigger than a pocket. A Game Boy had to run on four AA batteries for a whole car journey. The designers often turned these limits into virtues, but the limits came first.

Your app can have forty buttons for free. It can be any size, any colour, any number of screens. Adding a setting takes ten minutes. That freedom is why so much software sprawls. Nothing pushes back.

So when you study an object, separate three things: the surface (colour, texture, shape), the constraint (what physics or cost forced), and the reason (the human need the decision served). Translate the reason. Recreate the constraint on purpose when it helped. Leave the surface behind.

Four kinds of translation

  1. Direct. The idea works the same way on screen. The ET 66's colour-coded keys become a calculator app's coloured operator column. The Swiss clock's single red accent becomes one accent colour in your UI.
  2. Metaphorical. The behaviour transfers, not the mechanism. MUJI's pull cord doesn't become a drawn cord on screen; it becomes "the main action happens with one obvious gesture and no menu".
  3. Constraint transfer. A physical limit becomes a self-imposed budget. The Game Boy's battery life becomes a performance budget (load in under one second, run on a five-year-old phone). The 606's fixed rail spacing becomes a spacing scale.
  4. Anti-translation. Some lessons tell you what not to bring across. A physical shutter dial is great because fingers find it without looking; a drawn dial on a touch screen loses that, and is often worse than a plain list.

The constraint budget

Because software won't push back, write your own constraints down and treat them as physics. Examples that work for a solo product: at most five user-facing settings; one accent colour; first screen usable with no account; page weight under 200 KB; cold start under one second on a mid-range Android phone; any action undoable for 10 seconds. These are your "price per button". Breaking one should feel as expensive as adding a part to a production run.

Myth

"Flat design killed skeuomorphism, so physical objects have nothing to teach screens." Flat design rejected fake textures, which were surface. The reasons behind good physical objects (clear state, direct mapping, few controls, honest materials) are exactly what good flat design needed and often forgot. Many flat apps hide state and affordances that a 1958 radio showed plainly.

Do

  • Write each lesson in the form "because people need X, the object does Y; in software, Z".
  • Turn helpful physical limits into written budgets.
  • Ask which physical advantages your medium lacks (touch, detents, no-look use) and compensate.
  • Use screen-native strengths: search, undo, sync, instant change.

Don't

  • Draw knobs, cords, leather and wood to signal "quality".
  • Assume a physical control maps well to touch; most rotary gestures are awkward on glass.
  • Copy a limit that no longer serves anyone (a fixed small capacity, a one-colour screen) just for nostalgia.

In the wild

  • iOS 7Apple, 2013
    Removed the leather, felt and linen of earlier iOS apps and kept layered depth and motion to show structure. Drop the costume, keep the information the costume was carrying (hierarchy, state).
  • Halide camera appLux, 2017
    Takes cues from manual cameras (exposure and focus as direct, visible controls) but uses swipe gestures fitted to a phone rather than drawn dials. Translate the behaviour of a camera, not its chrome.
  • Teenage Engineering's OP-1 field and its software2022

For the solo creator

Put your constraint budget in the README of your project, right under the product's one-sentence purpose. When you are tempted to add a setting at midnight, you'll have to edit the budget first, in writing. That small friction is the closest thing you have to a factory's cost accountant.

Common mistakes

Skeuomorphic homage

You love an object, so you draw it. It feels like respect.

Ask what the object's form was for. Draw the function, not the object: a big target, a visible state, an instant response.

Importing scarcity

Old objects were limited, and limits look like purity.

Keep only limits that serve the user now. A 5 GB iPod was a limit of 2001; "one library, one control" is the lesson.

Forgetting what touch screens lack

Screens can show anything, so you assume they can do anything.

Physical controls give detents, resistance, no-look use. Compensate with large targets, haptics where available, and keyboard shortcuts.

Braun and the Rams school

Between the mid-1950s and the mid-1990s, Braun's design department under Dieter Rams, with Hans Gugelot and the Ulm school early on and Dietrich Lubs later, made radios, calculators, clocks and shelving that still define "good design" for most software designers. Five objects, five lessons: show what's inside, let one control dominate, code by colour, design a system not a thing, and spend your single accent where it matters.

Braun SK 4: the radio with nothing to hide

Taught by Dieter Rams and Hans Gugelot

A radio-phonograph that stopped pretending to be furniture. White metal, light wood ends, and a clear lid, so you could see the machine for what it was and the record you were playing.

In 1956 most radiograms in German living rooms were polished wooden cabinets with cloth grilles and brass trim: furniture that happened to contain electronics. The SK 4 put a turntable and a radio in a plain white steel body with wooden end panels, laid every control out on the top plate, and covered the top with transparent acrylic. People called it Schneewittchensarg, Snow White's coffin. It was meant as mockery. It became a compliment.

MakerBraun, Germany
DesignersDieter Rams, Hans Gugelot (HfG Ulm)
Year1956
CategoryRadio-phonograph combination
Study it forTransparency, honesty, appliance over ornament
  1. A clear acrylic lid instead of a wooden or metal one. You see the record, the arm and the dial with the lid closed: the machine explains itself. The usual account is that a metal lid was tried and rattled, and transparent acrylic solved the problem while showing the insides. Cost: acrylic scratches and shows dust, and the coffin jokes. Clear dust covers later became standard on turntables, which is the best evidence the decision was right.
  2. A white-painted metal body, not a veneered cabinet. It reads as equipment, closer to a laboratory instrument than a sideboard. Cost: to many buyers in 1956 it looked cold and unfinished. Braun accepted a smaller market for a clearer identity.
  3. Light wood only at the ends. The one warm material sits where hands touch and where the object meets the room. It isn't decoration; it's the structural end of the box. Cost: two materials to source and join, but the warmth made the white bearable at home.
  4. Every control in one field on the top plate. Knobs and keys line up in a grid beside the turntable, where your eyes already are when you put on a record. Nothing on the sides, nothing hidden. Cost: you must open the lid to change station.
  5. A slotted metal speaker front instead of cloth. The slots are the speaker's honest opening, cut into the same material as the body. Cost: less forgiving of dents than fabric; the pattern must be precise because it is the whole face.
  6. Small, plain labels. Short words in a clean sans-serif, no logos competing with function. The type supports the controls instead of announcing the brand.

What it left out

Gloss veneer, brass trim, cloth grilles, curved "radio cabinet" shapes, a large logo, legs. Most importantly it left out disguise: the assumption that technology in the home should look like something else. That single absence is the reason the object still matters.

Limits and criticism

It was a combination unit with a built-in speaker, so sound quality was bounded by the box, and Braun's own later hi-fi went modular (separate amplifier, tuner, speakers). It was a product for a modernist minority; many households kept their walnut radiograms. And "honest" is not the same as "warm": the SK 4 needed those wooden ends to be livable.

Steal this for your software

  • Show the work through a clear lid. Let people see the real content while processing happens: rows appearing as they import, a document that shows its saved state, a sync count rather than a spinner.
  • Look like a tool, not a decoration. Neutral ground, content in front, chrome minimal. Your UI is the white steel; the user's content is the record.
  • One warm material. In a neutral interface, choose one place for warmth (a serif for reading text, a single accent, a friendly empty state) and put it where people touch most.
  • One control field. Put all controls for a task in one place near the content they affect, not scattered across toolbars, menus and side panels.
  • Expect the coffin jokes. When something honest looks unusual, early mockery is normal. Judge by whether people keep using it.

Do

  • Expose state and progress in the content area itself.
  • Keep controls together beside what they change.
  • Use plain labels in your interface typeface.

Don't

  • Hide your product's real nature behind ornamental chrome.
  • Scatter one task's controls across three regions.
  • Let branding compete with function in the working view.

  • iA WriterInformation Architects, 2010

  • Stripe DashboardStripe

For the solo creator

Transparency is cheap to build and saves you support email. A visible "Saved 3 seconds ago", an import that shows rows arriving, an error that names the line: each is a few lines of code, and each removes a class of "is it broken?" messages you'd otherwise answer yourself.

Common mistakes

Transparency as clutter

"Show the work" gets read as "show everything": logs, IDs and timestamps on every row.

The SK 4 shows the record and the dial, not the wiring. Show what the user cares about (their content, progress, result); hide internals behind a details view.

Cold minimalism

You strip all warmth in pursuit of purity.

Keep one warm element where hands touch, like the wooden ends.

The clear lid shows the real material (your photos) and a plain state. a is still a closed lid with nicer paint; c is the wiring, not the record; d hides a long process people want to monitor.

Braun T3: one control, one face

Taught by Dieter Rams, with Jony Ive as a later student of the object

A pocket transistor radio whose whole front is two things: a grid of speaker holes and a big round tuning disc. The control you use most is the biggest thing on the face, and nothing competes with it.

Transistors made radios small enough to carry in the late 1950s. Most pocket radios of the time borrowed the look of their big cousins: chrome trim, little dials, logos. The T3 reduced the face to a rectangle of perforations and a disc under your thumb. More than forty years later, people placing it next to a first-generation iPod saw an obvious family resemblance: a grid-like or plain upper area and a large circular control below.

MakerBraun
DesignerDieter Rams
Year1958
CategoryPocket transistor radio
Study it forControl hierarchy, form follows the main task
  1. The speaker grid is the face. Instead of hiding the speaker behind a decorative grille, a regular field of holes makes the sound's exit the main visual pattern. Cost: the hole pattern must be precise and evenly spaced, or the whole face looks wrong.
  2. The tuning disc is the biggest control. Tuning was what you did most, so it got a large wheel under the thumb, readable at a glance. Cost: less room for anything else, which was the point.
  3. The scale is on the control itself. Numbers on the disc rotate past a fixed mark, so the control and its display are one object. No separate pointer and window to align in your head (Norman would call this good mapping).
  4. A pocket-shaped body with soft corners. Sized and rounded for a hand and a pocket rather than a shelf. Cost: a small speaker with limited sound, accepted as the price of portability.
  5. Nothing else on the front. Secondary functions stay out of the face. The front shows only what the listener needs while listening and tuning.

What it left out

Chrome bezels, a large logo, multiple small knobs of equal size, decorative typography, a carrying handle that would add bulk. It also left out the idea that a radio must look like a miniature of a bigger radio.

Limits and criticism

A single-band pocket radio with a small speaker. The rotating disc is lovely for slow browsing but slow if you know exactly what frequency you want, the same tension the iPod's wheel would face with large libraries. A big, single control is only right when one task really dominates.

Steal this for your software

  • Size by frequency. Rank your actions by how often they're used. The top one gets the largest target and the best position (thumb zone on mobile). Everything else steps down.
  • Put the display on the control. A slider that shows its value on the thumb, a segmented control that shows the current state, a date picker that shows the date. Don't make people match a control to a distant readout.
  • Let the necessary part be the pattern. In a reading app, the text is the decoration. In a dashboard, the data is. Don't add ornament on top of the thing that already fills the space.
  • Keep the face for the main mode. Secondary functions go behind one tap. The first screen shows only what someone needs while doing the main job.

Do

  • Draw a frequency ranking of your actions before you draw a layout.
  • Merge control and readout wherever you can.
  • Test the main action one-handed on a phone.

Don't

  • Give secondary actions the same visual weight as the primary.
  • Use a single big control when there are really two equally frequent jobs.
  • Copy a circular wheel onto a touch screen because a famous object had one.

In the wild

  • Apple iPodApple, 2001
    A screen above and one large round control below; Jony Ive later wrote the foreword to Sophie Lovell's 2011 book on Rams. The lineage is about hierarchy, not about circles.
  • ShazamShazam, 2008 on iPhone

For the solo creator

You don't need analytics to rank actions. Watch three people use your product for ten minutes each, tally which controls they touch, and redesign the hierarchy from the tally. One afternoon's work, often a bigger improvement than a week of new features.

Common mistakes

Democracy of buttons

Every feature cost you effort, so every feature feels like it deserves equal space.

Your effort is not the user's frequency. Rank by use, not by build time.

The wrong dominant control

You make your favourite feature big, not the most used one.

Check with real use. Sometimes the boring action (search, add) is the one that deserves the T3 disc.

Detached readouts

Settings show state in one place and controls in another, like a value shown above a plain slider.

Put the value on or beside the control, updated live.

Check yourself

Your habit-tracker's home screen shows six habits, each with "Edit", "Stats", "Share" and "Done" buttons of equal size. What's the T3 move?

Marking done is the job people do daily; the rest is occasional. c sizes by frequency and clears the face. a and b keep equal weight, d copies a surface.

Braun ET 66: colour as a map

Taught by Dietrich Lubs and Dieter Rams

A pocket calculator whose keys are round, slightly domed, and coloured by job: numbers in one tone, operations in another, and a single accent for the key that finishes the sum. You can read the keyboard's structure before you read a single label.

By the 1980s calculators were cheap, everywhere and mostly ugly: flat rubber keys, every key the same, labels crowded into the plastic. The ET 66 continued a line Braun had started with the ET 22 and ET 33 in the 1970s and refined it into a small, dark, quiet object. Its colour coding is so legible that the layout became one of the most imitated in software.

MakerBraun
DesignersDietrich Lubs, Dieter Rams
Year1987
CategoryPocket calculator
Study it forColour coding by function, touchable affordance, a later software homage
  1. A quiet display, set back. A plain digit window at the top, nothing around it but the dark body. The answer is the only thing to read. Cost: no second line of history, which software calculators later added.
  2. Round, convex keys. Each key is a small dome. The shape says "press me" to the fingertip, centres the finger, and makes adjacent keys feel separate even without looking. Cost: more complex moulding than flat keys, and fewer keys fit.
  3. Function keys in their own tone. Clear, percent and similar keys share a colour distinct from numbers, so the "dangerous" or unusual keys look different from the ones you press all the time.
  4. Operations in a column, in their own colour. The arithmetic keys line up on the right edge in one tone. Your eye learns "verbs on the right, nouns in the middle". The grouping does the work a label would otherwise do.
  5. One accent key. The equals key carries the only bright colour, marking the action that completes the task. A single accent is strong because it's alone. Cost: once used here, the colour can't be spent anywhere else on the object.
  6. A strict grid. Equal spacing and alignment so the colours read as regions, not as a random pattern. Colour coding only works on a disciplined layout.

What it left out

Scientific functions, a second display line, a solar panel strip across the face, printed brand slogans, and the visual noise of every key competing equally. It also left out multiple accent colours: the restraint is what makes the one accent mean something.

Limits and criticism

Colour coding by tone alone is fragile: in poor light, or for people with low contrast sensitivity, a dark key on a dark body can disappear. The labels are small. And a homage can become a trap: by the 2010s, colour-coded round keys became a "Braun look" applied to apps where the grouping itself was not clear.

Vitsœ 606 and the Braun TP 1: design the system, not the thing

Taught by Dieter Rams, with Niels Vitsœ

A shelving system of aluminium wall rails, pins and shelves that you can extend, rearrange and move house with, using parts that still fit decades later. The product isn't a shelf. It's a set of rules that make shelves.

Rams designed the 606 Universal Shelving System for the furniture company Vitsœ (originally Vitsœ + Zapf) in 1960, and it is still made today, now in the UK. Parts made decades apart are designed to work together, so owners add to it over a lifetime instead of replacing it. The year before, Rams's TP 1 for Braun had shown the same thinking at pocket scale: a small T 4 pocket radio and a portable record player that could be combined into one unit or used apart.

MakerVitsœ (606); Braun (TP 1)
DesignerDieter Rams
Year1960 (606); 1959 (TP 1)
CategoryModular shelving; combinable portable audio
Study it forModularity, backward compatibility, longevity, repair
  1. A wall rail as the foundation. The structure lives in slim aluminium tracks fixed to the wall, so the furniture has no heavy sides or legs. Everything else hangs from it. Cost: needs a wall you can drill, and careful installation.
  2. A fixed hole pitch as the "grammar". Holes at a regular spacing let any shelf go at any height. The spacing is the interface between parts, and it hasn't been changed. Cost: you can't fine-tune to the millimetre; you live on the grid.
  3. Few part types. Shelves, cabinets, desks and a few accessories. A small vocabulary keeps manufacture simple and keeps combinations predictable.
  4. Backward compatibility as a promise. New parts fit old systems. Owners extend instead of replace, and the company sells to the same customer for decades. Cost: the company can never "redesign" the interface; innovation must happen within it.
  5. Plain, neutral finishes. The system disappears behind the books and objects on it, and doesn't date as fashions change.
  6. Designed to be moved and reconfigured. The shelves come down and go up again in a new home, with a new layout. Vitsœ has long offered planning help and buys back or resells used systems. The relationship continues after the sale.

What they left out

The 606 left out fixed sizes, matching "sets", and the idea of a finished piece of furniture. There's no "complete" state; it is always ready for the next change. Both objects left out planned obsolescence: nothing about them invites you to replace the whole thing.

Limits and criticism

Systems ask more of the buyer: you have to plan. The 606 is expensive compared with flat-pack shelving, and the fixed grid will occasionally fight an odd wall. Backward compatibility also locks the maker in; every new part inherits decisions from 1960. That's a feature for owners and a constraint for designers.

Steal this for your software

  • Define your "hole pitch". One spacing unit (4 or 8 px) and a short scale built from it. Every margin and gap lands on it, so any component sits next to any other.
  • A small vocabulary of parts. A dozen components, not a hundred. New features are arrangements of existing parts first, new parts only when unavoidable.
  • Backward compatibility as respect. Old documents open, old URLs work, export formats stay readable, keyboard shortcuts don't move. Every break costs your users their investment.
  • Composable features. Like the TP 1, let pieces work alone and better together: a share sheet extension, a CLI next to the app, an API that powers integrations.
  • Keep the relationship after the "sale". Changelogs, migration paths and import tools are your planning service and buy-back scheme.

Do

  • Write down your data formats and URL structures as promises.
  • Design new features with existing components before drawing new ones.
  • Make export and import first-class.

Don't

  • Redesign the interface between parts every year.
  • Ship a "v2" that orphans v1 data.
  • Confuse a system with a style guide; the 606 is defined by how parts connect, not by how they look.

Braun clocks and watches: one accent, perfect legibility

Taught by Dietrich Lubs, with Dieter Rams

A small travel alarm clock (the AB series, and later the AW wristwatches) that you can read in a half-second from across a dark room: high-contrast face, clear sans-serif numerals, and a single yellow accent on the hand that matters most.

Dietrich Lubs joined Braun in the early 1960s and became responsible for much of its clock, watch and calculator work. The alarm clocks he designed with Rams in the 1970s and 1980s, including the AB 1, and the AW 10 wristwatch of 1989, share a recognisable language: matte black or white case, a face with nothing extra, careful numerals, and one warm colour. Braun still makes clocks in this language today, which says something about how little it has needed to change.

MakerBraun
DesignersDietrich Lubs, with Dieter Rams
YearAB series from the 1970s; AB 1 in the 1980s; AW 10 watch 1989
CategoryTravel alarm clocks and wristwatches
Study it forLegibility, a single accent colour, hierarchy of hands
  1. A simple geometric case. A square or rectangular body around a round face. The case stays matte and dark (or white), so the face is the only bright, readable area. Cost: almost no room for brand expression.
  2. Numerals chosen for reading, not style. Clean sans-serif digits, sized so the face reads at arm's length, often with all twelve numbers rather than marks only. Cost: a busier face than a "minimal" four-marker dial, accepted because reading the time quickly is the whole job.
  3. A hierarchy of hands. Hour hand shortest and thickest, minute hand longer and thinner. Weight tells you which is which before length does.
  4. One accent colour, on the moving part. The second hand (yellow on many Braun designs) is the only colour. It shows the clock is running and gives the face life without adding noise. Cost: the accent can't be used for anything else, including the brand.
  5. The alarm setting shown on the face. A distinct hand or marker shows when the alarm will ring, so you check it with a glance in bed, not by pressing a button.
  6. Few, well-placed physical controls. A large snooze or alarm-off area that's easy to hit half-asleep; small, recessed setting wheels you only touch occasionally. Frequency sets the size, again.
Lab: legibility and the single accent
Glance at the clock for half a second, look away, and say the time. Then switch off numerals and weighted hands and try again. Turn on "decoration" (extra rings, a logo, a second colour) and notice how the yellow hand loses its pull. One accent is a signal; two are a pattern.

What it left out

Date windows on most models, luminous decoration, multiple colours, a big logo on the face, textured dials, Roman numerals. It also left out any second accent: no red alarm button competing with the yellow hand.

Limits and criticism

Matte dark faces can be hard to read in the dark without lighting, and analogue faces in general are slower to read precisely than a digital display. Some models' setting wheels are small and fiddly. The style has been copied so much that a yellow second hand now reads as "Braun-like" decoration on products that lack the underlying hierarchy.

Steal this for your software

  • Accent the live thing. Use your accent colour for what's current, changing or active: the playing track, the selected item, the timer running. Static things stay neutral.
  • Hierarchy by weight. Like the hands, distinguish elements by weight and size first. Headline thick and short, body lighter, captions finest.
  • Show the scheduled state. An alarm hand is a preview of the future. Show upcoming events, scheduled sends and next billing dates where people glance, not inside settings.
  • Legibility over minimalism. If removing numerals makes the time slower to read, keep the numerals. Minimal means nothing unnecessary, not nothing.

Do

  • Test glanceability: a half-second look, then recall.
  • Keep one accent per view and give it a meaning.
  • Show scheduled or future states on the main surface.

Don't

  • Strip labels or numbers for elegance when they carry the job.
  • Use the brand colour for decoration and then again for state.
  • Put the most-used control in the smallest target.

MUJI as a system: emptiness and "this will do"

Taught by Kenya Hara, with Ikko Tanaka and Naoto Fukasawa

MUJI is not one object; it is a way of making thousands of objects that share no logo, little colour and plain packaging, so that each one is an empty vessel the owner fills with their own life. Its ideal is not "this is what I want" but a confident "this will do".

MUJI began in 1980 as a private label of the Japanese supermarket chain Seiyu, with about forty products. The name, short for Mujirushi Ryōhin, means roughly "no-brand quality goods". The founding art direction came from graphic designer Ikko Tanaka. Its early approach rested on three practices: careful selection of materials, streamlined manufacturing, and simplified packaging. Kenya Hara became its art director in the early 2000s and gave the philosophy its clearest language: emptiness.

MakerRyohin Keikaku (MUJI)
Key figuresIkko Tanaka (founding art direction), Kenya Hara (art director from 2002), Naoto Fukasawa (product advisory)
YearFounded 1980
CategoryBrand and system of household goods, stationery, clothing, food, retail
Study it forEmptiness, defaults, consistency across thousands of products

Emptiness is not simplicity

Hara draws a distinction that matters for software. Simplicity, as European modernism practised it, is reduction toward a correct, rational form: remove until only the essential remains. Emptiness is something else: a form that is open enough to receive whatever the user brings. A simple object says "here is the right answer". An empty object says "here is room for your answer". A plain MUJI notebook isn't the correct notebook; it's a notebook that becomes yours as you fill it.

Hara's famous illustration is the MUJI "Horizon" advertising campaign of 2003: photographs of nearly nothing, a horizon line over the salt flat of Uyuni in Bolivia and over Mongolian grassland, with the small MUJI logo. The images carry no message. Viewers project their own. That is the brand's whole idea in one picture.

"This will do" versus "this is what I want"

Hara describes MUJI's target feeling with two Japanese phrases. Kore ga ii: "this is what I want", a strong, specific preference, the feeling most brands chase. Kore de ii: "this will do", which in MUJI's sense is not resignation but a calm, confident satisfaction. The product is good enough that you stop searching. It doesn't need to be your favourite; it needs to be right enough that you never think about it again.

Software chases kore ga ii relentlessly: delight, personality, endless customisation so each user gets exactly what they want. That produces settings pages with ninety toggles and products that need tuning before they work. A kore de ii product ships excellent defaults, and most people never change them.

The system's decisions

  1. Materials shown as they are. Unbleached or off-white paper, kraft card, undyed fabrics where possible. Skipping bleaching and dyeing was originally a cost saving that became an aesthetic. Cost: a narrower colour range, and some people read it as "cheap".
  2. Transparent and translucent plastics. Clear gel pens show their ink level; translucent polypropylene boxes show their contents. The material tells you what's inside.
  3. Sizes that fit each other. Storage boxes, drawers and shelves are sized to stack and nest together across the range, so a new purchase fits an old one. A system, like the 606.
  4. The label does the talking, and it's quiet. Product name, material, size and price on a simple label or tag; usually no logo on the product itself. Information replaces branding.
  5. Standard formats and consistent stationery. Notebooks in standard paper sizes and simple rulings; pens in a few tip sizes and colours. The range is deep but not wide, so choosing is fast.
  6. The shop as part of the product. Stores use natural materials and calm lighting with products shown plainly. The retail experience and the objects speak one language.

What it leaves out

Logos on products, seasonal colour trends, character licensing, aggressive packaging, sale-driven urgency, and the idea that each product should be someone's favourite. It deliberately avoids peak emotion in favour of steady trust.

Limits and criticism

"No brand" became a very strong brand; the absence of a logo is now instantly recognisable, which is a paradox Hara himself has discussed. Quality varies across a range this large, and "this will do" can slide into "this is mediocre" if the defaults aren't actually excellent. Emptiness only works when the empty thing is very well made.

Steal this for your software

  • Design for "this will do". Spend your effort on defaults so good that most people never open settings. Every setting you add is an admission that you didn't decide.
  • Make the product an empty vessel. Neutral chrome, generous space, the user's content as the only colour. A notes app should feel like a MUJI notebook: plain until you write in it.
  • Labels instead of branding. Inside the product, replace logos and slogans with plain, useful information: what this is, what it costs, what will happen.
  • Deep, not wide. A few excellent templates or options, not a gallery of a hundred mediocre ones.
  • Sizes that fit. Consistent components and exports so every part works with every other, like stackable boxes.
  • One language from shop to product. Your landing page, onboarding, app and emails should feel like one calm store (see Identity & Launch).

Do

  • Count your settings and justify each one in writing.
  • Use your content area for the user's content first.
  • Prefer a few deep, excellent options over many shallow ones.

Don't

  • Confuse empty with unfinished; emptiness requires excellent craft.
  • Fill empty states with mascots and marketing.
  • Treat customisation as a substitute for deciding.

Super Normal and ±0: the ordinary, perfected

Taught by Naoto Fukasawa and Jasper Morrison

Some objects are so well suited to life that you only notice their quality after using them for a while. Fukasawa and Morrison called this "Super Normal": not design that stands out, but design that settles in.

In 2006 the two designers staged an exhibition in Tokyo called Super Normal, gathering ordinary objects (many anonymous, some designed by them) that had been refined by long use into something better than "designed". A book, Super Normal: Sensations of the Ordinary, followed in 2007. A few years earlier, in 2003, Fukasawa had helped launch ±0 (Plus Minus Zero), a Japanese brand of household appliances and goods built on a similar idea: not more, not less than necessary. Fukasawa often illustrated "without thought" with an umbrella stand that is nothing but a shallow groove in the floor near a wall: people already lean umbrellas there, so the groove simply holds the tip.

Study it forDesigning for long use, not first impressions
RelatedWithout Thought; MUJI
  1. Design the place, not the product. Instead of adding a new object (a bucket, a rack), modify the environment where the behaviour already happens. Cost: it only works where you control the floor; it's not a product you can sell in a box.
  2. Start from observed behaviour. The idea comes from watching people lean umbrellas against walls and set the tips in tile joints. Observation precedes form.
  3. Use the existing support. The wall already holds the umbrella; the groove only stops it sliding. Do the smallest thing that completes what people are already doing.
  4. No visible "design". Nothing to recognise as designed. The object succeeds when nobody notices it, which makes it impossible to market on looks.

What it leaves out

Signature shapes, "statement" colours, surprise for its own sake, and the designer's ego. Super Normal leaves out the moment where someone says "oh, that's designed". ±0's products tend to be calm, softly geometric household items that look like idealised versions of what you already expected a humidifier or a toaster to look like.

Limits and criticism

Super Normal is hard to sell: it doesn't photograph well against loud competitors, and it can be mistaken for blandness. It also relies on having a well-observed "normal" to perfect; for genuinely new product categories there's no normal yet, and someone has to design the first one boldly.

Steal this for your software

  • Design the environment. Sometimes the best feature is a change to where something happens: a default folder, a sensible file name, a pre-filled field. A groove, not a stand.

  • Judge after a month. Use your own product daily for a month before calling a design done. Write down what still annoys you on day 30.
  • Observe workarounds. When users do something odd to get their job done (copying into a note, renaming files a certain way), that's the tile joint. Build the groove there.

Do

Don't

Amazon Kindle: the device that disappears

Taught by the Kindle team at Amazon (Lab126), with E Ink

A reading device whose explicit goal is to vanish while you read: a paper-like screen that doesn't glow, a battery measured in weeks, buttons where your thumb rests, and almost nothing else to do.

The first Kindle (November 2007) was an odd wedge with a full keyboard, page-turn bars on both edges, and a free mobile data connection for buying books. Over fifteen years, the line removed and refined: the keyboard went (2011), a front light arrived with the Paperwhite (2012), page-turn buttons came back with an asymmetric grip in the Oasis (2016), warm adjustable light spread across the range, and the Scribe (2022) added a stylus for writing. Through every version the core promise stayed: reading, for hours, without the device getting in the way. (The reading software is broken down in Breakdowns: Reading; here we study the hardware.)

MakerAmazon (Lab126)
Year2007 (original) to today
CategoryE-ink e-reader
Key modelsKindle (2007), Paperwhite (2012), Voyage (2014), Oasis (2016), Scribe (2022)
Study it forSingle purpose, subtraction over time, calm technology
  1. E-ink instead of an LCD. Reflective electronic paper reads like print in daylight, doesn't flicker, and uses power mainly when the page changes. That's what gives weeks of battery. Cost: slow refresh, ghosting, poor video and animation, and (until recently) no colour.
  2. A keyboard, then none. The 2007 keyboard made searching and buying possible without a touchscreen. Once touch arrived, the keyboard became dead weight and went. The device got smaller, cheaper and more about reading. Lesson: a feature right for one era can be wrong for the next.
  3. Page turns on the edges. The original had long page-turn bars on both sides so either hand could turn. The core action was physical and always under the thumb.
  4. Battery measured in weeks. The headline spec was never speed or pixels. Battery anxiety ends; you stop thinking about charging, which is part of disappearing.
  5. A front light, not a backlight. The Paperwhite lit the screen from the edges onto its surface, keeping the reflective, paper-like quality while allowing night reading. Later models added warm light to shift the tone toward amber in the evening. Cost: slight unevenness near the light source on some units.
  6. Physical page buttons returned. The Oasis brought back buttons, because tapping glass with the hand that's holding the device is awkward. Touch is flexible; buttons are effortless. Both stayed.
  7. An asymmetric grip. The Oasis moved the weight to a thicker edge so one hand holds it like a book's spine, with the screen rotating so either hand works. Form shaped around how people actually hold a book for an hour.

What it left out

Notifications from other apps, a general web browser worth using, email, social feeds, video, games, a glowing backlight. This is the decision that matters most. A tablet can show books too; it also shows everything else, all the time. The Kindle's absences are its product.

Limits and criticism

The device is tied to one store and ecosystem, a real cost to owners. Lock-screen advertising on cheaper models undercuts the calm. The software has often lagged the hardware: slow menus, cluttered home screens that push store recommendations. And e-ink refresh still makes navigation feel sluggish. "Disappearing" is a goal, not always an achievement.

Steal this for your software

  • Define the "while" state. Your product has a core state (reading, writing, listening, focusing). In that state, hide every control and suppress every interruption. Chrome appears on tap and fades away.
  • Make absence the feature. No notifications, no badges, no feed in the focused state. Say so in your marketing: "nothing else happens here".
  • Battery as respect. Translate "weeks of battery" into low CPU, no polling, dark mode that actually saves power on OLED, small downloads. Your app shouldn't appear in anyone's battery settings.
  • Put the core action where the hand already is. Tap zones on the edges, keyboard arrows, volume keys or headphone controls for "next". Don't make the main action a small target in the middle.
  • Light that adapts to the hour. Respect system dark mode, and consider warmer, lower-contrast reading themes in the evening.
  • Subtract as you learn. Like the keyboard, remove features that the context no longer needs. Each version should be a little more focused.

Do

  • Name your product's "while" state and design it first.
  • Measure your app's energy use on a real device.
  • Let people reach the core action without looking.

Don't

  • Show upsells inside the focused state.
  • Keep a feature only because early users once needed it.
  • Use animation in a reading view; it's the opposite of e-ink calm.

Apple iPod and the click wheel: one control for a thousand songs

Taught by Apple's iPod team (with Tony Fadell, Jony Ive and Phil Schiller among those credited), after Dieter Rams

A music player that solved "how do you navigate a thousand songs with your thumb?" with a single circular control that scrolls faster the faster you turn it, and moved everything complicated off the device and onto the computer.

Apple announced the iPod on 23 October 2001 with the line "1,000 songs in your pocket". Competing MP3 players either held few songs or had large hard drives with clumsy button navigation. The first iPod's mechanical scroll wheel, then a touch-sensitive wheel (2002), then the click wheel with buttons built into the wheel itself (introduced with the iPod mini in January 2004 and the fourth-generation iPod later that year), made long lists fast to browse. Syncing through iTunes meant the device itself never needed a file manager.

Steal this for your software

  • One pattern for every screen. If every level of your hierarchy behaves the same way, people learn it once.
  • Move complexity to where it's easy. Heavy editing on desktop, consumption on mobile; bulk setup in a web dashboard, daily use in the app. Don't force every task into every device.
  • Feedback at the speed of the hand. Haptic ticks on a picker, a subtle sound, a counter changing as you drag: feedback tells people the control is alive and how fast they're moving.
  • Search when there's a keyboard. The wheel existed because there was no keyboard. If your users have one, type-to-filter beats any wheel.

  • Keep navigation identical across levels.

Don't

  • Cram desktop-level management into a phone screen just because you can.

Leica M and Fujifilm X100: state you can see without switching on

Taught by Leica (from Oskar Barnack's lineage to the M series) and Fujifilm

On a Leica M or a Fujifilm X100, shutter speed is a number on a dial, aperture is a number on a ring, focus distance is a mark on the lens. You can read every important setting with the camera switched off, in your hand or across the room. No menu needed.

The Leica M3 (1954) set the pattern for the M rangefinder line: a compact body, an optical viewfinder with a rangefinder patch for focusing, and manual controls each doing one thing. Digital M cameras kept that pattern. Leica's motto Das Wesentliche, "the essential", became most literal in variants that remove things: the "-P" models such as the M9-P (2011) dropped the red dot logo for discretion, and the M-D (2016) removed the rear screen entirely. In 2011 Fujifilm's X100 brought the dial-based approach to a fixed-lens digital camera with a hybrid viewfinder, and a whole category of "dial cameras" followed.

MakersLeica Camera (Germany); Fujifilm (Japan)

CategoryRangefinder and fixed-lens cameras

RelatedFujifilm X-Pro, X-T series

  2. Aperture and focus on the lens itself. The aperture ring and distance scale sit where those variables physically act. Mapping is direct: turn the lens to change what the lens does. Experienced photographers pre-focus by the scale without looking through the viewfinder.
  3. A focus tab under the finger. Many M lenses have a small tab on the focus ring; its position tells you roughly the focus distance by touch alone. A state readable by feel.
  4. An optical viewfinder you look through, not at. The rangefinder window shows the world directly, with frame lines, and you can see outside the frame (what is about to enter). Steal this for your software

  • Put controls where the effect happens. Formatting near the text, crop on the image, column sort on the column header. The lens-ring principle.
  • Remove to focus. Consider a "Wesentliche" mode: a variant of your app without the screen that distracts (stats, feed, notifications), for people who want only the core act.
  • Take your logo off the work. Exports, shared links and embeds should carry the user's content, not your branding, unless they choose otherwise.

Do

  • Show non-default states loudly (filters, modes, overrides).
  • Keep advanced settings in menus; keep essential ones out of them.

Don't

  • Hide modes that change behaviour behind icons with no label.
  • Draw skeuomorphic dials for things a segmented control would show better.
  • Brand users' exports by default.

Nintendo Game Boy and Wii: lateral thinking with withered technology

Taught by Gunpei Yokoi, with Nintendo (Satoru Iwata, Shigeru Miyamoto) carrying the idea forward

The Game Boy beat technically superior colour handhelds by using mature, cheap, power-efficient technology in a new way, and putting battery life, price, durability and great games ahead of specifications.

Gunpei Yokoi, head of Nintendo's R&D1, described his approach as kareta gijutsu no suihei shikō, usually translated "lateral thinking with withered technology": use technology that is mature, well understood and cheap, and find a new use for it. The Game Boy (1989) had a small greenish monochrome screen with no backlight when rivals such as the Atari Lynx and Sega Game Gear offered colour. But it ran for many hours on four AA batteries while the Game Gear drained six in a few, it was cheaper, it survived drops, and it came with Tetris in many markets. The Game Boy family went on to sell well over 100 million units. Yokoi died in 1997; years later the Wii (2006) applied the same thinking, using an inexpensive motion controller and modest graphics hardware to bring in people who had never played games.

MakerNintendo
DesignerGunpei Yokoi and Nintendo R&D1 (Game Boy)
YearGame Boy 1989; Wii 2006
CategoryHandheld and home game consoles
Study it forMature tech, battery and price as features, play first
  1. A monochrome reflective screen. Mature, cheap LCD technology with no backlight. It drew little power and kept the price down. Cost: blur on fast motion, hard to see in poor light, no colour, criticised at launch as outdated.
  2. The cross-shaped directional pad. Yokoi's team had already used the "plus" pad on Game & Watch handhelds. A flat, thin, durable control that the thumb operates without looking, far better than a small joystick in a pocket device.
  3. Two action buttons, angled. A and B sit at a diagonal matching the natural arc of the right thumb. Few buttons kept games simple to learn.
  4. A battery light. A small red LED by the screen dims as batteries run low. One honest indicator of the one resource that mattered.
  5. Four AA batteries for many hours. Battery life was a headline feature: a long journey's worth of play, with batteries available in any shop. Rivals with colour screens needed more batteries and lasted a fraction as long.
  6. Cartridges and a killer game. Tetris, bundled in many markets, was perfect for a small monochrome screen and short sessions. Hardware and software designed for each other.
  7. A link cable for play together. Two Game Boys connected for two-player games. A cheap cable created a social feature, years before online play.

What it left out

Colour, a backlight, a TV tuner add-on as a core feature, extra buttons, and the race for specifications. The Wii, later, left out high-definition graphics (at launch) and the complicated many-button controller that intimidated non-players.

Limits and criticism

The screen really was poor in dim light, spawning an industry of clip-on lights and magnifiers. "Withered technology" can become an excuse for being behind; Nintendo later had periods where hardware lagged too far. The Wii's motion controls were imprecise for some games and novelty faded for many owners.

Steal this for your software

  • Use boring technology. A proven database, a server-rendered web app, plain HTML forms, a mature framework. Mature tech is cheap to run, well documented and rarely breaks at 3 a.m. Spend your novelty on the product, not the stack.
  • Compete on the column you can win. You can't beat big companies on features. You can beat them on speed, price, battery, privacy, offline use, simplicity. Pick one or two and make them headline features.
  • Show the one resource that matters. Like the battery light: storage left, credits left, words left. One honest indicator, not a dashboard.
  • Design software and "hardware" together. If your product runs on phones, design for one-thumb sessions of two minutes, like Tetris for the Game Boy.
  • Play first. Get people doing the fun or useful thing in the first minute, before accounts and settings. The Wii's tennis took seconds to understand.

Do

  • Pick technology you understand deeply.
  • Choose and advertise the trade-off you win.
  • Get people to the core experience in under a minute.

Don't

  • Adopt new tech for its own sake.
  • Compete on feature count with funded teams.
  • Gate the first experience behind sign-up and setup.

The Swiss railway clock: legibility and system behaviour

Taught by Hans Hilfiker (SBB)

A station clock designed to be read from a moving train or across a platform: black bars on white, no numerals, and a red second hand shaped like the guard's signalling disc that sweeps round, pauses at the top, and waits for the signal from the master clock before the minute moves on.

Hans Hilfiker, an engineer at Swiss Federal Railways (SBB), designed the clock in 1944. The red second hand was added in 1953. Its tip is a disc like the Kelle, the signalling paddle a station guard raised to let a train go. Every clock in the network was driven by a central master clock sending a pulse each minute. The second hand is made to go round slightly faster than a minute, in about 58 to 59 seconds, then pause at twelve until the pulse arrives, so all the clocks restart together and the minute hand jumps. Swiss watchmaker Mondaine produces licensed watches and clocks in the design, and in 2012 Apple, after using a strikingly similar face in the iPad Clock app in iOS 6, reached a licensing agreement with SBB.

MakerSwiss Federal Railways (SBB CFF FFS)
DesignerHans Hilfiker
Year1944; red second hand 1953
CategoryStation clock, networked
Study it forLegibility at distance, synchronised system behaviour, one accent
  1. Bars, not numerals. Thick black bars for hours and thinner ones for minutes read faster at distance and from any language than small numbers. Everyone knows where three o'clock is. Cost: precise reading of a single minute takes a moment longer up close.
  2. Black hands shaped as simple bars. No ornament, no tapering tips; maximum contrast and weight on white. The hour hand is wider and shorter, the minute hand longer.
  3. A single red accent, shaped like a signal. The second hand is the only colour, and its disc tip borrows the guard's signalling paddle: a cultural sign of "departure" built into the clock. Cost: none worth naming; it's one of the best accent decisions in design history.
  4. A white face with a black rim. Maximum contrast in daylight on open platforms. The rim frames the clock against busy backgrounds.
  5. Stop-to-go: sweep, pause, release. Because every clock waits for the master pulse, small mechanical differences never accumulate. The pause is visible, and it makes the system's synchronisation something you can see. Passengers learn that the minute changes exactly when the red hand restarts.
  6. One design everywhere. Every station, every platform, the same clock. You learn to read it once. The clock becomes part of the network's identity and trust.
Lab: three ways to move a second hand

Press Run (the clock starts paused if your system asks for reduced motion). Watch the top of the face in stop-to-go mode: the red hand arrives early, waits, and leaves with the minute. Ticking is busy; a smooth sweep never tells you when the minute turns. Which one would you trust to catch a train?

What it left out

Numerals, brand names on the face, decorative hands, a date, and independent timekeeping. Each clock gave up being accurate on its own in exchange for being exactly as accurate as all the others.

Limits and criticism

Without numerals the face is slightly slower for children and for anyone who learned to read time digitally. The stop-to-go mechanism only makes sense inside a synchronised network; a standalone watch that pauses is charming, not functional. And the design is so recognisable that its use without permission became a legal matter.

Steal this for your software

  • One source of truth, visibly synced. All devices and views should agree, and say so ("Synced just now"). If they can't agree, show which is newer rather than pretending.
  • Make system behaviour visible. The pause at twelve shows the network working. A subtle "updating" state before refreshed numbers appear, or a timestamp on live data, does the same.
  • Design for the distance it's read from. A status page, a TV dashboard, a watch face: test at the real viewing distance and speed. Bars and big shapes beat small text.
  • An accent with cultural meaning. If your accent can borrow a meaning users already have (red for stop or live, green for go), it does double work.
  • Same design everywhere. Your timestamp format, status badge and date picker should be identical on every screen. Consistency is how strangers learn your system once.

Do

  • Show "last updated" on any live data.
  • Test glanceable screens at real distance.
  • Use a single, consistent format for time across the product.

Don't

  • Let devices quietly disagree.
  • Animate continuously when a discrete change is the information.
  • Use a different status style on each screen.

For the solo creator

OXO Good Grips peeler: design for the hardest hands

Taught by Sam Farber, with Smart Design

A vegetable peeler with a fat, soft, oval handle and flexible fins for the fingers, designed because the founder's wife had arthritis and couldn't grip a thin metal peeler comfortably. Everyone else found it better too.

Sam Farber had retired from the cookware business when he watched his wife Betsey struggle to hold a standard peeler because of arthritis in her hands. He worked with the New York consultancy Smart Design to create a range of kitchen tools with large, soft, non-slip handles. OXO launched in 1990 with a small set of Good Grips tools, the peeler among them. The handle is made of a soft, rubbery plastic (Santoprene), oval so it doesn't turn in the hand, with ribbed flexible fins where thumb and finger press. It's a textbook case of what's now often called the curb-cut effect: design for people with the greatest need and you often improve the product for everyone.

MakerOXO
DesignersSam Farber (founder) with Smart Design
Year1990
CategoryKitchen utensil
Study it forInclusive design, extreme users, the curb-cut effect
  1. A large-diameter handle. A wider handle spreads force over more of the hand and needs less grip strength to control. Cost: takes more drawer space and looked clumsy to some buyers at first.
  2. An oval cross-section. A round handle rotates in a weak grip; an oval one stays aligned, so the blade angle stays where you put it.
  3. Flexible fins where the fingers press. Soft ribbed fins give under the thumb and forefinger, providing grip even with wet hands. The handle meets the hand rather than the reverse.
  4. A soft, non-slip material. Santoprene is comfortable, grippy when wet and dishwasher-safe. Cost: more expensive than plain hard plastic or metal.
  5. A hanging hole. A large hole at the end lets you hang it, and it doubles as a visual end-stop. Small, practical, everyday.
  6. A sharp swivelling blade. All the grip comfort is wasted if the blade is dull. Inclusive design still needs excellent core function.

What it left out

The assumption that "assistive" products must look medical. OXO tools are sold as ordinary, attractive kitchen tools; nothing marks them as being "for" anyone. It also left out thinness as a value: many classic utensils prized slim metal elegance over comfort.

Limits and criticism

Big soft handles take up more space and cost more to make. The look, widely copied, became a generic "ergonomic" style that sometimes adds rubber without the research. And for some severe disabilities even a good mainstream tool isn't enough; inclusive design complements, not replaces, specialist assistive products.

Steal this for your software

  • Design for the extreme user first. Test with a screen reader, with 200% text, one-handed, with a slow network. Fix those and the average experience improves.
  • Make the handle big. Tap targets at least 44 by 44 points (iOS) or 48 by 48 dp (Android), with spacing between them.
  • Don't let it rotate in the hand. Stable layouts that don't shift as content loads (no layout jumps); controls that stay where they were.
  • Grip when wet. Forgiving inputs: accept many date formats, trim spaces, tolerate typos in search, undo mistakes.
  • No "assistive" ghetto. Don't build a separate "accessible version". Make the main product good for everyone.

Do

  • Recruit or simulate users with the greatest need.
  • Raise minimum sizes and contrast across the board.
  • Keep core function excellent; comfort can't excuse a dull blade.

Don't

  • Treat accessibility as a final checklist.
  • Create a separate, lesser version for disabled users.
  • Prioritise slimness and elegance over use.

In the wild

  • Captions and subtitlesAcross streaming and social video
    Built for deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers, now widely used by people watching in noisy places or quietly in public. The curb-cut effect in software.
  • Dynamic Type on iOSApple, 2013

  • Microsoft's Inclusive Design toolkitMicrosoft, 2016
    Frames disability as permanent, temporary and situational (one arm, an injured arm, a parent holding a baby). Extreme users stand in for many everyday situations.
For the solo creator

Harry Beck's Tube map and Vignelli's New York: the truth that matters

Taught by Harry Beck, with Massimo Vignelli and Bob Noorda (Unimark)

A transit map doesn't need to show where things are. It needs to show how to get from one to another. Beck's 1931 London Underground diagram threw away geography to show connections; Vignelli's 1972 New York map pushed the idea further and met the limits of how far you can push it.

Harry Beck was an engineering draughtsman who, in his spare time in 1931, drew the Underground as a diagram: lines only horizontal, vertical or at 45 degrees, stations evenly spaced, the crowded centre enlarged and the sparse suburbs compressed. London Transport published it in 1933 after initial scepticism, and passengers took to it at once. In New York, Massimo Vignelli and Bob Noorda of Unimark created a signage system for the subway from the late 1960s, codified in the 1970 Graphics Standards Manual: consistent sans-serif lettering, coloured route discs and a black band at the top of each sign. Vignelli's 1972 diagrammatic subway map was beautiful and logical but drew complaints for its geographic liberties (a beige sea, a square Central Park); it was replaced in 1979 by a more geographic map.

MakersLondon Transport; New York City Transit Authority
DesignersHarry Beck; Massimo Vignelli and Bob Noorda (Unimark)
YearBeck 1931, published 1933; NYC signage manual 1970; Vignelli map 1972
CategoryInformation design, wayfinding
Study it forAbstraction, choosing which truth to keep, systems of signs
Same network, two maps (a fictional city)
Geographic: accurate, and almost useless for the question "how do I get from Docks to Airport?". The centre is crowded, interchanges are ambiguous, and distances that don't matter dominate.
Diagram: Docks to Central on blue, change, red to Airport. Distance is gone; connection is everything. Beck's trade: give up one truth (geography) to make another (topology) obvious.
  1. Map: only three angles. Beck restricted lines to horizontal, vertical and 45 degrees. The eye follows straight lines effortlessly; the diagram reads as ordered. Cost: real routes curve, and some distortions mislead walkers.
  2. Map: even spacing and an enlarged centre. Stations are spaced for legibility, not distance. The dense centre gets room; the outskirts shrink. Cost: two stations that look far apart may be a short walk.
  3. Map: interchanges made obvious. Distinct symbols where lines meet make changing trains the visual focus, because that's the decision travellers make.
  4. Signs: a band and a fixed order. In the New York system, signs share a consistent structure (the band, then route symbols, then words, then direction), so you learn where to look once.
  5. Signs: routes as coloured discs. Each route gets a symbol and a colour, used identically on maps, signs and trains. Colour is backed by number or letter, so it still works without colour.
  6. Signs: one plain sans-serif. Consistent, legible lettering across the system (at first a typeface called Standard, later Helvetica). Uniformity is how a chaotic system looks trustworthy.
  7. Signs: arrows and information at the decision point. Signs say what you need where you need to choose, not before or after.
  8. Map (Vignelli 1972): abstraction pushed further. Every station a dot, every line a clean coloured stroke, the city reduced to a diagram. Cost: New Yorkers know their streets and parks, and the map's geography felt wrong. It was replaced within seven years.

What they left out

Beck left out geography, scale, streets and most landmarks except the river. The New York signs left out the decades of mixed lettering and ad-hoc notices that had grown on the system. Vignelli's map left out almost all geography, which was one step too far for that city.

Limits and criticism

Diagrams shape behaviour: people ride trains between stations that are quicker to walk because the map makes them look far apart. Vignelli's map shows the limit of abstraction: you can remove the truth people don't need, but not the truth they already hold strongly (here, the shape of their own city). Later maps of both systems have negotiated between diagram and geography ever since.

Steal this for your software

  • Choose the question, then the truth to keep. A dashboard, settings page or onboarding flow should show the relationships people decide with, not every fact. Write the question first.
  • Make the decision points the visual focus. Interchanges in a map; in software, the step where users choose a plan, a path or an option.
  • Few angles, few sizes. Constrain your layout grid and type sizes like Beck's three angles. Order reads as trustworthy.
  • One sign system. Status badges, labels and icons used identically everywhere, in a fixed order, backed by text. Learn once, use everywhere.
  • Don't abstract away what users already know. If people have a strong mental model (their calendar, their folder structure, their city), respect it. Abstraction that fights a known truth loses.

Do

  • Name the decision a diagram supports before drawing it.
  • Back colour codes with letters, numbers or labels.
  • Put information at the point of decision.

Don't

  • Show everything because it's accurate.
  • Abstract away a model users already hold strongly.
  • Let each screen invent its own labels and badges.

The final exercise

Exercise 20.1 2 hours over two days · an object on your desk and your own product

Break down, translate, ship

  1. Choose. Pick an object on your desk you use daily and have never thought about (a charger, a mug, a notebook, a lamp, a keyboard).
  2. Use it. Do its job three times, slowly, noting every touch, glance and sound.
  3. Compare. Find a cheaper or different version (in a shop, online) to make absences visible.
  4. Break down. Fill the one-page template from chapter 1: seven lenses, five to eight numbered decisions with reasons and costs, one criticism.
  5. Draw it. A schematic sketch with numbered pins matching your decisions.
  6. Translate. Write three translations in the master-table format: physical lesson, software pattern, where in your product.
  7. Ship one. Build the smallest of the three in your product within the week.
  8. Reflect. After a week of use, write three sentences: what changed, what it cost, what you'd do next.
You're done when: you have a one-page breakdown with a pinned sketch, three translation rows, one shipped change, and a three-sentence reflection.

Wrap-up

Ten things to remember

  1. A breakdown names decisions, reasons and costs. If you can't name the cost, you haven't understood the decision.
  2. Translate the reason, re-impose useful constraints as budgets, leave the surface behind.
  3. Size and place controls by frequency of use (T3, iPod, Kindle).
  4. Show state at rest: through clear lids, dials, chips and timestamps (SK 4, Leica, railway clock).
  5. Colour by role; spend one accent, on the live or completing thing (ET 66, Lubs clocks, Hilfiker).
  6. Borrow behaviour people already have; let opening the app be the pull (MUJI CD player, pull-to-refresh).
  7. Aim for "this will do": excellent defaults, few settings, room for the user (MUJI, Super Normal).
  8. Design the system's fixed interface and keep your promises for decades (606, transit signage).
  9. Use mature technology and compete on the column you can win (Game Boy).
  10. Design for the hardest hands and the hardest conditions; everyone benefits (OXO, Lamy, railway clock, Beck).
