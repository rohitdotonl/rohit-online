Motion & Feel. How things move, and why it matters more than how they look.

Motion is the part of an interface you cannot see in a screenshot. It tells people where things came from, what caused what, and whether the app heard them. This guide teaches you to time, ease, choreograph and build motion that explains instead of decorates, and to make a product feel fast, calm and physical even when you are the only person building it.

Part one

Why things move

Before any curve or millisecond, you need a reason. These two chapters give you a test for whether a piece of motion earns its place, and a vocabulary borrowed from a century of animators and a decade of interface designers.

1

What motion is for

Taught by Mike Matas, with Apple's Human Interface Guidelines and Material Design

Good interface motion carries information the static screen cannot: where something came from, where it went, what caused it, and whether the system heard you. If removing an animation loses no information, it was decoration.

A screen is a series of states. Between any two states, something changed. Without motion, the change is a cut: one frame shows the old state, the next frame shows the new one, and the person has to compare them to work out what happened. That comparison costs attention. With well-designed motion, the change is shown instead of implied. The eye follows the object, and the brain gets the answer for free, through the same visual system that tracks a ball thrown across a room.

This is why motion is not a garnish you add at the end. It is part of the structure. When Mike Matas designed interfaces at Apple (he worked on the original iPhone and iPad software), then at Push Pop Press and on Facebook Paper (2014), his work was admired less for being flashy than for being legible: you always knew where a photo came from because it grew out of the thumbnail you touched, and you could push it back with your finger. The motion was the explanation.

The six jobs

Almost every useful animation in software does one or more of these six jobs. Learn the list; it is the test you will apply to every motion decision for the rest of your career.

Continuity
An object keeps its identity across a change. A thumbnail grows into the full photo, so you know it is the same photo. Without continuity, the person has to re-find things after every change.
Orientation
Motion builds a mental map of where screens live. If drilling in slides content from the right, then "back" must slide it to the right again. The map is only as good as its consistency.
Causality
Motion links an action to its result, especially when they are far apart. Add to cart, and the item visibly travels to the cart icon. Delete a row, and its neighbors close the gap so you see what moved.
Feedback
The system acknowledges input instantly, before the result is ready: the button depresses, the switch slides, the field shakes. This is the job most often skipped, and the one people notice most when it is missing.
Attention
Motion is the strongest attention signal in vision; our peripheral vision is tuned to it. One moving element on a still screen will be seen. Two moving elements fight. Use it for things that truly need eyes now.
Personality
The way something moves says a lot about its character. A crisp, quick settle feels precise. A soft spring feels friendly. Personality is real, but it is the last job, never the first.

Explains versus decorates

Here is the test. Imagine the animation replaced with an instant cut. Then ask: what does the person no longer know? If the answer is "where the item went" or "that my tap registered", the motion is doing work. If the answer is "nothing, but it looked nice", it is decoration. Decoration is not always wrong: a small moment of delight on a rare event (finishing a project, the first launch) can be worth it. But decoration on a frequent path is a tax. People pay it every time, and after the tenth time it stops being charming and starts being slow.

Key idea

Motion is information delivered through the visual system's fastest channel. Spend it on explanation; ration it for delight.

Do

  • Name the job (continuity, orientation, causality, feedback, attention, personality) before you design any animation.
  • Animate the thing that changed, from where it was to where it went.
  • Give every user input a visible response within one frame or two.
  • Keep delight for rare moments and let people skip it.

Don't

  • Add motion because a screen "feels static". Still is good; still is calm.
  • Animate things that did not change (a whole page bouncing in when one value updated).
  • Use motion to hide slowness. A long transition over a slow load just makes the wait longer.
  • Let two things compete for attention by moving at the same time for different reasons.

For the solo creator

You do not have time to animate everything, and you should not. Your best return is feedback (press states, toggles, instant responses) and continuity on the two or three transitions people do most. That is perhaps a day of work. Character animation and delight come later, if ever.

Common mistakes

Motion as polish, added last

Why it happens: animation feels like a finishing touch, so it lands in the last week, after the structure is fixed, and becomes decoration layered on cuts.

Fix: decide the spatial model and the key transitions while you design structure. Sketch where each screen "lives" and how you get there (chapter 7).

Animating to look modern

Why it happens: you have seen a polished launch video and want that feeling. Then every card fades and slides on every load.

Fix: run the cut-to-zero test. Keep only motion that loses information when removed, plus one or two rare delights.

Silent inputs

Why it happens: the button calls an API and the screen changes when the response arrives, so nothing happens for 300 to 800ms after the tap.

Fix: separate acknowledgement from result. Show the press state immediately; show the result when it arrives (chapter 10).

Check yourself

Your notes app fades every note in the list from 0 to 100% opacity, one after another, each time the app opens. People open it 20 times a day. Which job does this motion do?

a. Nothing moved or changed identity, so there is no continuity. Every note animates, so no single note gets attention. Content appearing is not feedback on an input. Cut to zero, the person loses nothing and gains time on every open.

Borrowed principles: from Disney to UX in Motion

Taught by Frank Thomas & Ollie Johnston, with Issara Willenskomer, Val Head and Rachel Nabors

Animators spent a century learning what makes movement believable, and interface designers spent a decade learning which of those lessons survive on a screen people are trying to use. Borrow both vocabularies, but keep only what serves the person, not the show.

In 1981, two of Disney's senior animators, Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston, published The Illusion of Life. In it they set out the twelve principles the studio had worked out since the 1930s for making drawings feel alive: squash and stretch, anticipation, staging, straight ahead and pose to pose, follow through and overlapping action, slow in and slow out, arcs, secondary action, timing, exaggeration, solid drawing, and appeal. Almost every motion designer since has learned them.

But a cartoon and an interface have opposite goals. A cartoon wants you to watch. An interface wants you to finish your task and forget it was there. So the principles translate unevenly. Some carry over almost directly (slow in and slow out is just easing). Some need to shrink by a factor of ten (exaggeration). And some, like squash and stretch on a text field, would be ridiculous.

Two teachers worth reading in full

Val Head's Designing Interface Animation (2016) is the most practical book on choosing motion for interfaces: purpose, timing, easing and how to talk about animation with others (including your future self). She also wrote one of the first widely read pieces on motion sensitivity, "Designing Safer Web Animation for Motion Sensitivity" (A List Apart, 2015), which shaped how the industry thinks about reduced motion. Rachel Nabors' Animation at Work (2017) is short and sharp on why animation helps cognition, and on how to make it part of a design system instead of a one-off. Both treat animation as a tool for understanding, which is the stance of this whole guide.

Animation is not the art of drawings that move but the art of movements that are drawn.Norman McLaren, animator, National Film Board of Canada

McLaren's line is about film, but it fits interfaces well. The question is never "what should this look like while it moves?" It is "what is the movement, and what does it say?" Design the movement first; the visuals ride on it.

Do

  • Use slow in and slow out, timing, staging and offset on almost everything.
  • Use anticipation and follow-through at a tenth of film scale.
  • Name the UX in Motion principle when you describe an animation in a spec: "cloning from the plus button, 250ms, ease-out".

Don't

  • Squash and stretch interface chrome. Buttons are not rubber balls.
  • Exaggerate on frequent paths. What reads as charming in a demo reads as wobbly by the hundredth use.
  • Treat a principle as a requirement. Most screens need two or three, not twelve.

Timing: how long things take

Taught by Material Design's motion guidance, with Walter Doherty, Val Head and Jakob Nielsen

Most interface transitions should last between 150 and 300 milliseconds. Go shorter for small, nearby and frequent changes; go longer, up to about 500ms, only for large, far-traveling and rare ones. When in doubt, go shorter.

Duration is the first thing people feel and the last thing they can name. Too short and the eye cannot follow: the change reads as a jump, and continuity is lost. Too long and the person notices they are waiting for your animation to finish before they can act. Between those two failures there is a fairly narrow band, and it depends on three things: how far the element travels, how big it is, and how often people see it.

Why the band is where it is

A display at 60Hz draws a frame every 16.7ms. An animation of 100ms is only six frames: enough to register that something moved, not enough to see much of the path. At 200ms you get twelve frames at 60Hz (twenty-four on a 120Hz screen), which is enough for the eye to track an object smoothly. Beyond about 400 to 500ms, a transition starts to feel like a wait. That boundary is not arbitrary: in 1982, Walter Doherty and Ahrvind Thadani at IBM reported that people's productivity rose sharply when system response time fell below about 400ms, because they stayed in a flow of work instead of drifting while waiting. The "Doherty threshold" is about system response, not animation, but the same human clock is ticking in both cases. Your animation is part of the response time.

Rule 1: distance and size

An element that travels across a full screen needs more time than one that slides 20 pixels, or it will seem to teleport. A large surface (a full-screen sheet) needs more time than a small chip, because there is more to look at and more pixels changing. Material's older guidance put mobile transitions mostly between 200 and 300ms, made desktop transitions shorter (around 150 to 200ms, because pointer movements are quick and screens are seen at arm's length), and suggested longer durations on tablets, where elements travel further. You do not need precise rules; you need the habit of asking "how far, how big?"

Distance and duration do not scale in proportion, though. Doubling the distance does not mean doubling the time; it means adding a little. A sheet that rises 300px and a sheet that rises 700px might be 250ms and 320ms. If you scale linearly, long moves become sluggish.

Rule 2: frequency

This rule matters more than the first. Every animation is a tax paid each time it plays. A 300ms transition on an action people do twice a day is free. The same 300ms on an action people do 200 times a day (switching tabs, opening a command palette, moving between emails) is a minute of waiting, every day, made of tiny frustrations. So the more often something happens, the shorter its animation should be, down to zero.

Keyboard-triggered actions deserve special care. Someone pressing ⌘K to open a command palette, or J to move to the next email, has already decided; they want the result, not a show. macOS Spotlight appears essentially instantly for this reason. Fast email clients built around shortcuts move between messages without slide transitions. If you animate at all, keep it to a short fade under 100ms that never blocks the next keypress.

Rule 3: never block input

An animation that ignores taps while it plays turns duration into lag. If a person taps "back" during a 300ms push, the back should happen now, reversing from wherever the animation is, not after it finishes. This is called interruptibility, and it is the main reason springs (chapter 5) became the default on modern platforms.

Common mistakes

The slow-motion trap

Why it happens: you watch an animation over and over while building it, get used to it, and keep lengthening it to "see it better".

Fix: set the duration, then do the real task ten times in a row at full speed. If you notice the animation by the fifth time, it is too long.

One duration for everything

Why it happens: a library default (often 300ms or 400ms) gets applied globally.

Fix: map your transitions to the three-step scale by size and frequency.

Animations queueing up

Why it happens: each tap starts a new animation that waits for the previous one, so fast tappers see the app replay their history.

Fix: retarget the running animation to the new end state instead of queueing (springs and the Web Animations API both support this).

Check yourself

In your email app, moving to the next message with the J key uses a 300ms horizontal slide. Power users press it hundreds of times a day. What should you do?

d. Frequency beats everything here. A keyboard user has already decided, so orientation is cheap to provide in other ways (a position counter, a highlighted row). Touch swipes are different: the finger is moving the content, so the motion is the interaction itself.

Easing: how things speed up and slow down

Taught by Thomas & Johnston ("slow in, slow out"), with Robert Penner, Val Head and the CSS Working Group

Nothing in the physical world starts or stops instantly, so constant-speed motion looks mechanical. Use ease-out (fast start, gentle stop) for things that enter or respond, ease-in (gentle start, fast finish) for things that leave, and ease-in-out for things that move from one place to another on screen.

An easing curve maps time to progress. The horizontal axis is time, from 0 (start) to 1 (end). The vertical axis is how far along the movement is. A straight diagonal line is linear: equal progress in equal time. Any curve that bends is easing. Where the curve is steep, the object is moving fast; where it is flat, it is moving slowly. That one idea lets you read any easing curve at a glance.

In the early 2000s the designer and developer Robert Penner published a set of easing equations (quad, cubic, quint, sine, expo, back, elastic, bounce) for Flash animation. They spread into nearly every animation library since, which is why you will see names like easeOutCubic everywhere. On the web and in most design tools, easing is usually expressed as a cubic Bézier curve with two control points, written cubic-bezier(x1, y1, x2, y2). The first point shapes the start; the second shapes the end.

Why ease-out for entering

When you tap something, you want a response now. Ease-out moves the most in the first few frames: by a third of the duration, a typical ease-out curve has covered well over half the distance. So the element appears to react instantly, then slows down so the eye can land on it and read it. This also mirrors physics: a thrown or pushed object starts at speed and is slowed by friction.

Why ease-in for exiting

An element that leaves is no longer interesting. Ease-in starts slowly (so the departure is visible and the person sees where it went) and then accelerates away, like a car pulling off. Exits are also usually shorter than entrances, often by 20 to 30%. Material's older guidance, for example, used about 225ms for elements entering and about 195ms for elements leaving on mobile. The asymmetry is a feature: we care about arrivals more than departures.

Why ease-in-out for moving on screen

When an element is visible both before and after (a card moving to a new slot, a panel resizing), it should start gently, move, and land gently. Pure ease-in-out can feel a little slow to start, so many systems use an asymmetric "standard" curve that accelerates quickly and decelerates for longer. Material's long-standing standard curve, cubic-bezier(0.4, 0, 0.2, 1), is that shape.

Myth

"Ease-in-out is the safe default." It is the safest for elements moving on screen, but the worst common choice for responses to input, because its first 20% of time covers very little distance. That slow start reads as lag. Default to ease-out for anything that answers a tap.

Where linear belongs

Linear is not banned; it is specialized. Use it for things that loop or represent time: a spinning loader, a progress bar filling at a known rate, a marquee. Use it for color and opacity changes too, where there is no physical movement to ease (though a short ease-out fade looks fine as well). Avoid it for anything that travels.

Do

  • Ease-out for entrances and input responses.
  • Ease-in, a little shorter, for exits.
  • An asymmetric standard curve for on-screen moves.
  • Linear for loops, progress and pure color.
  • Keep two or three named curves, like your durations.

Don't

  • Use ease-in for anything that answers a tap.
  • Use bounce and elastic easings in productivity UI.
  • Use the browser default ease everywhere without deciding.
  • Mix six curves across one screen.

Springs and physics

Taught by Apple's "Designing Fluid Interfaces" team (WWDC 2018), with Facebook's Paper and Origami designers and the SwiftUI team

A spring has no fixed duration. It pulls an element toward a target with a force, and the motion ends when the energy runs out. That makes springs naturally interruptible: they can change target mid-flight and carry their current velocity with them, which a fixed-duration curve cannot do.

A Bézier curve is a timeline: at 40% of the time, be at 70% of the distance. That works perfectly until something interrupts it. Suppose a sheet is halfway up and moving fast when the person flicks it down. A timeline animation has two bad options: finish going up first (ignoring the person), or start a new animation downward from zero velocity, which makes the sheet stop dead and then start again. Either way, the motion has a visible kink. In the real world, a moving object you push the other way slows down, reverses and accelerates; its velocity changes smoothly.

A spring simulation models exactly that. At every frame it asks: how far am I from the target, and how fast am I moving? The spring force pulls toward the target in proportion to the distance; the damping force resists motion in proportion to the speed. Change the target, and the same simulation just keeps running from the current position and velocity. No kink.

The three knobs, and the one that matters

Stiffness (k) is the strength of the pull. Higher stiffness means faster motion. Damping (c) is friction. Higher damping means less bounce and, past a point, slower settling. Mass (m) is inertia. Higher mass makes the object slower to start and slower to stop, as if it were heavier.

Designers rarely think in those raw numbers. The useful derived quantity is the damping ratio, usually written ζ (zeta): ζ = c ÷ (2 × √(k × m)). When ζ = 1, the spring is critically damped: it reaches the target as fast as possible without overshooting. Below 1 it overshoots and oscillates; the lower, the bouncier. Above 1 it creeps in slowly. For most interface motion you want ζ between about 0.8 and 1: a hint of liveliness, or none, and no visible wobble.

Apple's designers, in the WWDC 2018 talk "Designing Fluid Interfaces", recommended thinking in two designer-friendly parameters instead of three physical ones: damping ratio (how bouncy) and response (roughly how quickly it gets there). SwiftUI follows the same idea; since iOS 17 you can write a spring as a perceived duration and a bounce, and use presets named .smooth (no bounce), .snappy (a little) and .bouncy (more). You still get a physically correct spring, but you specify it in words a designer would use.

Duration-based or spring?

Use a spring when the motion is connected to a gesture, can be interrupted, or is triggered by something the person might immediately reverse (open and close, expand and collapse). Use a duration and curve when the motion is short, not interactive and needs to be exactly synchronized with something else, like a crossfade that must end at a specific time, or a loading progress indicator. Most modern platforms default to springs for interactive transitions; SwiftUI's default animation became a spring in iOS 17.

On the web, CSS transitions are timeline-based, but you can fake a spring with the linear() easing function, which accepts many points and can approximate any curve (including a spring's settle). It is still a timeline, so it will not preserve velocity when interrupted. For real interruptible springs on the web, use a library such as Motion (chapter 14) or a small simulation like the one in the lab above, which is about 30 lines of JavaScript.

Key idea

A spring is defined by forces, not time. That is why it can be interrupted and still feel physical. Keep the damping ratio near 1 for serious UI.

Do

  • Use springs for anything gesture-driven or reversible.
  • Specify springs as damping ratio plus response (or duration plus bounce), not raw stiffness numbers.
  • Pass the gesture's release velocity into the spring.
  • Keep bounce near zero for productivity, a little for playful touch moments.

Don't

  • Use bouncy springs on text and dense layouts; wobbling text is hard to read.
  • Chain timeline animations to fake interruption.
  • Let a spring take a second to settle a 1px wobble; check the settle time.

Direct manipulation: following the finger

Taught by Bas Ording, with Ben Shneiderman and Apple's fluid interfaces team

When a person drags something, it must stay under their finger, exactly, every frame. At the edges, it should resist rather than stop. When they let go, it should keep the speed they gave it. Those three rules (1:1 tracking, rubber-banding, momentum) are what make a touch interface feel like a physical object instead of a picture of one.

Ben Shneiderman coined "direct manipulation" in 1983 to describe interfaces where you act on visible objects with continuous, reversible actions and see the results immediately. Touch screens made the idea literal. And the moment the finger is on the glass, the bar for motion rises. With a mouse, a few milliseconds of lag hide in the gap between hand and pointer. With a finger, any lag is visible as the content sliding away from your fingertip, and the illusion breaks.

Rule 1: track 1:1, with no smoothing

During a drag, set the element's position from the pointer position directly. Do not ease toward it, do not animate to it, do not smooth it. Smoothing is tempting because it hides jittery input, but it turns into lag: the content trails the finger like it is on a rubber leash. Also preserve the grab point: if the person grabbed the card 30px from its left edge, it should stay 30px from its left edge under the finger, rather than jumping to center itself.

Rule 2: resist at the edges (rubber-banding)

What should happen when you drag a list past its end? A hard stop feels broken: your finger keeps moving and the content refuses, so you wonder whether the app froze. The iPhone answer, credited to Apple designer Bas Ording and central to one of the patents in Apple's 2012 case against Samsung, is rubber-banding: the content keeps moving, but with increasing resistance, so it travels a fraction of your finger's distance and the fraction shrinks the further you pull. Let go, and it springs back. You learn the boundary physically, without an error message.

A widely shared approximation of the iOS curve, reverse-engineered by developers, is offset = (1 − 1 ÷ (distance × 0.55 ÷ size + 1)) × size, where size is the height of the view. It starts close to 1:1 for small pulls and flattens toward a limit for big ones. The exact constant matters less than the shape.

Three ways to scroll a list

Reduced motion is on. Dragging still tracks your pointer (that is direct manipulation, not decorative motion), but momentum and spring-back are replaced with an instant settle.

Drag the list up and down with a mouse or finger, past the top and bottom, and flick it.

Hard stop: it tracks your finger, but at the ends the content freezes while your finger keeps going, and a flick stops dead at the edge.
Smoothed: the content eases toward your finger. It looks "smooth" in a video and feels detached in the hand. No momentum on release.
1:1 + rubber band: exact tracking, resistance past the edges, momentum on release, and a spring back to the boundary. This is the feel people call "native".

Rule 3: keep the momentum, and project it

When the finger lifts, the content is moving at some speed. Throwing that speed away (stopping dead) feels like the content hit glue. Keeping it means letting the content glide and decelerate by friction. iOS scroll views decelerate by multiplying velocity by a constant every millisecond (0.998 at the normal rate, 0.99 at the "fast" rate used for paging-like views).

Apple's fluid interfaces talk added a subtle, powerful idea: projection. When someone flicks a sheet or a picture-in-picture video, do not choose where it lands from where the finger let go. Choose it from where the motion would come to rest, given the release velocity. A gentle flick near the middle should go back; a fast flick from the same position should carry through. This is how the iOS app switcher and PiP window feel like they read your intent.

Do

  • Update position from the raw pointer every frame during a drag.
  • Keep the grab offset.
  • Rubber-band at edges and spring back on release.
  • Project release velocity to choose a snap point.
  • Let any in-flight animation be grabbed and stopped by a new touch.

Don't

  • Smooth or ease the drag position.
  • Hard-stop content at the edge.
  • Start the release animation from zero velocity.
  • Pick snap points by position alone.
  • Build your own scrolling when the platform's will do; native scroll views already do all of this.

Choreography: space, sequence and stagger

Taught by Material Design's choreography guidance, with Issara Willenskomer and Apple's navigation model

When several things move, they need a director. Decide where every screen lives in space, move things in an order that tells one story, and offset related items by a few tens of milliseconds so the eye can follow them as a group without waiting for them.

A spatial model: where screens live

Every navigation animation makes a claim about space. A push from the right says "the new screen was to the right, and you moved deeper". A sheet from the bottom says "this is a temporary layer on top; dismiss it downward". A crossfade says "this replaced that, in the same place". People build a mental map from these claims, and they use it to predict where "back" goes. The map only works if your claims are consistent.

The z-axis deserves a note. A layer above (sheet, popover, dialog) should come from where it is anchored and cast a shadow or dim what is behind, so you know the base is still there underneath (Willenskomer's "obscuration"). It leaves the way it came. If a sheet slides up, it slides down; a sheet that fades out after sliding in breaks the model.

Sequence: one story at a time

When a change involves several elements, order them by cause and effect. The thing the person touched responds first. The thing that results comes next. Supporting elements (counts, badges, secondary content) follow. If everything starts at once, the eye does not know where to look; if the order is wrong, causality reads backwards. Keep the whole sequence short: a choreographed transition that takes longer than about 400 to 500ms in total is usually showing off.

Stagger: related, but separate

When a group of similar items appears (list rows, grid cards, menu items), animating them all together makes the group read as one blob. Animating them one fully after another makes the person wait. Staggering each item's start by a small offset, usually 20 to 50ms, gives the best of both: the eye perceives a wave that reveals the order and grouping, and the whole group is visible within a quarter of a second. Cap the number of staggered items (the first six to ten); anything below the fold should simply appear with the last one, or the stagger becomes a slow drip.

        Stagger 150ms: a slow drip. The last row arrives after about a second. Delightful once, irritating on the twentieth visit.

        Shared elements

        The strongest continuity tool is the shared element transition: an element present on both screens (a photo, a title, an avatar) moves and resizes from its old position to its new one, while the rest of the screen changes around it. The person's eye stays on the thing they tapped, and the new screen assembles around it. Photos opening from a grid, an album cover growing into a player, a card expanding into a detail page: all shared-element transitions. Material calls this the "container transform". On the web, the View Transitions API makes it a few lines of code (chapter 14).

        Do

        • Write down your spatial model: depth, peers, layers.
        • Reverse every transition exactly on the way back.
        • Order motion by cause and effect.

        • Use shared elements when the same object appears on both screens.

        Don't

        • Mix push directions for the same kind of navigation.
        • Use a depth push for switching tabs.
        • Stagger fifty rows.
        • Let total choreography exceed half a second on a common path.

        State changes, morphing and FLIP

        Taught by Paul Lewis (FLIP), with Issara Willenskomer (transformation) and the View Transitions spec authors

        When content reflows (an item is removed, a list is filtered, a card resizes), animate each element from its old position to its new one. The FLIP technique makes this cheap: measure where things were, measure where they are, and animate the difference with transforms.

        Most interface changes are not navigation; they are layout changes within one screen. A row is deleted and the rows below jump up. A filter hides half a grid and the rest snap into new places. A panel opens and the content beside it jumps sideways. Each jump breaks continuity: the person has to find their place again. Animating the reflow fixes that, but it used to be hard, because layout positions are computed by the browser or UI framework, not by you.

        FLIP: First, Last, Invert, Play

        In 2015, Paul Lewis (then at Google) described a technique he called FLIP. It turns any layout change into a smooth animation using only transforms, which are cheap to animate (chapter 14).

        // FLIP for any set of elements (vanilla JS, Web Animations API)
const first = new Map([...items].map(el => [el, el.getBoundingClientRect()]));
applyTheLayoutChange();                       // e.g. reorder, filter, remove
for (const el of items) {
  const a = first.get(el), b = el.getBoundingClientRect();
  const dx = a.left - b.left, dy = a.top - b.top;
  if (!dx && !dy) continue;
  el.animate(
    [{ transform: `translate(${dx}px, ${dy}px)` }, { transform: 'none' }],
    { duration: 260, easing: 'cubic-bezier(.2, 0, 0, 1)' }
  );
}
        Reflow a grid

        Reduced motion is on, so both versions reflow instantly. That instant reflow is also the correct reduced-motion behavior.

        Without: tiles teleport. After a shuffle, try to find tile 5: you have to scan the whole grid.
        With FLIP: each tile travels from its old place to its new one. Your eye can follow any tile you were watching, and filtering shows which tiles closed the gaps.

        Morphing: one thing becoming another

        Transformation (chapter 2) is the UX in Motion name for an element that changes into another element while staying recognizably itself. Icon morphs are the small version: a play triangle becoming a pause pair, a hamburger becoming a close cross, a "Send" button shrinking into a spinner and then a check. They work because the control stays where your eye already is and tells you its new state without a separate message. They fail when the morph is slow (anything over about 250ms for an icon), or when the two states have nothing in common visually, so the in-between frames are a mess.

        Do

        • Animate reflows after insert, delete, filter and sort.
        • Use transforms (FLIP) rather than animating top, left, width or height.
        • Keep icon morphs under about 250ms.
        • Morph between shapes that share geometry.

        Don't

        • Animate layout properties directly on large lists.
        • Animate a reflow the person did not cause (a background sync reshuffling the list while they read).
        • Morph with distortion; FLIP with scale can squash text, so counter-scale children or crossfade.

        In the wild

        Microinteraction motion

        Taught by Dan Saffer, with Apple's and Material's control guidelines

        The smallest motions do the most work: a button that depresses on touch-down, a switch that slides, a field that shakes on a wrong entry, a check that draws itself on success. They are fast (50 to 200ms), they answer the person directly, and they are where most products feel cheap or crafted.

        Dan Saffer's book Microinteractions (2013) breaks every small interaction into a trigger, rules, feedback, and loops and modes. Motion lives mostly in the feedback part: it is how the control says "got it" and "here is the new state". Because microinteractions happen hundreds of times a day, they must be fast, quiet and completely consistent. A good press state is never noticed; a missing one is noticed every time.

        Press: respond on touch-down

        The most important microinteraction is the press state, and the most important rule is when it appears: on touch-down (or mouse-down), not on release. The press state is the acknowledgement. It must happen within a frame or two of contact, before the action runs. A slight darkening, a scale to about 0.96 to 0.98, or both, over 50 to 100ms in, and a slightly slower release. On release, the action fires.

        Toggles, checkboxes and selection

        A switch should slide its knob (about 150 to 200ms, ease-out or a crisp spring) and change color with it, so the new state is unmistakable. A checkbox can draw its check quickly (a 100 to 150ms stroke animation) instead of popping in. Selection in a list (highlighting a row) should be near-instant; it is a very frequent action. None of these should bounce in productivity software. A tiny overshoot in a switch is tolerable; more is noise.

        Success and errors

        Success is usually best shown in place: the button becomes a check, the row gets a brief tint. Avoid modal celebrations for ordinary actions. Errors should also appear where the problem is. The classic motion for "no" is a short horizontal shake, the way a head shakes. macOS has shaken its login password field on a wrong entry for many years. A shake works because it is fast (about 300 to 400ms, three or four oscillations of a few pixels), localized, and universally understood. It must come with a text explanation for anyone who cannot see the motion or has motion turned off.

        Attention cues, used sparingly

        Sometimes you need the person to notice something: a new message, a required field, a feature they have not found.         • Use ripples or bounces that outlast the action.
        • Rely on the shake alone to say "wrong".
        • Loop attention animations until clicked.

        Perceived performance

        Taught by Robert Miller and Jakob Nielsen (response limits), with Luke Wroblewski (skeletons), Mike Krieger (Instagram) and Rahul Vohra (Superhuman)

        How fast software feels matters more than how fast it is. Respond within 100ms, show progress after about a second, keep people oriented past ten seconds, and wherever you can, do the work before or behind the moment instead of in front of it.

        Three numbers have guided interface speed for decades. Robert Miller described them in 1968, and Jakob Nielsen popularized them in Usability Engineering (1993). About 0.1 second is the limit for feeling that the system reacts instantly, as if you were acting on a physical object. About 1 second is the limit for your flow of thought staying uninterrupted, though you notice the delay. About 10 seconds is the limit for keeping attention on the task at all; beyond it, people switch to something else. Doherty's 400ms (chapter 3) sits between the first two.

        Optimistic UI: assume success

        Most actions in most apps succeed. Likes, stars, marking as read, reordering, renaming, checking off a to-do: their failure rate is tiny. So instead of waiting for the server, update the interface immediately as if the action succeeded, send the request in the background, and only if it fails, roll back and say so clearly. The person feels zero latency. In early Instagram, Mike Krieger described a related trick: the photo upload started in the background while people were still writing the caption, so by the time they pressed share, much of the work was done.

        Spinner, skeleton or progress bar?

        When you must wait, pick the indicator by what you know about the wait. A spinner (indeterminate) says "working, no idea how long"; fine for short, unknown waits, but it focuses the eye on the waiting itself. A skeleton screen shows the shape of the content that is coming (grey blocks where text and images will be); the term was popularized by Luke Wroblewski around 2013. Skeletons make waits feel shorter because the page appears to be already loading in place, and because nothing jumps when content arrives. A determinate progress bar says "this much done, this much left"; use it whenever you can estimate progress and the wait exceeds a few seconds, such as uploads, exports and imports.

        Two refinements matter as much as the choice. First, delay the indicator: if most loads finish in under about 300 to 500ms, showing a spinner immediately produces a flash that makes things feel slower. Start it after a short delay. Second, once an indicator appears, keep it for a minimum time (around 300 to 500ms) so it does not flicker on and off.

        Layout shift is when content moves after it first appears because something above it loaded late: an image without dimensions, an ad, a banner, a font swap. Give images and embeds explicit dimensions or an aspect ratio, reserve slots for late content, and use skeletons that match the real layout.

        Haptics and sound

        Taught by Apple's haptics guidelines and Android's haptics design principles

        Haptics and sound are motion you feel and hear. Used well, they confirm a moment the eye might miss (a snap, a success, a boundary). Used badly, they are a buzzing, beeping nuisance. Keep them rare, tied to meaningful events, consistent with what the eye sees, and always optional.

        Since the iPhone 7 (2016) and its Taptic Engine, phones can produce crisp, short taps rather than the old rattling buzz. That changed haptics from an alarm into a design material. A good haptic is like a good press state: you feel a detent when a picker ticks over a value, a soft knock when a sheet snaps into place, a distinct double tap when something succeeds. The best haptics are synchronized exactly with a visual event; a tap that arrives 50ms after the snap feels wrong in the same way as a lagging drag.

        The vocabulary on each platform

        UIKit's UIFeedbackGenerator family (iOS 10 and later) gives three semantic types. Impact (UIImpactFeedbackGenerator) for physical collisions: light, medium and heavy, plus soft and rigid since iOS 13. Selection (UISelectionFeedbackGenerator) for a value ticking over, like a picker wheel. Notification (UINotificationFeedbackGenerator) for outcomes: success, warning, error. Core Haptics (iOS 13) allows custom patterns. In SwiftUI, the .sensoryFeedback modifier (iOS 17) attaches feedback to a value change declaratively.

        // SwiftUI: tick when the selection changes, success when saved
Picker("Size", selection: $size) { … }
  .sensoryFeedback(.selection, trigger: size)
Button("Save") { save() }
  .sensoryFeedback(.success, trigger: savedCount)

        Android recommends View.performHapticFeedback() with semantic HapticFeedbackConstants (such as CLOCK_TICK, KEYBOARD_TAP, LONG_PRESS, and since Android 11 CONFIRM and REJECT) over raw vibration, because it respects the user's settings and adapts to each device's actuator. For richer effects, VibrationEffect compositions use primitives like click and tick on capable hardware. Android's own guidance favors clear, crisp haptics and warns against long, buzzy vibrations for UI feedback.

        // Kotlin: confirm a successful action
view.performHapticFeedback(HapticFeedbackConstants.CONFIRM)

        The web has only navigator.vibrate(), which works in some Android browsers and not in Safari on iOS. It produces coarse buzzes, not crisp taps. Treat it as optional progressive enhancement, never as the only signal, and keep patterns very short (10 to 20ms). For most web apps, the right amount of haptics is none.

        Sound: subtle, synchronized, optional

        Sound is the most intrusive feedback channel: it leaves the device and reaches everyone in the room. So in most software, sound should be reserved for a few events where it truly helps (a message sent, a payment completed, a timer finished), quiet, short, and off when the device is silenced. Good UI sounds share a family: the same timbre and tone, like a typeface for the ear. A sound must be perfectly synchronized with the visual; even small offsets feel like a dubbed film. Always provide a setting to turn sounds off, and never put information only in sound.

        Do

        • Use semantic haptic types and let the platform render them.
        • Fire a haptic exactly at the visual event (snap, threshold, success).
        • Prepare the generator ahead of time to avoid latency (iOS prepare()).
        • Keep sounds short, quiet, from one family, and respect silent mode.

        Don't

        • Vibrate on every tap, row or keystroke of your own invention.
        • Use long buzzes for UI feedback.
        • Use haptics or sound as the only signal.
        • Play sounds on launch or on routine navigation.

        Reduced motion and safe motion

        Taught by Val Head, with Eric Bailey, WCAG and Apple's Reduce Motion setting

        For some people, motion on screen causes dizziness, nausea, migraines or worse. Every operating system lets people ask for less. Honoring that request is not about removing all animation; it is about replacing large spatial motion with gentle alternatives, while keeping the feedback and meaning.

        The vestibular system in the inner ear senses balance and movement. When the eyes report large motion that the inner ear does not feel (a screen zooming toward you, a background sliding at a different speed than the foreground, a whole page swooping sideways), people with vestibular disorders can experience real physical symptoms that last hours. Vestibular problems are not rare: one US study (Agrawal and colleagues, 2009) estimated that about a third of adults over 40 had some evidence of vestibular dysfunction. Motion sensitivity also comes with migraines, concussion recovery, some attention conditions and simply age.

        In 2013, iOS 7 introduced heavy zoom transitions and a parallax home screen. Many people reported feeling sick, and Apple soon added a Reduce Motion setting. Today every major platform has an equivalent: Reduce Motion on iOS and macOS, Remove animations on Android, an animation effects switch on Windows. Browsers expose all of them through one CSS media query, prefers-reduced-motion.

        Reduce, do not remove

        A common mistake is to treat reduced motion as "no motion". That throws away feedback and continuity that everyone needs. The better model: identify which motion is spatial and large (the triggers) and swap it for something calmer, usually a crossfade or an instant change, while keeping small, non-spatial feedback.

        Opening a photo

        Your system has reduced motion on, so both versions open instantly here. That is the right call for you; with motion on, the full version zooms from the tile and the reduced alternative crossfades in place.

        Photo

        Full motion: the photo grows out of the tile you tapped (a shared-element zoom), and shrinks back into it on close. Great continuity, and exactly the kind of large scale change that can trigger symptoms.
        Reduced alternative: a 150ms crossfade in place. You lose some sense of "which tile", so the title names the photo; you keep a calm, clear transition with no movement through space.

        Flashing: the hard limit

        Separate from vestibular sensitivity, flashing content can trigger seizures in people with photosensitive epilepsy. WCAG success criterion 2.3.1 (Level A, the baseline) says content must not flash more than three times in any one-second period, unless the flash is below specific size and brightness thresholds. In practice: never flash. No strobing loaders, no rapidly blinking alerts, no full-screen color flips for celebrations. WCAG 2.2.2 also requires that anything that moves, blinks or scrolls automatically for more than five seconds alongside other content can be paused, stopped or hidden, and 2.3.3 (Level AAA) asks that motion triggered by interaction can be disabled.

        Implementation for one person

        Taught by Paul Lewis and the Chrome rendering team, with Matt Perry (Motion), Airbnb (Lottie) and Rive

        Animate only transform and opacity, finish every frame within its budget (16.7ms at 60Hz, 8.3ms at 120Hz), and pick the simplest tool that does the job: CSS for state changes, the Web Animations API or a spring library for interactive motion, View Transitions for page changes, SwiftUI's built-in springs on Apple platforms, and Lottie or Rive only for illustrative animation.

        Why transform and opacity

        To draw a frame, a browser (and, in similar ways, a native UI framework) goes through stages: compute styles, lay out boxes, paint pixels into layers, and composite the layers onto the screen. Changing width, height, top or margin forces layout, which can ripple through the whole page, then paint, then composite, every frame. Changing transform or opacity on an element with its own layer skips straight to compositing, which the GPU handles cheaply and often off the main thread. That is the difference between a smooth sheet and a stuttering one on a mid-range phone.

        Profiling in ten minutes

        1. Throttle. In Chrome DevTools, set CPU throttling to 4x or 6x slowdown to simulate a mid-range phone.
        2. Record. Use the Performance panel, trigger the animation, stop. Look for long purple (layout) and green (paint) blocks inside the animation.
        3. Show repaints. The Rendering panel's paint flashing highlights what repaints each frame. A sheet sliding should not repaint the page under it.
        4. On iOS, use Instruments (the Animation Hitches template) or simply record the screen at 120fps and count stutters.
        5. Fix by changing what animates, not by adding will-change everywhere. Use will-change: transform only on elements about to animate, and remove it after.

        Do

        • Animate transform and opacity.
        • Use the simplest tool that works: CSS first.
        • Define tokens once and reuse them everywhere.
        • Test on the slowest device you support, throttled.
        • Use Lottie or Rive for illustrations, not for interface transitions.

        Don't

        • Write transition: all.
        • Animate layout properties on large or frequently changing elements.
        • Run animation logic inside a heavy re-render each frame.
        • Ship a 500KB animation file for a loading spinner.

        Motion specs for future-you

        Taught by Rachel Nabors and Val Head, with the Material and Carbon motion systems

        Motion that is not written down drifts. A small set of named tokens (durations, curves, springs) and a one-line spec per transition (trigger, what moves, from and to, timing, reduced-motion alternative) keeps your product feeling like one product, even when the only person on the team is you, months apart.

        Teams write motion specs so designers and engineers agree. As a solo creator you might think you do not need them. But your future self is a different person who has forgotten why the sheet uses 260ms and the menu 180ms. Without a record, each new feature invents its own motion, and within a year the product feels like six apps. Rachel Nabors and Val Head both argue for treating motion as part of the design system rather than one-off decisions; IBM's Carbon design system, for example, documents "productive" motion (quick, efficient) and "expressive" motion (for significant moments) as two named modes.

        Layer 3: a motion principle in one sentence

        Finally, write one sentence that describes how your product moves, and use it to settle arguments with yourself. For a calm reading app: "Motion is quick, quiet and physical: it explains changes and then gets out of the way." For a playful kids' app it might say "springy and generous".         Do

        • Keep tokens in code, as the single source of truth.
        • Spec only the transitions that matter: the top five and anything complex.
        • Include the reduced-motion alternative in every spec line.
        • Write your one-sentence motion principle and keep it next to the tokens.

        Don't

        • Write a forty-page motion guideline for a one-person product.
        • Keep specs only in a design file that drifts from the code.
        • Add a new token for every new animation; reuse first.

        Specs that do not mention exits

        Wrap-up

        Go back to the note you wrote in the warm-up: one moment that felt good and one that felt wrong in your most-used app. You can now name what was happening. Maybe the good moment was a spring carrying your flick's velocity, and the bad one was a menu easing in, a spinner flashing for 200ms, or a list hard-stopping at its end. That shift, from "it feels off" to "the exit is as long as the entrance and uses the wrong curve", is the skill this guide set out to build.

        Ten things to remember

        1. Motion is information. If cutting an animation to zero loses nothing, it was decoration; ration decoration for rare moments.
        2. Six jobs: continuity, orientation, causality, feedback, attention, personality. Personality comes last.
        3. Most UI motion lives between 150 and 300ms. Shorter for small, near and frequent; longer only for large, far and rare. Keyboard actions are instant.
        4. Ease-out for entering and responding, ease-in (shorter) for leaving, a standard curve for moving on screen, linear for loops and progress.
        5. Springs are defined by forces, not time, so they can be interrupted and keep their velocity. Keep the damping ratio near 1.
        6. Under a finger: track 1:1, rubber-band at the edges, keep momentum, and choose the destination by projecting velocity.
        7. Draw a spatial model and keep it: depth pushes right, peers swap in place, layers rise and fall. Back always reverses.
        8. Animate reflows with FLIP; morph controls in place; acknowledge every press on touch-down.
        9. Feeling fast beats being fast: optimistic updates, delayed and held indicators, skeletons, prefetching and zero layout shift.
        10. Reduce, do not remove: swap large spatial motion for fades when people ask, never flash more than three times a second, and write it all down as tokens.
