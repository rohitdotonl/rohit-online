Almost every product shows numbers somewhere: a word count, a streak, a balance, a chart of last month, a table of invoices. Diagnostic 1

Your habit app shows weekly completion as a bar chart. This week's bar is 71%, last week's was 68%. The y-axis starts at 60% so the change looks big and motivating. Is that fine?

People read the length of a bar, not the tick labels. A bar starting at 60% makes 71 look almost four times 68's lead over 60 (11 versus 8 above the baseline), so the visual says "big jump" while the data says "+3 points". Bars need a zero baseline. A line or dot plot could start at 60 honestly, because position, not length, carries the value. You will play with this in chapter 3.

Part one

Software that informs

Most of the software people use exists to answer questions: when is the next train, will it rain, how much did I spend, am I on track. The first two chapters set the philosophy for this kind of software. Show the data. Infer the context. Spend ink, pixels and clicks only where they carry information. Then we look at how graphics lie, and at two of Tufte's most useful inventions for small screens: small multiples and sparklines.

1

Information software and the cost of interaction

Taught by Bret Victor, with Edward Tufte

If people open your software to learn something, it is information software, and its job is to show the answer with as little interaction as possible. Every question the user has to answer is a question you failed to infer.

In 2006 Bret Victor published a long essay called "Magic Ink: Information Software and the Graphical Interface". Its argument is simple and still under-used. He splits software by what the person is trying to do. Manipulation software is for creating and changing things: a text editor, Figma, a photo editor. The user is building a model in their head of the thing they're making, and direct manipulation (drag, resize, type) is the heart of it. Information software is for learning: a weather app, a train timetable, a bank balance, a flight tracker, a search result, a reading list. The user wants to answer a question, compare options and decide. Communication software (email, chat) sits between the two.

Victor's point is that most software is information software, yet it is designed like manipulation software: full of forms, menus, filters and buttons. That is backwards. For information software, the design problem is mainly a graphic design problem. What should be on screen, at what size, in what arrangement, so the answer is visible? Interaction is what you resort to when the graphic cannot know enough.

Interaction is a cost, not a feature

Victor titled one section of the essay "Interaction considered harmful". Not because interaction is bad, but because in information software every click, tap, field and menu is a toll the person pays before they see what they came for. Navigation means they have to know where the answer lives. A form means they have to phrase the question in your terms. A "Submit" button means they cannot see the effect of their choice until they commit to it. Each step also hides the comparison: if you can see only one result at a time, you have to hold the others in memory, which people are bad at (see The Human Mind on working memory).

The alternative is to infer the question from context. Victor names three sources:

  1. Environment. What the device already knows: the time, the date, the location, the calendar, the connected network, the screen size, what's on the clipboard. A transit app that opens at 6:10 pm on a weekday near your office can guess you are going home.
  2. History. What this person did before. If they checked the same route every weekday for a month, show that route first. If they always sort invoices by due date, remember it. History should make the software feel like it has learned, without making it unpredictable.
  3. Interaction. Only what is left. When you do need input, make it reveal results immediately (no submit step), keep the previous answers visible for comparison, and let the input be graphical where the answer is graphical (drag a time slider, not type "18:30").

For the solo creator

Inferring context is cheap to build and expensive to skip. "Remember the last sort order", "open on today", "default to the user's locale and currency", "show the item they edited last" are each a few lines of code. You don't need machine learning: a time-of-day check and a stored preference beat most filter panels. Start every screen sketch by writing the one question the person opens it to answer, then design the graphic that answers it with zero taps.

Do

  • Name the question each information screen answers, in one sentence.
  • Default from environment (time, place, locale) and history (last view, last sort).
  • Make inputs live: results update as the person drags or types, no Submit.
  • Keep alternatives visible side by side so people compare by looking, not remembering.

Don't

  • Open an information screen on an empty form or a menu of reports.
  • Hide the answer behind tabs when it fits on one screen.
  • Ask for anything the device already knows.
  • Make context guesses you can't undo or explain; a wrong guess must be one tap from fixed.

Common mistakes

Designing the controls first

Builders think in database queries, so the screen becomes a query builder: fields, filters, a button.

Sketch the answer first. Ask: if I could only show one graphic and no controls, what would it be? Then add the minimum controls to correct its guesses.

Over-confident inference

A guess that hides the alternatives (auto-switching to "home" when the person is going to the gym) feels creepy and wrong.

Show the guess with its reason ("Weekday evening: showing Home") and one visible way to change it. Context sets the default, never the cage.

Paging through answers

One result per screen, with Next and Previous, forces memory comparisons.

Lay comparable answers side by side or on one shared axis, even if each gets less space.

Check yourself

Your invoicing app has a Reports page with a form: pick a date range, pick a client, press Run. Most users run "this month, all clients". What does Magic Ink suggest?

Environment (today's date) and history (last choice) answer the most common question with zero input. The control still exists but updates live. Adding report types (b) adds more interaction; a modal (c) hides the same form; a tutorial (d) documents the cost instead of removing it.

Data-ink, chartjunk and the case for some decoration

Taught by Edward Tufte, with Scott Bateman and Stephen Few

Every mark on a chart should earn its place by carrying information. Remove what doesn't, and the data gets louder. But "minimal" is a means, not the goal: the goal is understanding, and sometimes memory.

In The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983, second edition 2001), Edward Tufte defined the data-ink ratio: the share of a graphic's ink that presents data and could not be erased without losing information. Gridlines, frames, background fills, shadows, 3D sides, decorative icons and repeated labels are non-data ink. His instructions are almost mechanical: above all else show the data; maximise the data-ink ratio, within reason; erase non-data ink; erase redundant data ink; revise and edit.

Why does this work? Because of how vision works. Your eye picks out differences before you consciously look (pre-attentive processing; see The Human Mind). Every heavy gridline, frame and gradient is a difference too, and it competes with the bars for that first glance. Removing it does not just look tidier: it lowers the number of things the eye has to reject before it reaches the data. On a phone, where a chart may be 320 pixels wide, every pixel of frame is a pixel taken from the data.

Chartjunk

Tufte's name for the worst non-data ink is chartjunk. He described three families: vibrating patterns (cross-hatching and stripes that shimmer, a moiré effect that makes the eye work); the grid (heavy, dark gridlines that compete with the data); and the duck (a graphic whose whole structure is decoration, named after a duck-shaped building on Long Island). The software equivalents are easy to find: gradients on bars, drop shadows, glossy 3D, icons stacked to make bars, a chart inside a decorative card inside a card, and animated count-ups that make you wait for a number.

Within reason: the honest critique

Tufte's rule is a superb default and a poor religion. Researchers tested it. In 2010 Scott Bateman and colleagues at the University of Saskatchewan published "Useful Junk?" at the CHI conference. They compared plain charts with heavily illustrated versions in the style of the illustrator Nigel Holmes (think a monster whose teeth are the bars). Interpretation accuracy was about the same for both. But two to three weeks later, people remembered the embellished charts' topic and message significantly better. In 2013 Michelle Borkin and colleagues found that charts with recognisable pictures and distinctive visual features were more memorable than plain, common chart types.

So the right question is not "how minimal can I go?" but "what is this chart for?":

Also note where pure erasing fails. Tufte's own "range-frame" and minimal box plots strip so much that many readers no longer recognise the form. Faint gridlines, when people need to read values off the chart, are not junk: they are data-adjacent ink that carries information. Erase what is redundant, not what is quiet.

Myth

"Minimal charts are always better." Minimal charts are faster to read and harder to misread, which matters most for screens people use repeatedly. For a one-off story, a relevant illustration can make the message stick. The line you never cross is distortion: decoration must not change the apparent size of anything.

For the solo creator

Charting libraries ship with junk on by default: legends, gridlines, borders, tooltips with five fields, animation on load. Make a single wrapper (one function or component) that turns all of it off and sets your defaults: faint gridlines or none, no legend, direct labels, your type, no entry animation. Then every chart in your product starts clean, and you only add back what a specific chart needs. See Systems & Platforms for building that as part of your design system.

Do

  • Remove frames, backgrounds, shadows and 3D first; they never carry data.
  • Replace legends with direct labels where you can.
  • Keep a baseline and faint gridlines if people need to read values.
  • Use decoration only when the goal is memory, it relates to the data, and it doesn't distort scale.

Don't

  • Accept your chart library's defaults.
  • Use gradients, hatching or texture to fill bars.
  • Erase until the chart is a riddle (no axis, no labels, no title).
  • Animate numbers counting up on a screen people visit daily; it's a delay dressed as delight.

Common mistakes

Polishing the frame instead of the data

Card shadows, rounded containers and coloured headers are easy to tweak, so effort goes there.

Spend the polish on the data marks: sort order, labels, highlight, title. The container should disappear.

Erasing context people need

"Remove gridlines" taken literally leaves people unable to read a value off the chart.

Either label values directly or keep faint gridlines (about 10–20% contrast against the background). Never neither.

Decoration that changes size

Icon stacks and pictures scaled in two dimensions exaggerate differences (doubling height also doubles width, so area quadruples).

If you use pictograms, repeat identical icons (a unit chart) instead of scaling one icon.

Check yourself

You are designing a "Your 2026 in reading" summary that people will see once and probably share. Which choice best fits the evidence?

Bateman's study found embellishment helped long-term memory without hurting accuracy, which suits a one-off, shareable summary. But 3D (b) distorts, and removing numbers (d) loses the information. Pure minimalism (a) is fine but gives up memorability where memory is the goal.

Honest graphics: lie factor, baselines and frames

Taught by Edward Tufte, with Alberto Cairo and Dona Wong

A chart is honest when the size of an effect on screen matches the size of the effect in the data. Most dishonest charts aren't drawn by liars; they're drawn by defaults, cropped axes and convenient time windows.

Tufte gave this a number. The lie factor is the size of the effect shown in the graphic divided by the size of the effect in the data. A lie factor of 1 is honest. His famous example was a 1978 New York Times graphic of US fuel-economy standards, where a 53% increase in the data was drawn as a 783% increase in line length: a lie factor of about 14.8. Tufte suggested anything outside roughly 0.95 to 1.05 is a substantial distortion.

When the baseline must be zero

The rule depends on the encoding, and this is where most people go wrong in both directions.

  • Bars, columns and areas must start at zero. Their length or area is the value. A truncated bar is a false statement about size. Dona Wong's The Wall Street Journal Guide to Information Graphics (2010) is blunt about this: bar charts start at zero.
  • Lines and dots don't have to. Their position encodes the value, and what people read from a line is change and shape. Body temperature charted from 0 °C would flatten a fever into nothing; a chart of 36–41 °C is honest because that is the meaningful range. Stock prices, weight, response times: zoom the axis to the range where differences matter, and label it clearly.
  • But "not required" doesn't mean "anything goes". Zooming a line until noise looks like a trend is the same lie by another route. Ask: would a domain expert call this change big? If not, don't draw it big. Cairo calls this showing the right amount of uncertainty and variation, not just the right numbers.

Other ways graphics mislead

Truncated bars are the famous one. Alberto Cairo's How Charts Lie (2019) catalogues the subtler ones you are more likely to commit by accident:

Cherry-picked window
Starting a time series at a low point makes any growth look heroic. Show a window that includes a normal period, or let people choose.
Cumulative charts
A cumulative total only goes up, so it hides that monthly sign-ups are falling. Fine for "progress towards a goal"; misleading for "how are we doing".
Missing denominators
"Most crashes on Android" may only mean most users are on Android. Show rates (per 1,000 sessions), not counts, when comparing groups of different sizes.
Unadjusted money
Revenue over ten years without inflation, or prices across countries without currency or purchasing-power context.
Aspect-ratio drama
Squash a chart horizontally and every line becomes a cliff. Chapter 8 covers choosing an aspect ratio deliberately.
Hidden uncertainty
A single forecast line implies a precision you don't have. Show a range band, or say "about".
Watch out

You will be tempted most when the chart is about your own success: a launch post, an investor update, an app-store screenshot, a "you saved 3× more time" stat. Readers who notice a truncated bar stop trusting every other number you show. Honesty is cheap insurance for credibility.

For the solo creator

Hard-code the rule into your chart wrapper: if the chart type is bar or area, the axis minimum is 0 and cannot be overridden. For line charts, compute a padded range from the data but never let a tiny range (say, less than 2% of the mean) fill the full height; show it flat and say "steady". These two lines of code prevent most accidental lies.

Do

  • Start bars and areas at zero, always.
  • Zoom lines to a meaningful range and label the axis ends.
  • Show rates when comparing groups of different sizes.
  • State the size of the change in words in the title ("up 0.2 points").
  • Show uncertainty when the numbers are estimates.

Don't

  • Truncate a bar chart to make a change visible; use a dot plot or show the difference directly.
  • Crop time windows to start at a dip.
  • Use cumulative charts to report health.
  • Use a broken-axis zigzag as a licence to truncate bars.

Common mistakes

Auto-scaled bar charts

Many chart libraries auto-fit the y-axis to the data range, including for bars.

Set the bar axis minimum to zero explicitly in your wrapper. Test with values like 98 and 99.

Forcing zero on every line

People over-learn "always start at zero" and flatten meaningful changes in a line (weight, temperature, latency).

For lines, pick the range a knowledgeable person would care about, and label it.

Counts where rates belong

Raw counts are what the database gives you.

Divide by the right denominator (users, sessions, days) whenever groups or periods differ in size.

Check yourself

A sleep app plots nightly resting heart rate (normal range 50–70 bpm) as a line chart with the axis from 0 to 100. Every night looks identical. What should you change?

Lines don't require a zero baseline; a meaningful range reveals real variation. Bars starting at 45 (c) would be a truncated bar and therefore dishonest. A pie (d) is for parts of a whole.
Check yourself

Your changelog says "Crash reports dropped from 120 to 60 this month". Active users also fell from 12,000 to 5,000. What is the honest headline?

The denominator changed. 120/12,000 is 10 per 1,000; 60/5,000 is 12 per 1,000. The rate went up. Counts without denominators are one of the commonest accidental lies.

Small multiples, sparklines and layers

Taught by Edward Tufte, with Josef Albers

Comparison is the heart of understanding numbers. Small multiples repeat one simple chart so the eye compares many; sparklines shrink a chart to the size of a word so data sits inside sentences and rows; layering makes context quiet so data can be loud.

Tufte's question for any graphic is "compared to what?". A single number or a single line rarely answers it. Two of his most practical answers are designs that put many comparisons in a small space.

Small multiples

A small multiple is a grid of the same little chart, one per category, with the same scales and the same design in every panel. Once you've understood one panel you've understood them all, so the eye spends all its effort on the differences. Tufte called them the best design solution for a wide range of problems in data presentation. They beat the alternative (one chart with eight overlapping coloured lines and a legend) because each series gets its own clean space and no colour lookup is needed.

Sparklines

In Beautiful Evidence (2006) Tufte introduced sparklines: small, intense, word-sized graphics with no axes, placed where the words and numbers are, in a sentence, a table cell or a list row. A sparkline answers "compared to what?" for a single number: "Revenue ₹4.2 lakh" says nothing; the same number with a tiny line of the last twelve months says whether that's a good month. Microsoft added sparklines to Excel in 2010, and they are now common in finance apps and dashboards.

Rules that keep them honest: show the same time window in every sparkline you place side by side; decide deliberately whether they share a vertical scale (shared to compare sizes, independent to compare shapes) and don't mix the two in one list; mark the last point (a small dot) and, if useful, the min and max; keep them about the height of a capital letter so line spacing isn't disturbed.

Layering and separation

In Envisioning Information (1990) Tufte describes layering and separation: put information on visually distinct layers using differences in value (lightness), weight and colour, so the reader can focus on one layer while the others stay quietly available. Gridlines, axes, context data and previous periods go on a pale back layer; the data you want read goes on a dark or saturated front layer. He borrows Josef Albers's observation that "1 + 1 = 3 or more": two strong marks create a third, unintended shape (the vibrating gap between them), so heavy context marks don't just add noise, they multiply it.

Micro and macro readings

Tufte's other idea from the same book: the best dense displays work at two distances. From afar (macro) you see the overall pattern; up close (micro) every detail is real data. The Vietnam Veterans Memorial is his example: from a distance, a wall of names; up close, one name. In software, a year calendar heatmap is the everyday case: the macro reading shows seasons and streaks, the micro reading shows a single day. Dense is fine when it is organised; what fails is clutter, which is density without structure.

Key idea

Clutter is not too much information; it's too little organisation. Repeat a simple form (small multiples), shrink it to word size (sparklines), and push context back a layer.

For the solo creator

A sparkline component is about 20 lines of SVG code: map values to points, draw a polyline, add a dot. Build it once and you can put context next to every number in your product: list rows, settings pages, email digests. Small multiples are a CSS grid of that same component with a shared scale. Both are cheaper to build than a full charting library and usually more useful.

Do

  • Use small multiples instead of more than three or four overlapping series.
  • Keep scales identical across panels unless you say otherwise.
  • Put sparklines next to the numbers they explain, with a dot on the latest value.
  • Push gridlines, comparison periods and annotations back a layer with lighter value.

Don't

  • Give each small panel its own auto-scaled axis without warning; it makes every series look equally dramatic.
  • Make sparklines taller than a line of text.
  • Use equal weight and colour for grid, context and data.
  • Mistake emptiness for clarity; a sparse screen with no context is not calm, it is uninformative.

Common mistakes

Independent scales by accident

Part two

How the eye reads quantity

Charts work because the visual system turns marks into estimates of size without conscious effort. But it doesn't do this equally well for every kind of mark. This part starts with the experiments that measured which encodings people read accurately, uses them to settle the old arguments (pies, 3D, dual axes), and then turns that into two practical skills: choosing the chart for a question, and crafting it so the answer is the first thing seen.

5

The perceptual ranking: position beats colour

Taught by William Cleveland & Robert McGill, with Jacques Bertin and Jeffrey Heer

People judge quantities most accurately from position on a common scale, then length, then angle, then area, then colour shade. Put your most important comparison in the most accurate channel.

In 1984 William Cleveland and Robert McGill, statisticians at Bell Labs, published "Graphical Perception" in the Journal of the American Statistical Association. They showed people pairs of values encoded in different ways and asked them to judge what percentage the smaller was of the larger. The errors differed a lot by encoding. In 2010 Jeffrey Heer and Michael Bostock re-ran similar experiments with crowdsourced participants on Mechanical Turk and found the same general order. That makes it one of the most reliable findings in visualisation.

Why the order exists

Comparing two dots on the same axis is a straight distance judgement against a shared reference. Lengths are almost as good, but without a shared baseline you compare them by eye, which adds error. Angles are harder because our sensitivity varies with orientation (we're good near 0°, 90° and 180°, worse in between). Area is worse still: research on perceived magnitude (Stevens's power law) found people tend to underestimate differences in area, so a circle with twice the area looks less than twice as big. Colour shade is the weakest: lightness perception depends on what surrounds it, so the same grey looks different on different backgrounds.

The practical rule: put the comparison that matters most into position. Use length next. Use area and shade only for rough impressions (big vs small, hot vs cold), or when position is already taken (a map uses position for geography, so the data must go into shade or size).

Do five guesses per encoding. Most people's average error is smallest for position and length, and two or three times larger for area and shade. That gap is the whole argument for bar and dot charts.

Bertin's visual variables

Long before the experiments, the French cartographer Jacques Bertin set out the vocabulary in Sémiologie graphique (1967; English translation Semiology of Graphics, 1983). A mark on a page can vary in position (two dimensions), size, value (lightness), texture, colour (hue), orientation and shape. His key contribution was to ask which variables can express which kinds of data. Later, Jock Mackinlay (1986) ranked the channels separately for quantitative, ordered and categorical data.

For the solo creator

When you sketch a chart, write next to it: "the comparison that matters is ___, and it's encoded as ___". If the answer is area, angle or colour for something people must compare precisely, change the chart. This takes ten seconds and catches most bad chart choices before you write code.

Do

  • Encode the main comparison as position or length on a shared baseline.
  • Use hue for categories and lightness for order.
  • Label values directly when precision matters, whatever the encoding.
  • Use area and shade for rough "big vs small" impressions, or when position is already used (maps).

Don't

  • Use rainbow hue scales for quantities; hue has no natural order.
  • Use bubble size for close comparisons.
  • Rely on colour alone to carry meaning (see Accessible & Inclusive).
  • Scale icons in two dimensions to show one-dimensional data.

Choosing the chart for looks

Bubbles, radial bars and donuts look more designed than bars.

Ask what comparison matters. If it needs precision, use position or length, and make the bars beautiful with type, spacing and colour instead.

Heatmaps default to rainbow palettes, which look vivid and imply order that doesn't exist (is green more than yellow?).

Use a single-hue light-to-dark scale for quantity, and a diverging scale (two hues meeting at a neutral midpoint) only when there's a meaningful middle.

Pies, 3D and dual axes: the usual suspects

Taught by Stephen Few, with Dona Wong and Alberto Cairo

Pie charts are fine for one simple message about parts of a whole and poor for comparison. 3D adds distortion and nothing else. Dual axes let the designer invent any relationship they like.

Pie charts, fairly judged

Pies rank low on the ladder (angle and area), and Stephen Few's essay "Save the Pies for Dessert" (2007) laid out the case against them. Yet pies survive because they do one thing well: they say "these parts make a whole" instantly. That message is the best case for a pie. When the question is "roughly what share is this?", and there are two or three parts, a pie (or a single filled ring) is quick and familiar. When the question is "which is bigger?" or "how did shares change?", it fails.

Donut charts are a pie with the centre removed; readers then judge arc length rather than angle, which research by Robert Kosara and Drew Skau (2016) suggests is read about as accurately as a pie, not better. The centre hole is useful for a single label. A waffle chart (a 10×10 grid of squares) is often a better part-to-whole form for one share: "23 of 100 squares" can be counted.

3D charts

There is no data reason for 3D in a chart of two-dimensional data. Perspective makes near slices and bars larger than far ones, tilting a pie turns angles into ellipses that no one can judge, and 3D bars sit their visual top above or below the true value depending on the viewing angle. Occlusion hides some bars behind others. Dona Wong's advice is simply not to use it. The only reasonable 3D in data graphics is for genuinely three-dimensional data (terrain, a molecule, a building), usually with interaction to rotate it.

Dual axes

A dual-axis chart plots two series with different units on one chart, one scale on the left and one on the right. The problem: both scales are arbitrary choices, so the designer can make the lines cross, run parallel or diverge simply by adjusting either axis. Readers then see a relationship (correlation, crossing points, "revenue overtook costs") that the data doesn't contain. Few, Cairo and the Datawrapper team all advise against them in most cases.

For the solo creator

Storage meters, quota rings and "profile 60% complete" bars are the legitimate pies of product UI: one share of one whole. Keep them. For anything with more than three parts, ship sorted bars; they're also easier to build responsively, since bars reflow into rows on a phone while pie labels collide.

Do

  • Use a pie, ring or waffle for one share or two or three very different parts.
  • Start slices at 12 o'clock, largest first, labels on the slices.
  • Use stacked small charts or indexed lines instead of dual axes.
  • Keep 2D data in 2D.

Don't

  • Compare pies side by side to show change in shares.
  • Use exploded, tilted or 3D pies.
  • Put two unrelated units on one chart and let people infer a link.
  • Use donut charts with seven slices and a legend.

In the wild

  • iPhone and Mac storage barsApple
    Storage use is shown as one horizontal bar split into a few categories, with the largest named first and the free space shown. A segmented bar is a pie unrolled: easier to read and to fit on a narrow screen.
  • Apple Activity ringsApple Watch, 2015
    Three rings, each one share of a personal goal (Move, Exercise, Stand), and rings can go past 100%. Each ring is "one share of one whole", the case where circular forms work; nobody compares the rings to each other.
  • Google Sheets and Excelchart menus
    Both still offer 3D pie and 3D column options. Being available in a menu is not an endorsement.

Common mistakes

Pie for change over time

Shares are part-to-whole, so a pie per month feels natural.

Use 100% stacked bars (one per period) with the key segment at the baseline, or a line per share.

Dual axis "to save space"

Two series feel related and space is tight on mobile.

Stack two short charts sharing one x-axis; it takes barely more height and makes no false claims.

Percentages that don't sum to 100

Rounding each slice independently gives 99% or 101%.

Use a largest-remainder rounding method, or show one decimal, or note "may not sum due to rounding".

Check yourself

A finance app shows "Revenue" (₹, left axis) and "Users" (count, right axis) on one chart; the lines cross in March and the caption says "users overtook revenue". What's wrong?

With dual axes the crossing depends entirely on where each axis is set. Rupees and users aren't comparable quantities, so "overtook" is meaningless. Index both to 100 at the start or show two stacked charts.

Choosing the chart from the question

Taught by Stephen Few, with Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic, Francis Anscombe and Alberto Cairo

Don't start from chart types; start from the question the reader is asking. Each question (compare, change, part-to-whole, distribution, relationship) has one or two forms that answer it well, and a few that look plausible and fail.

Most bad charts come from choosing a type first ("let's do a donut here") and pouring data into it. Reverse the order. Write the reader's question as a sentence. Then identify its shape. Nearly every question in product UI is one of a handful:

  • Comparison: which is bigger, by how much? ("Which project took the most hours?")
  • Ranking: what's the order? ("My top five apps.")
  • Change over time: going up or down, how fast, any pattern? ("Am I reading more than last year?")
  • Before and after: how did each item change between two moments?
  • Part-to-whole: what share is this? ("How much of my storage is photos?")
  • Distribution: what's typical, how spread out, any outliers? ("How long are my sessions usually?")
  • Relationship: does one thing move with another? ("Do I sleep worse when I work late?")
  • Pattern across two categories: when does it happen? ("Which hours of which days am I most productive?")

Always plot the distribution once

Averages hide shape. In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe published four small data sets, now called Anscombe's quartet, with nearly identical means, variances, correlations and regression lines. Plotted, they're completely different: a noisy line, a curve, a perfect line with one outlier, and a vertical cluster with one far point. In 2017 Justin Matejka and George Fitzmaurice extended the idea in "Same Stats, Different Graphs" with the "Datasaurus Dozen", a set that includes a dinosaur drawing (based on Alberto Cairo's original "Datasaurus") with the same summary statistics as the others.

Myth

"A table is a failure to visualise." Tables are the right form when people need to look up exact values or compare several attributes of one item. Charts are for shapes, patterns and comparisons. For the solo creator

Do

  • Write the reader's question before choosing a form.
  • Prefer bars, lines and dots; they're on the top rungs of the ladder.
  • Plot raw distributions before you summarise with an average.
  • Use horizontal bars when category names are long; they stay readable at 400px.

Don't

  • Pick a chart for novelty (radar, radial, sankey) when a bar answers the question.
  • Stack more than two or three segments if people must compare the middle ones.
  • Report "average" for skewed data without the median or a distribution.
  • Use a line for unordered categories; a line implies continuity.

Chart craft: titles, labels, colour and shape

Taught by Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic, with Dona Wong, William Cleveland and Edward Tufte

A good chart is designed like a good sentence: it has a point, it says it first, and everything else supports it. Title with the finding, label directly, use colour for one thing, sort with purpose, and choose the shape of the frame deliberately.

Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic's Storytelling with Data (2015) distils a practice that came from years of teaching analysts at Google: understand the context, choose an appropriate visual, eliminate clutter, focus attention where you want it, think like a designer, tell a story. The crafting steps below are where most of the improvement in a chart actually happens. Choosing the right type gets you to "acceptable"; craft gets you to "obvious".

1. Titles that state the finding

"Sign-ups by month" is a label. "Sign-ups doubled after the free tier launched" is a finding. An insight title tells the reader what to look for, so the chart confirms it in a glance instead of being decoded from scratch. Put the descriptive label (metric, units, period) in a smaller subtitle. In product UI, where the data changes, generate the title from the data: "You read 3 more books than last year" or "Spending is 12% below your monthly average". When there's no clear finding, say so honestly ("About the same as last month") rather than inventing drama.

2. Direct labels instead of legends

A legend asks the reader to hold a colour-to-name mapping in working memory and look back and forth. Put the name at the end of the line or on the bar. Datawrapper, Our World in Data and most newspaper graphics do this by default. A legend is acceptable only when lines are so tangled that labels would collide, and that's often a sign you need small multiples instead.

3. Colour with purpose

Start everything in grey. Then add one colour to the thing the title talks about. That single move does most of the work in Knaflic's "focus attention" step: pre-attentive processing finds the one coloured thing instantly. Use more colours only when categories genuinely need distinguishing, keep categorical palettes small (about six hues at most, distinguishable in colour-blind simulation), use a single-hue light-to-dark ramp for quantities, and a diverging ramp (two hues through a neutral middle) when there's a meaningful midpoint like zero or a target. Keep a colour's meaning consistent across every chart in your product. More on colour and accessibility in Visual Design and Accessible & Inclusive.

Lab: grey the rest, highlight one
Compare the two styles. In the rainbow version, finding "Journal" means a trip to the legend. In the highlight version the eye lands on it immediately, the title tells you what it did, and the other five lines become context instead of competition.

4. Sorting

Unless categories have a natural order (months, age bands, steps in a funnel, rating scales), sort bars by value. Alphabetical order is a database habit; it makes the reader do the ranking. For rating scales, keep the natural order and use a diverging layout. For small multiples, sort panels by the thing you want compared (largest change first), not by name.

5. Aspect ratio and banking

The shape of a line chart's frame changes how steep slopes look, and therefore how dramatic change feels. William Cleveland proposed banking to 45°: choose the aspect ratio so that the average absolute slope of the line segments is about 45°, where people judge slope differences best. You don't need to compute it. The practical version: if a line looks like a cliff, the chart is too narrow or too tall; if it looks like a flat wire, too wide or too short. Pick the frame where the meaningful changes are visible and no steeper. On phones this matters because charts get squeezed into tall narrow boxes, which turns every wobble into a spike.

6. Gridlines, ticks and axis numbers

Gridlines should be just visible: a light rule colour, thin, and only on the value axis. Three to five ticks is plenty for most product charts. Format tick labels for humans: "20k" not "20000.00", "Mar" not "2026-03-01", "₹2L" or "₹2 lakh" for Indian audiences. Drop the axis line on the value axis if gridlines exist; keep a darker baseline at zero for bars. Put units in the subtitle or on the top tick ("60 min") instead of an axis title rotated 90°, which nobody reads on a phone.

7. Annotation

An annotation is a sentence placed on the chart where the thing happened: "Free tier launched", "Server outage", "Started new job". It turns a shape into an explanation. In personal data products, letting users annotate their own charts ("moved city", "injury") is powerful: their context explains their data better than any algorithm can. Keep annotations in the muted colour, attached to the data with a thin line, and limit them to the two or three that explain the shape.

For the solo creator

Generate titles from data with a few rules: compare this period to the previous one; if the change is under a threshold (say 5%), write "About the same as last week"; otherwise "Up 18% on last week" or "Your best week since March". This is a handful of lines of code, and it's the single highest-value improvement you can make to a stats screen.

Do

  • Write the title as the finding; put units and period in the subtitle.
  • Label lines and bars directly.
  • Start grey; add one accent for the point.
  • Sort by value unless there's a natural order.
  • Format ticks for humans and keep them few.
  • Annotate the events that explain the shape.

Don't

  • Use a legend when labels fit.
  • Give every series its own bright colour by default.
  • Rotate axis labels or category names; switch to horizontal bars.
  • Squeeze line charts into tall narrow boxes.
  • Write titles that oversell a flat result.

  • Our World in Dataline charts

Numbers, tables and dashboards

Most of the data in your product isn't in charts. It's a number in a sentence, a balance on a card, a column in a table, a tile on a stats screen. 9

Formatting numbers for humans

Taught by Dona Wong, with Edward Tufte and the Unicode CLDR locale data

A number should be shown with exactly the precision the reader's decision needs, in the grouping and units they read fluently, and with the comparison that tells them whether it's good.

Formatting looks like a detail. It isn't: a misplaced separator turns ₹12,34,567 into nonsense for an American reader and 1,234,567 into a puzzle for many Indian readers who count in lakhs. Too many decimals make numbers slow to read and imply false precision. Too few hide real differences. Every number in your product goes through this decision, so it's worth getting the rules into one formatting function.

Precision: significant figures, not decimal places

For overview screens, two or three significant figures are almost always enough. "4.2 lakh" or "420k" is read instantly; "4,23,817.46" has to be parsed digit by digit, and the extra digits rarely change a decision. Detail views, invoices, receipts and exports need exact values. A good rule: summary = rounded, detail = exact, money owed = exact to the paisa or cent. And keep decimals consistent within a column or a comparison: 4.0 next to 3.75 next to 12 is harder to compare than 4.00, 3.75, 12.00.

Beware false precision: "Your reading speed is 247.36 words per minute" claims an accuracy the measurement doesn't have. "About 250 wpm" is more honest. The precision you show is a claim about how much you know.

Units, signs and symbols

  • Always show the unit, close to the number: "42 min", "3.2 km", "₹1,200". A bare number is a riddle. In tables, put the unit in the column header once.
  • Use a real minus sign (−, U+2212) for negative values in display type; the hyphen is shorter and misaligns. Accounting contexts may use parentheses: (1,200).
  • Keep the number and its unit together with a no-break space so "42" doesn't end one line and "min" start the next.
  • Durations: "1 h 32 min" reads better than "1.53 hours" for people; "92 min" is good for comparison in a column.
  • Times and dates: relative for the recent past ("4 min ago"), absolute for anything older than about a day ("12 Mar"), and always absolute on hover or in detail. Follow the user's 12/24-hour setting.
  • No data is not zero. Show "No data" or a muted dash for missing values, so a gap isn't read as nothing happening.

Relative and absolute, percent and points

Two common traps. First, a relative change without the absolute base is easily misread: "risk doubled" might mean 1 in 10,000 became 2 in 10,000. "+200% sales" might mean 1 sale became 3. Show both: "3 sales (up from 1)". Second, percentages of percentages: if conversion goes from 10% to 12%, that's +2 percentage points or +20%. Both are true; "+2%" is wrong and common. Say "points" for differences between percentages.

For the solo creator

Write one formatNumber(value, kind) function, where kind is money, count, percent, duration or delta, and route every number in your product through it. Use the platform formatter (Intl.NumberFormat, NumberFormatter on Apple platforms) with the user's locale. You'll fix grouping, decimals, units and missing values in one place, forever.

Do

  • Round summaries to 2–3 significant figures; keep detail exact.
  • Use the reader's locale for grouping and decimals.
  • Use lakh and crore for Indian audiences when amounts are large.
  • Say "points" for differences between percentages.
  • Show absolute numbers next to relative changes.

Don't

  • Mix decimal places within a column.
  • Show 0 when you mean "no data".
  • Hard-code commas and dots in strings.
  • Use a hyphen as a minus sign in large display numbers.

Big numbers, deltas and trend arrows

Taught by Stephen Few, with Edward Tufte and Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic

A big number on its own is a statement without a meaning. Give every headline number a period, a comparison and a judgement of good or bad, and let the typography keep it steady.

The "big number tile" (a large figure with a small label, often in a grid of four to eight) is the most common data element in software. It is also the most commonly misused. The failure is always the same: Tufte's "compared to what?". "1,284 users" doesn't tell you if that's good. "1,284 users this week, up 9% on last week, highest since March" does.

Deltas and trend indicators

Arrows and percentages are where most tiles go wrong:

  1. Say the comparison. "+12%" against what? Previous day, same day last week, last month, last year? Weekly cycles are strong in almost any human activity, so "vs same day last week" is usually fairer than "vs yesterday" (Monday vs Sunday is not news).
  2. Encode good and bad, not up and down. Costs going up is bad; error rate going down is good; screen time going down might be good. Green for up everywhere is a bug. And never rely on colour alone: the arrow and the word carry it too.
  3. Mind the base. Small numbers swing wildly. +200% on a base of 2 is noise. Show the absolute change, and suppress percentages under a minimum base.
  4. Show "about the same". Changes within the normal noise of the metric (say ±3%) should read as neutral, in grey, without an arrow. Otherwise users react to random wobble.
  5. Prefer a trend over a single delta. One comparison can mislead; a sparkline of 8 to 12 periods shows whether this week is a blip or a direction.

Vanity and the cumulative trap

A vanity metric looks good and informs no decision. "Total users ever" only goes up; it can't tell you that the product is shrinking. Typography for numbers

Numbers that change, align or sit in columns need tabular figures: digits of equal width, set with font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums (if the font supports it). Without them, a timer or live counter shimmies left and right as digits change ("1" is narrower than "8" in proportional figures), and columns of numbers don't line up. In running prose, proportional figures look better. Use lining figures (all digits the height of capitals) in UI and tables; oldstyle figures (with ascenders and descenders) suit long-form reading. See Typography for the full treatment.

Watch out

Count-up animations on big numbers (0 → 48,912 over a second) are popular and costly. They delay the answer on every visit, they make the number unreadable while moving, and screen readers may announce intermediate values. If you animate, keep it under about 300 ms, only on first view, and respect reduced-motion settings (see Motion & Feel).

For the solo creator

Don't

  • Show cumulative totals as health metrics.
  • Show percentages on tiny bases.
  • Use more than four to six tiles in a row; nobody reads the seventh.
  • Animate numbers counting up on every visit.

Tables people can scan

Taught by Stephen Few, with Edward Tufte and Dona Wong

A table is for looking up and comparing exact values. Its design job is to make columns easy to scan down and rows easy to follow across, with the least ink that does it: aligned numbers, quiet separators, and emphasis on the column that matters.

Stephen Few's Show Me the Numbers (2004, second edition 2012) treats tables as seriously as charts, and argues that for lookup they're often better. Tables are linear: people scan a column to find a row, then read across. Every design decision should help those two motions.

Alignment is the whole game

  • Numbers right-aligned, so units line up under units and tens under tens; magnitude is then visible from the length of the number alone. Use the same number of decimals in a column, and tabular figures.
  • Text left-aligned, because we read from the left edge. Centred text gives every row a different starting point.
  • Headers aligned like their column: a numeric column's header goes right too, so it sits above the numbers.
  • Dates in a consistent, fixed-width format (12 Mar 2026, or 2026-03-12 for technical audiences), left-aligned or right-aligned consistently.
  • Units in the header ("Amount (₹)", "Duration (min)"), not repeated in every cell.

Rows: separators and density

Readers need help following a row across, especially in wide tables. Three options, from lightest to heaviest: whitespace alone (works for short, narrow tables), thin horizontal rules (the best general default), and zebra striping (alternating subtle background tints; helpful for very wide tables with many columns, distracting for short ones). Avoid vertical rules: alignment already defines columns, and vertical lines create a grid of boxes that slows scanning. Few recommends using the lightest separation that works.

Density is a trade-off between scanning and seeing more at once. A comfortable row (about 44–48 px tall on desktop) is easy to read and click; a compact row (about 32 px) lets experts see more. Products for professionals (Linear, spreadsheets, finance tools) often offer a density setting. On touch screens, keep tap targets at least 44 pt even if the visual row is dense.

Do

  • Use fixed decimals and tabular figures in numeric columns.
  • Use the lightest row separation that works; thin rules by default.
  • Put units in headers and totals under a heavier rule.
  • Mark exceptions in the cell, with more than colour.

Don't

  • Centre numbers.
  • Draw vertical gridlines or boxes around every cell.
  • Colour entire rows to flag one value.
  • Use ALL CAPS headers in heavy weight that outshout the data.

Sorting, filtering and tables on small screens

Taught by Stephen Few, with Luke Wroblewski and Bret Victor

In software, a table is a live object: it sorts, filters, truncates and has to survive a 400px screen. Make every one of those states visible, reversible and honest about what's hidden.

Sorting

  • Choose a meaningful default sort (most recent, most urgent, largest), not database order. This is Magic Ink again: the default should answer the common question.
  • Show the active sort with an arrow on the header, and make the whole header clickable. Clicking again reverses; consider a third click to return to default.
  • Sort numbers as numbers, dates as dates (10 after 9, not before 2) and names with natural ordering (file2 before file10) and locale-aware collation.
  • Sort sensibly by default direction: numbers usually descending first (largest is most interesting), text ascending.
  • Keep sorts stable so equal items don't jump around, and remember the user's choice.

Filtering and search

Filtering hides data, which makes it dangerous: people forget a filter is on and conclude something is missing. Show active filters as removable chips above the table, show the count ("Showing 12 of 340"), and offer a single "Clear filters". Filters should apply live as they're changed, without an Apply button, when the data set is small enough (Victor's point about immediate feedback). For text search, highlight matched text in the results so people see why each row matched. An empty filtered result should say what filter caused it and offer to remove it, which is different from a truly empty table (see Structure & Interaction on empty states).

Truncation

When text doesn't fit, truncate with an ellipsis and give access to the full value (a tooltip on desktop, the detail view on mobile). Truncate in the right place: titles at the end, file names and email addresses in the middle (so "Invoice-2026-…-final.pdf" keeps the distinguishing ending), and never truncate numbers: a number cut short is a different number. Give the most important text column the flexible width and let other columns stay fixed.

Tables on a phone

At 400px a five-column table doesn't fit. Luke Wroblewski's "mobile first" argument (his 2011 book Mobile First) applies directly: the constraint forces you to decide what matters. There are five honest strategies; pick by what people do with the table.

Dashboards that answer "so what?"

Taught by Stephen Few, with Ben Shneiderman and Bret Victor

A dashboard is a single screen that lets someone check, at a glance, whether things are as they should be. Decide whether it's for monitoring or analysis, put the summary first, keep normal quiet, and make every element lead to a decision.

Stephen Few's Information Dashboard Design (2006) defined a dashboard, roughly, as a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives, fitted on a single screen so it can be monitored at a glance. Each part of that definition is a design constraint: most important (so choose), objectives (so tie each element to a goal), single screen (so no scrolling to find problems), at a glance (so pre-attentive encoding of what's wrong).

Normal is quiet, exceptions are loud

A dashboard where everything is coloured has nothing highlighted. Encode status by exception: normal values in neutral ink, attention values with a warm colour plus a symbol, critical with a strong colour plus a symbol and text. Never rely on red and green alone (about 1 in 12 men has some form of colour-vision deficiency). Few criticised the traffic-light habit, where every metric gets a red, amber or green dot: when twenty dots are green the one amber is lost, and the dot says nothing about how far off target a value is.

Few also designed the bullet graph as a compact replacement for dashboard gauges and meters. A gauge spends a large circle of space to show one value on an angle (a low rung). A bullet graph is a single bar (length, a high rung) over a few shaded bands for qualitative ranges (poor, OK, good), with a short line for the target. It fits in a table row.

Density done right

Calm is not the same as sparse. A good dashboard can be dense, because it's organised: aligned grid, consistent chart forms, shared scales, quiet chrome, and a clear reading order. Few lists common dashboard mistakes, including exceeding a single screen, giving inadequate context for the numbers, excessive detail or precision, choosing poor measures, meaningless variety of chart types, cluttering with decoration, and misusing colour. Notice that "too much data" isn't the problem; "too little thought about which data" is.

The "so what?" test: for each element, finish the sentence "If this changes, I will…". If there's no action, it belongs in an analysis view or nowhere.

Personal stats, progress and the ethics of the mirror

Taught by Stephen Few, with the designers of Strava, Apple Health and Screen Time, and Amber Case

When the data is about the person looking at it, every chart is also a judgement of them. Design personal stats to inform and encourage, show what's normal for them, treat gaps kindly, and never use the mirror to manipulate.

Small apps often have one data screen: the stats page. A writing app shows words per day; a reading app shows books per year; a habit app shows streaks; a fitness app shows weekly distance. These are dashboards with a twist: the subject is the user, and they have feelings about it. The same bar chart can motivate or shame depending on framing.

Compare to themselves, not to strangers

The most useful comparison for personal data is the person's own baseline: "more than your usual Tuesday", "your longest run this year", "within your normal range". Comparison with others (leaderboards, percentiles) motivates a competitive few and discourages most. Strava handles this by making social comparison opt-in by context (segment leaderboards, clubs) while personal progress (weekly totals, personal records, a year-in-sport summary) is the default view.

Streaks and their dark side

A streak counts consecutive days. It's simple and motivating, and it has a cliff: one missed day resets it to zero, often after weeks of effort, and the user's feeling of the product turns from pride to loss. Products have softened this with streak freezes and repairs (Duolingo is the best-known example). A calmer alternative is a rolling consistency measure ("5 of the last 7 days", "18 of 30") or a calendar of done days, which records effort without a cliff.

Scores, rings and composite numbers

Composite scores (a "readiness" of 74, a "health score" of B+) are attractive because they're one number. They're also opaque: the user can't tell what moved it or what to do. If you use one, show its contributors and how each changed, use plain labels for ranges, and never let the score be the only view of the data. Apple's Activity rings work well because each ring is a single, understandable quantity against a goal the user set, and the goal can be changed.

A lighter week: 3 writing days, 2,140 words

Your usual is 4–5 days. You've written on 19 of the last 30 days, and your monthly total is on track with last month.

Days without the app count as "no data", not zero.

Without: red headline, loss framing, a percentage drop with no context, and a guilt-driven button. It might drive a session today and a churn next month.
With: the same facts, framed against the person's own normal and a longer window. It informs, and lets them decide.

Quantified-self ethics

  • Missing data is not a bad day. A watch left on the charger, a phone left at home, a day the app wasn't opened: show gaps as gaps.
  • Rest is part of progress. Fitness apps increasingly show rest and recovery as positive. Habit apps can allow planned days off.
  • Let people choose what they see. Weight, calories and some health metrics can be harmful for some users. Offer to hide them, and don't push them in notifications.
  • Notifications about data are interruptions: send summaries at a predictable time, and let users turn them off. See Honest & Calm.
  • The data is theirs. Personal data deserves export, deletion, and care about what you collect at all. Amber Case's Calm Technology (2015) argues that technology should require the smallest possible amount of attention; for a stats screen, that means informing when asked and staying quiet otherwise.
For the solo creator

Your stats screen is often the most emotional screen in your product, and the cheapest to make kind. Write the copy for the bad week first. If it's honest, specific and free of guilt, the good-week version will be easy. Then add the personal baseline: "your usual" is just a rolling median of the last 8 weeks.

Do

  • Compare to the person's own usual range.
  • Use rolling consistency alongside or instead of streaks.
  • Show what composite scores are made of.
  • Treat missing data as missing, and rest as normal.
  • Let people hide sensitive metrics and silence summaries.

Don't

  • Use loss framing and red headlines for normal variation.
  • Rank users against strangers by default.
  • Use a single opaque score as the main view.
  • Send guilt notifications based on data.

Common mistakes

Zero-filling missing days

The query returns no row for a day, and the chart draws it as zero.

Engagement-optimised stats

Stats that create anxiety bring people back, and retention charts reward it.

Part four

Beyond charts

Some of the best information design in software isn't a chart at all. A transit map, a calendar, a flight timeline, an explanation you can play with. This last part looks at space and time as information, and at interactivity: when letting people manipulate data teaches them something a static picture can't, and when it just adds work.

15

Maps, timelines and calendars

Taught by Harry Beck, Charles Joseph Minard and Massimo Vignelli, with Edward Tufte

Maps, timelines and calendars are charts whose axes are place and time. Use geography only when location is the question, keep time proportional when duration matters, and always show "you are here" and "now".

Maps: is location the question?

A map is irresistible and often wrong. If the question is "which states have the most users?", a sorted bar chart answers it better: maps put the comparison into colour (low on the ladder) and give visual weight to land area, not to people. A large, empty region dominates a choropleth (a map with regions shaded by value) while a small, dense city disappears. Map when the question is genuinely spatial: where is it, what's near me, how does this spread, what's the route. And when you shade regions, use rates (per 1,000 people), not counts, or every map becomes a population map.

Topology over geography

In 1933 London Underground began issuing Harry Beck's diagram, which threw away accurate geography for a cleaner truth: what connects to what, and in what order. Lines run horizontal, vertical or at 45°; stations are evenly spaced; the crowded centre is enlarged. Riders don't need distances underground; they need sequence and interchanges. Massimo Vignelli's 1972 New York subway diagram took the same approach and was replaced in 1979, partly because New Yorkers, who walk between stations above ground, found its geographic distortions confusing. The lesson for software: choose the abstraction that matches the user's task, and know what you're sacrificing.

Minard and the multivariate map

Tufte's most famous example is Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 graphic of Napoleon's 1812 Russian campaign. A band whose width is the size of the army flows from the Polish border to Moscow and back, shrinking from about 422,000 men to 10,000, with a temperature chart along the bottom tied to the retreat. Tufte counts six variables in one picture: army size, two dimensions of location, direction, temperature and dates. Tufte wrote that it may well be the best statistical graphic ever drawn. Its lesson isn't "draw more variables"; it's that when the story is a relationship between variables, show them together on one shared frame so the causes are visible.

Timelines: time proportional, now visible

A list of times ("14:05, 14:40, 16:10") makes people compute gaps. A timeline makes gaps visible. When duration and gaps matter (travel, schedules, deliveries, build pipelines), draw time to scale, mark "now" with a line, and style the past differently from the future (muted vs full). Flight trackers and food-delivery apps use exactly this: a progress line along a sequence of stages, with the current stage emphasised and an estimated time to the next.

Calendars are information design

A calendar's day and week views are charts with time on one axis and days on the other. Good ones draw events with heights proportional to duration, so free time is visible as empty space, and show a "now" line so you know where you are. The month view is a different tool: it's for spotting busy and free days, so it summarises (dots, counts or shading) rather than listing everything. A month grid of shaded days is also the best form for habit and activity data, which is why the GitHub contribution graph and many habit apps use it.

For the solo creator

You rarely need a map library. Most "where" questions in small products are answered by a sorted list with distances ("2.1 km away") or a static thumbnail. Timelines and month grids, on the other hand, are cheap to build with CSS grid and pay off in any product with dates: habits, journals, invoices, reading logs.

Do

  • Map only when location is the question; otherwise rank with bars.
  • Shade regions by rates, with a single-hue sequential scale.
  • Draw time proportionally when gaps and durations matter.
  • Show "now" and "you are here".
  • Use month grids for daily activity data.

Don't

  • Shade regions by raw counts.
  • Use rainbow scales on maps.
  • List times when the question is about free time or gaps.
  • Style past and future identically on a timeline.

In the wild

  • London Underground diagramHarry Beck, 1933
    Topology over geography, still the model for transit maps worldwide. Abstraction is a design decision fitted to the task.
  • Google Calendar and Fantasticalday and week views
    Events drawn with height proportional to duration and a horizontal line for the current time. Free time becomes visible empty space.
  • Food delivery and ride appsorder tracking
    A stage timeline (ordered, preparing, on the way, delivered) with the current stage emphasised and an estimate, often alongside a live map. A timeline answers "how long?"; the map answers "where?".

Common mistakes

The map because it looks impressive

Maps make dashboards look sophisticated.

Ask whether a user would make a different decision based on where something is. If not, use a ranked list.

Non-proportional timelines labelled as if they were

Evenly spaced dots are easy to lay out.

If spacing isn't to scale, make that obvious (a stepper, not a time axis); if durations matter, scale them.

Check yourself

Your app has users in 30 Indian states. You want to show where usage is growing fastest. Best first choice?

The question is a ranking of rates, so bars or dots answer it. A choropleth of totals (a) shows population and land area; a pie (d) with 30 slices is unreadable.

Explorable explanations and interactive restraint

Taught by Bret Victor, with Nicky Case, Vi Hart and Hans Rosling

Interactivity earns its place when it lets people ask "what if?" and see the answer immediately. Make the default view tell the story, let interaction deepen it, and never hide the main point behind a hover.

Chapter 1 argued that interaction is a cost in information software. Bret Victor's 2011 essay "Explorable Explanations" is the other side of the coin. He describes reactive documents, where the numbers in a text can be adjusted (by dragging them) and the conclusions update, so the reader can test the author's assumptions instead of trusting them. He built a small JavaScript library, Tangle, for exactly this. In "Up and Down the Ladder of Abstraction" (also 2011) he showed how controlling a parameter and seeing a whole family of results builds intuition that a single example can't.

The interaction here isn't navigation or querying. It's direct manipulation of the model: change an input, see every consequence at once. That's the kind of interactivity that teaches.

Lab: a reactive sentence

If you read 20 pages a day, on 5 days a week, and books average 300 pages, you'll finish about 17 books a year: one every 3 weeks.

Drag the numbers inside the sentence. The conclusion and the row of books update at once. Try finding how many pages a day you'd need for 24 books a year. That search, done by feel, is what Victor means by building intuition.

Examples worth studying

Nicky Case and Vi Hart's Parable of the Polygons (2014) turns Thomas Schelling's segregation model into a playable story: you move shapes, and see how small individual biases produce large-scale segregation. The New York Times Upshot's "You Draw It" (2015) asked readers to draw their guess of a trend before revealing the real data, so the gap between belief and fact was felt, not just read. Hans Rosling's Gapminder talks (his 2006 TED talk is the famous one) animated bubble charts of income and life expectancy over time, with Rosling narrating the story the motion showed. Bartosz Ciechanowski's long interactive articles (on mechanical watches, GPS, cameras) let you rotate and adjust working models of each idea.

Restraint: rules for interactive data

  1. The default view tells the story. Most people won't interact. Analytics on news graphics have repeatedly suggested that only a minority of readers touch the controls, so anything hidden behind interaction is missed by most.
  2. Tooltips are not labels. If a value matters, label it. Use tooltips (or taps) for the secondary details.
  3. Hover doesn't exist on touch. Every hover interaction needs a tap equivalent, and a tap shouldn't be the only way to read critical information.
  4. Immediate feedback. Inputs update the view as they change, with no Apply button, so cause and effect stay connected.
  5. Keep the reference visible. When a parameter changes, show the original (a ghost line, the default value) so people see the difference, not just the new state.
  6. Make state shareable and resettable. A reset button and, on the web, the state in the URL.
  7. Accessible by keyboard and screen reader. Sliders with labels and values, and a text summary that updates (see Accessible & Inclusive).
For the solo creator

A what-if control is one of the most delightful things a small product can offer: a pricing page where you drag your team size and see the cost, a savings app where you drag the monthly amount and see the date you reach your goal, a reading app that shows when you'll finish this book at your current pace. One slider, one live sentence, one small chart. It's a weekend of work and it makes the product feel thoughtful.

Do

  • Use interaction to let people test "what if?" against a model.
  • Tell the main story in the default, static view.
  • Update instantly and keep the original visible for comparison.
  • Give every hover a tap equivalent and every slider a label and value.

Don't

  • Hide key values in tooltips.
  • Add filters and toggles to a monitoring view.
  • Animate transitions so long that cause and effect separate.
  • Build interaction you haven't watched a person use.

  • "You Draw It"The Upshot, New York Times, 2015

Interactivity as a feature checkbox

Interactive charts feel modern and libraries make them easy.

Ask what the reader will learn by interacting that they can't see in the static view. If nothing, ship the static view.

Losing the reference

The chart redraws completely when a parameter changes.

Keep a ghost of the default state so the change itself is visible.

Part four recap · connect the ideas

How do chapter 1 ("interaction is a cost") and chapter 16 ("explorable explanations") fit together?

Victor wrote both. Navigation and queries make people tell the computer what it could infer. Manipulating a model with immediate feedback is the opposite: the computer shows everything that follows from a choice. The same idea links to direct manipulation in Structure & Interaction.

Wrap-up

Ten things to remember

  1. Most software is information software. Design the answer first; interaction is the last resort.
  2. Every mark should carry information. Erase frames, shadows and 3D; keep what helps reading.
  3. Decoration can help memory for one-off stories, but never at the cost of distorting size.
  4. Bars and areas start at zero. Lines may zoom to a meaningful range. Rates beat counts when groups differ.
  5. Position beats length beats angle beats area beats shade. Put the key comparison high on the ladder.
  6. Choose the chart from the question: compare, rank, change, before/after, part, distribution, relationship, pattern.
  7. Title with the finding, label directly, grey the rest and highlight one, sort, and bank to 45°.
  8. Format numbers for the reader: locale grouping (lakh and crore), sensible precision, units, points vs percent.
  9. Tables: numbers right with tabular figures, text left, quiet rules, visible sorts and filters, a deliberate mobile strategy.
  10. Dashboards and personal stats: summary first, normal quiet, exceptions loud, compare people to themselves, and never shame.
