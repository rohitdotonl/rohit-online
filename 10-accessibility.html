Warm-up: what do you already believe?

Before you read, find out where your instincts are already right and where they are quietly wrong.

Most capable developers carry a handful of accessibility myths: that it is mostly about blind people, that it is a final checklist before launch, that adding ARIA makes things more accessible, that it makes interfaces ugly. Each of those beliefs produces specific bugs. Answer the three questions below without scrolling ahead. Getting one wrong is useful: it tells you which chapter to read most slowly.

Also recall two ideas from earlier guides, because this guide builds on them. From The Human Mind: working memory is small and attention is fragile, so every extra thing a person must hold in their head costs them. From Visual Design: hierarchy is made with size, weight, position and contrast, and colour is the weakest of those signals. Accessibility is what happens when you take both ideas seriously for people whose senses, hands or attention differ from yours.

Diagnostic 1

You add a delete icon button (a trash can SVG, no text) to every row of a list. Which single change helps the largest number of people?

c. A native button is focusable and works with Enter and Space for free; the name tells screen-reader and voice-control users what it does and which row it belongs to; the target size helps tremor, touch and older users. Hover tooltips (b) never reach touch or keyboard users. role="button" on an SVG (a) adds a role but no keyboard behaviour. Red (d) is invisible as meaning to many colour-blind users and to every screen-reader user.
Diagnostic 2

An automated scanner like axe or Lighthouse reports zero accessibility issues on your app. What can you honestly conclude?

b. Automated tools find a real but limited slice of problems: estimates range from about 30 to 57 percent depending on how you count. A clean scan is a good start, not a verdict. Chapter 15 teaches the manual passes that cover the rest.
Diagnostic 3

Which of these people does the WCAG "Reflow" requirement (content works at 320 CSS px wide without horizontal scrolling) help most directly?

d. A 1280 px wide window at 400% zoom behaves like a 320 px viewport. If your layout reflows into one column, the zoomed reader scrolls in one direction only. If it does not, they must scroll sideways on every single line. Chapter 5 has a live demo.

Part one

Why it matters and what "accessible" means

Accessibility is not a feature for a small group. It is the measure of how many situations your product survives. This part reframes disability as a mismatch between a person and a design, shows the legal and business ground under your feet, and gives you a compact map of WCAG 2.2 and colour contrast, the standard you will be measured against.

1

Disability is a mismatch, and it is common

Taught by Kat Holmes, with Microsoft Inclusive Design and Angela Glover Blackwell

Disability is not a property of a person; it is a mismatch between a person and the thing they are trying to use. Designers create those mismatches, so designers can remove them.

The World Health Organization estimates that about 1.3 billion people, roughly 16 percent of everyone alive, experience significant disability. That is the permanent slice. Add everyone who is temporarily injured, recovering from surgery, or just ageing (eyesight starts losing near focus in most people's forties) and the group grows. Add everyone in a situation that limits them for a few minutes, and the group becomes "all of your users, some of the time".

Kat Holmes, who led inclusive design at Microsoft, makes the point sharply in her book Mismatch (2018): the medical model treats disability as a condition inside a body, but the social model locates it at the point of contact between body and world. A wheelchair user is not disabled by a ramp. They are disabled by stairs. A blind user is not disabled by a well-labelled button. They are disabled by an unlabelled one. Every time you ship an icon with no name, you build a small staircase.

The persona spectrum

Microsoft's Inclusive Design toolkit (2016) gave designers a simple tool: for each human ability (touch, see, hear, speak) think of a permanent, a temporary and a situational version of the same limitation. A person with one arm, a person with a broken arm in a cast, and a parent holding a baby all need to use your app with one hand. Design for the permanent case and the other two come along for free. That is why the spectrum is useful: it turns a "niche" requirement into a mainstream one.

The curb-cut effect

In the early 1970s, disability activists in Berkeley, California, pushed the city to cut ramps into sidewalk curbs so wheelchair users could cross streets. Once curb cuts existed, everyone used them: parents with strollers, travellers with suitcases, delivery workers with trolleys, kids on bikes. Angela Glover Blackwell named this pattern "the curb-cut effect" in a 2017 essay in the Stanford Social Innovation Review: designs made for a specific group routinely benefit far more people than intended.

Software is full of curb cuts. Captions were made for Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers; today a large share of social video is watched with the sound off. Dark mode and "reduce motion" settings came out of accessibility needs and became mainstream preferences. Keyboard shortcuts help people who cannot use a mouse and make power users fast. Plain language helps people with cognitive disabilities and everyone reading in their second language, which in India is most people using English software.

Key idea

Solve for one, extend to many. Design for the permanent case and you cover the temporary and situational cases too.

What this means for your design process

Treat ability as a range, not a binary. Ask "what if this person cannot see the screen right now, cannot hear, cannot use a pointer, cannot read fast, cannot remember the last screen?" at the moment you design a flow, not after. Most fixes are cheap at sketch time and expensive after launch, because accessibility problems are rarely cosmetic: an unlabelled custom control, a gesture-only action, or a colour-only status are structural decisions.

Do

  • Ask the persona-spectrum question for every new flow: one hand, no sound, no sight, no pointer, low attention.
  • Send important signals through at least two channels (text plus colour, sound plus visual, icon plus label).
  • Respect the system settings people already chose: text size, reduce motion, increase contrast, dark mode.
  • Think of accessibility as product quality, measured like performance.

Don't

  • Treat disabled users as an edge case to handle "later".
  • Assume your users look like you: young, sighted, with a new phone and a fast connection.
  • Build a separate "accessible version"; it always falls behind the main one.
  • Confuse accessibility with a contrast slider bolted onto an otherwise unchanged design.

For the solo creator

You cannot run a research programme with disabled participants every week. You can do three things that cost almost nothing: use your own phone with VoiceOver or TalkBack for ten minutes a week, use your product one-handed and with the screen at minimum brightness in daylight, and put "who does this fail?" at the top of every design note. That last habit catches more mismatches than any audit.

Common mistakes

"My users don't have disabilities"

It feels true because disabled users rarely email to say they left; they just leave. Many disabilities are invisible, and many users never disclose them.

Assume your audience matches the population: one in six with a significant disability, many more with situational limits. Design for that by default.

Accessibility as the last sprint

It is scheduled after "the real work" because it looks like polish. But it lives in structure: custom controls, gestures, flows.

Make it a design-time question and a definition-of-done item for every component. Retrofitting a custom dropdown costs ten times more than choosing a native select.

Designing only for blind users

Screen readers are the most visible assistive technology, so "accessibility" gets reduced to alt text.

Cover all four ability areas (vision, motor, hearing, cognition) plus situational limits. Low vision and cognitive needs affect far more people than total blindness.

Check yourself

You are designing a recipe app. Using the persona spectrum, which feature is the best "solve for one, extend to many" investment?

a. Users with limited hand mobility (permanent), a cut finger (temporary) and anyone with flour on their hands (situational, nearly every cook) all need it. Alt text (c) matters too but serves fewer people in this context. A hidden theme (b) and a statement page (d) do not change the core experience.

The law, the market, and the solo business case

Taught by W3C Web Accessibility Initiative, with the ADA, the European Accessibility Act and India's RPwD Act

Accessibility is increasingly a legal requirement for digital products, but the stronger reason for a solo creator is simpler: accessible products are better products, and they reach more paying people.

This chapter is orientation, not legal advice. Laws differ by country, change over time, and depend on who your customers are and how big your business is. What you need is a working map: which rules exist, which technical standard they almost all point to, and why the cost of complying is lowest if you design for it from the start.

The legal map in one picture

Nearly every accessibility law in the world, whatever its name, ends up pointing at the same technical yardstick: the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), usually at level AA. Learn WCAG once and you have learned the test that most regulators and courts use.

United States

The Americans with Disabilities Act (1990) predates the commercial web, and its Title III, covering "places of public accommodation", has been applied to websites and apps by many courts. Thousands of digital accessibility lawsuits are filed each year, many against small online stores. The best-known case, Robles v. Domino's Pizza, involved a blind customer who could not order through the website and app; in 2019 the Supreme Court declined to hear Domino's appeal, leaving in place a ruling that the ADA applied. In 2024 the Department of Justice adopted a rule requiring state and local government websites and apps to meet WCAG 2.1 AA. Section 508 covers federal agencies and what they buy.

European Union

The European Accessibility Act (Directive 2019/882) applies from 28 June 2025. It covers a defined list of products and services sold to consumers in the EU, including e-commerce, consumer banking, e-books and the software used to read them, electronic communications and parts of transport. Its technical standard is EN 301 549, which incorporates WCAG for web and app content. Microenterprises providing services (fewer than 10 staff and annual turnover or balance sheet of no more than €2 million) are exempt from the service requirements. If you are a one-person studio you are likely exempt today, but that changes the day you grow, sell through a larger partner, or a customer asks for a conformance report.

India

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 requires accessibility in information and communication technology (sections 40 and 42 in particular) and the government publishes guidelines to implement it. The Guidelines for Indian Government Websites and Apps (GIGW 3.0, 2023) align with WCAG 2.1 for public-sector sites and apps, and the Bureau of Indian Standards publishes IS 17802, an ICT accessibility standard modelled on the European EN 301 549. If you build for Indian government, banks, or education, expect these to come up in procurement.

Watch out

"Accessibility overlay" widgets, the scripts that add a toolbar and promise instant compliance, do not fix inaccessible markup and are widely criticised by disabled users and accessibility practitioners. Several companies using them have still been sued. Fix the product itself.

The business case when you are one person

Large companies justify accessibility with risk and market size. Your case is more direct:

  1. Reach. One in six people, plus everyone over 50 losing near vision, plus everyone on a cheap phone in sunlight. Many of your competitors fail these people, so they look for the products that do not.
  2. Loyalty. Disabled users who find a product that works tend to stay, recommend it in their communities, and write the reviews that tell others it is safe to try. Word travels fast in small, connected communities.
  3. Quality. Accessible code is semantic, testable and resilient. Native buttons and labelled fields work with browser autofill, password managers, voice assistants, UI test tools and AI agents that operate interfaces through the same accessibility tree.
  4. Cost. Doing it at design time is nearly free. Retrofitting after launch, or after a demand letter, is slow and stressful, and you have no team to absorb it.
  5. Sales. Business and education buyers often ask for an accessibility conformance report (a VPAT or ACR). Having one lets a one-person product compete with bigger vendors.

Do

  • Target WCAG 2.2 AA from day one; it covers most laws.
  • Publish a short, honest accessibility statement: what you support, known gaps, how to contact you.
  • Keep an a11y-log.md of known issues and fix dates; it is your conformance report in embryo.

Don't

  • Install an overlay widget and call it done.
  • Claim "fully WCAG compliant" without having tested; a false claim is worse than an honest gap list.
  • Rely on a small-business exemption as a design strategy.

In the wild

  • GOV.UKGovernment Digital Service, UK, 2012 onwards
    Publishes an accessibility statement on its services, tests with disabled users, and open-sources an accessible design system that many governments copy. Being public about what works and what does not builds trust faster than a vague "we care about accessibility".
  • Domino'sUS, case decided on appeal 2019
    Years of litigation over a pizza ordering flow that a screen reader could not complete. The cheapest version of that fix was a labelled form in the first place.
  • E-reading apps in the EUEAA scope, 2025

For the solo creator

Write your accessibility statement now, even if it says "we are testing with VoiceOver and keyboard; known gaps: the chart view". It costs fifteen minutes, gives disabled users a way to reach you, and forces you to know your own gaps. Link it from your footer and settings.

Common mistakes

Buying an overlay

It promises compliance with one line of script, which is very tempting when you are alone.

Spend the same money and an afternoon on the solo audit in Chapter 15. Fix the source.

Treating the law as the ceiling

"AA" sounds like a finish line.

Treat AA as the floor. Some AAA criteria (7:1 body contrast, 44 px targets, no timing) are easy wins for a calm product.

Check yourself

A university in Pune wants to license your note-taking app for 4,000 students. Their procurement form asks about accessibility. What is the most useful thing to send?

c. Buyers need specific, honest information they can map to their own obligations. A Lighthouse score (a) covers only automatable checks. An unqualified claim (b) is unverifiable and risky. Exemptions (d) depend on jurisdiction and do not help you win the deal.

WCAG 2.2 for app makers, in one sitting

Taught by W3C WAI, with WebAIM

WCAG is organised around four principles: content must be Perceivable, Operable, Understandable and Robust (POUR). Around twenty of its success criteria cause most real-world failures in apps; learn those cold.

WCAG 2.2 became a W3C Recommendation in October 2023. It is backwards compatible: anything that meets 2.2 also meets 2.1 and 2.0. It has 86 success criteria (one older criterion, 4.1.1 Parsing, was removed as obsolete), each graded A, AA or AAA. Level A is the bare minimum, AA is what laws and contracts usually ask for, and AAA is a stricter set that is not required as a whole for entire sites because some content cannot meet it.

Do not try to memorise all 86. Understand the four principles as questions you ask of every screen, and memorise the handful of numeric thresholds, because numbers are where designs fail silently.

How to read a criterion

Each criterion on the W3C site has an "Understanding" page that explains intent, who it helps, and examples, plus "Techniques" that show sufficient ways to meet it and common failures. When you are unsure whether something passes, read the Understanding page rather than a blog summary; the edge cases are written there. The W3C's "How to Meet WCAG (Quick Reference)" lets you filter by level and topic, which is the fastest way to build your own short list.

What about WCAG 3?

W3C is developing WCAG 3, a different structure with outcomes and a new scoring model. As of late 2026 it is still a Working Draft, not a standard, and laws do not reference it. Read it to understand where things are heading; build to 2.2 AA.

Do

  • Learn the numbers: 4.5:1, 3:1, 200%, 320 px, 24 px, 20 seconds.
  • Read the Understanding page when in doubt.
  • Map your components to criteria once (button: 2.1.1, 2.4.7, 2.5.8, 4.1.2) and reuse the map.

Don't

  • Treat WCAG as the whole of usability; a page can pass and still be confusing.
  • Read success criteria as loopholes ("it says 'only', so…").
  • Ignore AAA entirely; several items are cheap for a calm, minimal product.

Common mistakes

Confusing "large text" with "big enough"

The 3:1 allowance is for text 24 CSS px and up (or about 18.66 px bold). Designers apply it to 18 px regular labels.

Body copy and UI labels at normal sizes need 4.5:1. Check the actual rendered size.

Forgetting non-text contrast

Text gets checked, but input borders at #E5E5E5 on white, faint toggle tracks and focus rings slip through.

Anything a person must see to operate a control needs 3:1 against what is next to it.

Sticky things hiding focus

Sticky headers and footers are added late and nobody tabs behind them.

Use CSS scroll-padding-top equal to the sticky header height and tab through every page after adding a sticky element.

Check yourself

Your sign-in screen sends a 6-digit code by SMS and has a "paste" blocker on the field "for security". Which criterion does the paste blocker most directly hurt?

b. Transcribing a code is a cognitive function test. Allowing paste, and better, using autocomplete="one-time-code" so the phone offers the code automatically, removes the test.

Contrast: the ratio, the reason, and APCA

Taught by W3C WAI, with Andrew Somers (APCA) and Lea Verou

Contrast is the difference in lightness between text and its background. Below a threshold, reading becomes slow and tiring for everyone and impossible for some; WCAG sets that threshold at 4.5:1 for body text.

Our eyes are far more sensitive to differences in lightness (luminance) than to differences in hue. Contrast sensitivity also falls with age, with conditions like cataracts and glaucoma, with screen glare, and with cheap displays. A pale grey that looks elegant on your calibrated monitor at night can vanish on a budget phone in the afternoon sun. Low-contrast text is the single most common failure on the web in WebAIM's yearly scans.

How the WCAG ratio works

The formula converts each colour to relative luminance: it undoes the sRGB gamma curve, then weights the channels by how sensitive the eye is to each (roughly 21% red, 72% green, 7% blue). The ratio is (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05), where L1 is the lighter colour. It runs from 1:1 (identical) to 21:1 (black on white). The 0.05 term models a little ambient light falling on the screen.

Lab: check any pair of colours

Pick a text colour and a background. The lab computes the WCAG 2 ratio live and gives verdicts for body text, large text and UI components. Try the presets, then try to find the lightest grey on white that still passes 4.5:1 (it is close to #767676).

Where the WCAG 2 formula is imperfect

The WCAG 2 formula is simple and widely implemented, but it has known weaknesses. It treats a pair the same whichever colour is the text, while real perception differs between dark-on-light and light-on-dark. It tends to overstate the contrast of dark colour pairs (some dark greys on black pass on paper but are hard to read) and can understate some mid-tone pairs, such as white text on certain oranges and blues that many people find perfectly readable. It also ignores font size and weight beyond the single "large text" threshold, though thin, small text needs much more contrast than bold, large text.

APCA: an emerging model, honestly described

The Accessible Perceptual Contrast Algorithm (APCA), developed by Andrew Somers, tries to model perceived lightness contrast more accurately. It is polarity-aware (dark text on light is scored differently from light text on dark), reports a value called Lc (lightness contrast) from roughly 0 to about 106 for dark-on-light, and links the required contrast to font size and weight through lookup tables. As a rough guide from its documentation, Lc 90 is preferred for body text, Lc 75 is a minimum for body text, and lower values are acceptable for large or bold text and non-text elements.

Its status matters. APCA has appeared in drafts and discussions around WCAG 3, but it is not part of any W3C Recommendation, WCAG 3 is still a Working Draft, and no law references APCA. The honest practice in 2026 is: meet WCAG 2 ratios because that is what you are measured against; use APCA as a second opinion, especially for dark mode and for thin or small type, where WCAG 2 can approve combinations that are actually hard to read.

Myth

"High contrast looks harsh." Pure black on pure white at large sizes can glare, and many calm products use a near-black on an off-white (this course uses an ink of about 15:1 on its paper). That is still far above 4.5:1. Calm comes from restraint in the number of colours and weights, not from washing out text.

Do

  • Define a muted text token that passes 4.5:1 on every surface it sits on, in light and dark themes.
  • Check placeholder text, disabled-looking labels, captions and timestamps; they fail most often.
  • Test text on images with the worst part of the image, or add a scrim.
  • Respect "Increase contrast" (iOS) and prefers-contrast: more with a slightly stronger palette.

Don't

  • Use light font weights (300 and below) for body text at small sizes.
  • Put white text on yellow, light orange or light green buttons.
  • Rely on opacity to make text secondary; the result varies by background.
  • Use placeholder text as the only label (it is usually too light, and it disappears).

  • Windows contrast themesMicrosoft

Common mistakes

Checking light mode only

Dark themes are often made by inverting and dimming, and muted greys end up too dark against near-black.

Low vision: zoom, large text and reflow

Taught by W3C WAI, with Apple Human Interface Guidelines and Material Design

Many more people have low vision than are blind, and most of them use what they already have: bigger text and zoom. Your job is to make sure your layout survives both without clipping, overlapping or scrolling sideways.

Low vision covers blurred vision, loss of central vision (macular degeneration), loss of peripheral vision (glaucoma), light sensitivity and reduced contrast sensitivity. Add the age-related loss of near focus (presbyopia) that affects nearly everyone from their mid-forties, and a large share of your users will prefer text bigger than your default. On phones they raise the system text size. On desktops they use browser zoom, sometimes up to 400%, or a screen magnifier that shows a small portion of the screen enlarged.

Two mechanisms matter. First, text scaling: the system or browser makes text larger while the rest of the layout stays the same size, unless you sized it relative to text. Second, zoom: the whole page scales, which on the web shrinks the effective viewport (1280 px at 400% is a 320 px viewport). Layouts break in predictable ways under each: fixed heights clip text, fixed widths force sideways scrolling, absolute positioning makes things overlap, and truncation hides the very words people zoomed in to read.

Colour vision: never let colour work alone

Taught by W3C WAI (1.4.1 Use of Color), with Masataka Okabe and Kei Ito (colour-blind-safe palettes)

About one in twelve men and one in two hundred women of Northern European ancestry have some form of colour vision deficiency. If colour is the only thing that distinguishes "error" from "success" or one chart line from another, those people cannot tell them apart.

Most of us have three kinds of cone cells, sensitive roughly to long (red), medium (green) and short (blue) wavelengths. Colour vision deficiency happens when one kind is missing or shifted. The common forms are inherited and carried on the X chromosome, which is why far more men are affected.

Deuteranomaly / deuteranopia
Medium-wavelength (green) cones shifted or missing. The most common form. Reds, greens, oranges and browns collapse toward similar olive and yellow tones.
Protanomaly / protanopia
Long-wavelength (red) cones shifted or missing. Similar confusion to deuteranopia, and reds also look darker, so red text on black can nearly disappear.
Tritanomaly / tritanopia
Short-wavelength (blue) cones affected. Rare. Blues and greens, and yellows and pinks, become confusable.
Achromatopsia
Very rare total colour blindness, often with light sensitivity. The world appears in shades of grey.

The practical rule, WCAG 1.4.1, is simple: colour may reinforce meaning but must not carry it alone. Pair it with text, an icon, a shape, a pattern, a position or an underline. This also helps people on monochrome e-ink screens, people in bright sunlight, people with night-shift filters, and anyone who prints your page in black and white.

  • Remove underlines from links in running text without another non-colour cue.
  • Assume dark mode fixes colour problems; it often makes reds darker and harder to see.

How screen readers actually work

Taught by Léonie Watson, with W3C WAI-ARIA and WebAIM

A screen reader does not read your pixels. It reads a parallel structure, the accessibility tree, in which every meaningful element has a role, a name, a state and a place in order. If you get those four right, the experience follows.

When your app renders, the browser or operating system builds two things: the visual layout and an accessibility tree. The tree is a simplified version of your interface that platform accessibility APIs expose to assistive technology: NSAccessibility and UIAccessibility on Apple platforms, AccessibilityNodeInfo on Android, UI Automation and IAccessible2 on Windows. Screen readers, voice control, switch control and automated test tools all read this tree. Purely decorative elements are dropped; generic containers mostly disappear; buttons, links, headings, fields and landmarks remain.

Role, name, state, value

Every interactive node needs four answers. Role: what kind of thing is it (button, link, checkbox, tab, slider, heading)? The role tells users what they can do with it and tells the screen reader which keys work. Name: what is it called ("Save", "Search notes", "Groceries")? State: is it checked, expanded, selected, pressed, disabled, busy? Value: for things like sliders and text fields, what is the current value? WCAG 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value is the criterion behind all of this.

A screen reader combines them into an announcement, usually in the order name, role, state, sometimes followed by a hint. VoiceOver on iOS might say "Dark mode, switch button, on. Double-tap to toggle setting." NVDA on Windows might say "Dark mode, toggle button, pressed". The exact words differ between screen readers and versions; the information is the same.

The accessible name is computed by a defined algorithm (the W3C's Accessible Name and Description Computation). In practice, the main sources in priority order are: aria-labelledby (text of other elements), aria-label, the native label (a <label> for an input, alt for an image), the element's own text content for buttons, links and headings, and finally title or placeholder as weak fallbacks.

Reading order, headings and landmarks

Screen-reader users rarely listen to a page from top to bottom. They skim, like sighted users do, but with the keyboard or gestures: jump from heading to heading (the H key in NVDA and JAWS, the rotor on VoiceOver), jump between landmarks (main, navigation, search, banner, contentinfo), or pull up a list of all links. In WebAIM's screen-reader surveys, navigating by headings has consistently been the most common way to find information on a long page, used by roughly two thirds or more of respondents. So your headings are your table of contents.

Reading order follows the order in the code (DOM order on the web, view hierarchy order on iOS and Android), not the visual position. If you use CSS grid, order, absolute positioning or flex reversal to move things visually, the screen reader will still read them in source order. Keep the source order the same as the visual order.

Meet the main screen readers

VoiceOver is built into every Apple device. On iPhone: Settings > Accessibility > VoiceOver, or set the Accessibility Shortcut to a triple-click of the side button. Swipe right or left to move to the next or previous element, double-tap to activate, rotate two fingers to open the rotor and pick "Headings", then swipe up or down to jump between them. TalkBack is Google's screen reader for Android, part of Android Accessibility Suite. Enable it in Settings > Accessibility > TalkBack, or set a volume-key shortcut. Swipe right or left to move, double-tap to activate. Reading controls let you move by headings, links or controls. Test on a mid-range or low-end device too: TalkBack on slow hardware exposes performance problems that become accessibility problems.

NVDA (NonVisual Desktop Access) is a free, open-source screen reader for Windows from NV Access, first released in 2006. Together with JAWS (a commercial reader from Freedom Scientific) it is among the most used screen readers on desktop in WebAIM's surveys. Press H to jump between headings, D for landmarks, Tab for focusable controls, Insert-F7 for a list of elements. Install it once on a Windows machine or virtual machine to test web apps; it behaves differently from VoiceOver in useful ways.

  • Give every control a role, a clear name and its state.
  • Use real headings in a logical outline; one h1 per screen.
  • Keep DOM order equal to visual order.
  • Announce async results ("Saved", "3 results") with a polite live region or the platform's announcement API.

Don't

  • Repeat the role in the name ("Delete button button").
  • Use headings for styling or skip levels for size.
  • Hide content with aria-hidden that is still focusable.
  • Rearrange visual order with CSS so it no longer matches reading order.

Writing alt text and labels that help

Taught by W3C WAI images tutorial, with WebAIM

A text alternative should do the job the image does, in the context where it appears. Describe the purpose, not the pixels; and say nothing at all for images that are purely decorative.

Alt text is writing, and it follows the same rule as all UI writing in Words: say what the reader needs, in as few words as possible. The trick is to ask "what would I say if I were reading this screen aloud to a friend on the phone?" For a logo in a header that links home, you would say the product name. For a photo of a recipe step, you would describe what the cook should notice. For a decorative flourish, you would say nothing.

  • Put essential text only inside images.

Hearing: captions, transcripts and visible sound

Taught by W3C WAI media guide, with Deaf and hard-of-hearing communities

Anything your product says with sound must also be said with sight: captions for video, transcripts for audio, and visual or haptic equivalents for alerts.

Hearing loss is common and increases with age; many people are hard of hearing rather than profoundly deaf, and some Deaf people use a sign language as their first language and read captions as a second language. Add the situational group (open-plan offices, trains, sleeping babies) and the case for visual alternatives is overwhelming. For a software product, sound appears in three places: media (video, audio, podcasts), feedback (alerts, chimes, notification sounds) and voice interfaces.

Captions done properly

Captions are synchronised text of speech plus meaningful sound ("[door slams]", "[soft piano music]"). Subtitles usually translate speech only, assuming the viewer can hear. Good captions are accurate, synchronised, complete and readable: one or two lines, high contrast, not covering important on-screen text, with speaker identification when it is not obvious. Automatic captions have improved a lot but still mangle names, jargon, accents and Indian English mixed with Hindi; correct them for anything you publish.

Transcripts

For audio-only content (podcasts, voice notes), provide a transcript. For video, a transcript is also useful: it lets people skim, search, read at their own pace, and use a braille display. Deaf-blind users rely on text that can be sent to braille. A transcript is also great for search engines and for you, since it becomes a blog post.

Visible and tangible feedback

If a sound means something (message received, timer done, error), pair it with a visual change and, on phones, a haptic. Never make the sound the only cue. Allow sounds to be turned off and respect system mute. For voice features, offer a typed alternative: people with speech differences, strong accents, or in quiet places cannot always speak to your app.

Do

  • Caption every video you publish, including marketing and onboarding videos.
  • Correct auto-captions for names, product terms and code.
  • Pair sounds with visual and haptic cues.
  • Offer typed input wherever you offer voice input.

Don't

  • Autoplay video with sound.
  • Burn captions into the video as the only option; users cannot resize or restyle them.
  • Use phone verification by voice call as the only option.
  • Rely on a sound to confirm a destructive action.

Keyboard and focus: the spine of operability

Taught by W3C WAI, with Sara Soueidan and Heydon Pickering

If something works with a mouse, it must work with a keyboard. And a keyboard user must always be able to see where they are: the focus indicator is their mouse pointer.

Keyboard access is the foundation that many other assistive technologies build on. Switch devices, sip-and-puff controllers, some voice control modes and many screen-reader interactions are translated into keyboard-like "move next" and "activate" commands. People with tremor, repetitive strain injury or limited fine motor control often prefer the keyboard. Power users choose it because it is fast. If your app is fully keyboard operable with a visible focus, you have served all of them.

The keyboard contract

Users expect a small, consistent set of keys. Tab and Shift+Tab move between interactive elements. Enter activates links and buttons. Space activates buttons and toggles checkboxes. Arrow keys move within a composite widget (radio group, tabs, menu, listbox, grid). Escape closes the thing that just opened (dialog, menu, popover). Home and End jump to the first and last items in a list-like widget. Native elements already behave this way; custom ones must be taught every key.

Designing a focus indicator

Browsers draw a default focus ring, but many designs remove it with outline: none because it appears on mouse click. The modern fix is :focus-visible, which browsers apply when focus came from the keyboard (and for text inputs always), so you can style a strong indicator without it flashing on every click. A good indicator is thick enough to see (2 to 3 px or more), contrasts at least 3:1 with the colours around it, is not only a colour change of the element itself, and is not hidden by overflow or sticky elements. WCAG 2.2's AAA criterion 2.4.13 Focus Appearance gives a precise target: an outline area at least as big as a 2 CSS px perimeter, with 3:1 contrast between focused and unfocused states.

/* One focus style for the whole product */
:focus-visible {
  outline: 3px solid var(--focus);
  outline-offset: 2px;
}
/* Keep focused things clear of sticky headers (2.4.11) */

Focus management

When the interface changes, put focus somewhere sensible. Opening a dialog moves focus into it; closing returns focus to the button that opened it. Deleting a list item moves focus to the next item (or the list heading), not to the top of the page. After a route change in a single-page app, move focus to the new page's heading or main region, otherwise screen-reader users have no idea anything changed. Never use positive tabindex values (1, 2, 3); they create an order that fights the DOM. Use tabindex="0" to make a custom element focusable and tabindex="-1" to make something focusable only by script.

Skip links

A skip link is the first focusable element on a page, usually hidden until focused, that jumps to the main content. Without it, a keyboard user tabs through every header link on every page. In apps with a sidebar, a "Skip to editor" or "Skip to results" link saves dozens of key presses.

Do

  • Use native buttons, links and inputs.
  • Style :focus-visible with a thick, high-contrast ring, globally.
  • Return focus to the trigger when a dialog or menu closes.
  • Document your keyboard shortcuts and avoid single-letter shortcuts that can't be turned off or remapped (2.1.4).

Don't

  • Write outline: none without a replacement.
  • Use positive tabindex.
  • Create keyboard traps (a widget you can Tab into but not out of).
  • Make hover the only way to reveal actions.

Targets, gestures, switches, voice and time

Taught by W3C WAI, with Apple HIG, Material Design and Paul Fitts

People reach your interface with fingers that shake, a single switch, their voice, a head pointer, or one thumb on a moving bus. Make targets big and well spaced, give every gesture a simple alternative, and never make people race a clock.

Fitts's law, which you met in The Human Mind, says the time to hit a target grows as it gets smaller and farther away. For people with tremor, arthritis, cerebral palsy, Parkinson's, or cold hands at a bus stop, small targets are not just slower; they produce errors, and errors on destructive actions are costly. Motor accessibility is mostly about margin: bigger targets, more space, simpler gestures, more time, undo.

Target size

WCAG 2.2's 2.5.8 sets a minimum of 24 by 24 CSS pixels (or enough spacing). Platform guidance is more generous and you should follow it: Apple recommends at least 44 by 44 points, Material Design 48 by 48 dp (about 9 mm). The visible icon can be smaller than the target; extend the hit area with padding. Space destructive actions away from frequent ones.

Lab: target size and tremor
Tap or click the "Save" target. Each tap is shifted by a random amount, like a hand tremor.
"Save" sits between "Archive" and "Delete". With a small target, small gap and some tremor, a surprising share of taps hit the wrong button, and one of the neighbours destroys data. Raise size and gap until the simulated miss rate falls near zero; notice it happens around 44 px.

Gestures and dragging

Complex gestures (multi-finger swipes, pinches, path-based gestures like drawing a shape) are hard or impossible for many people, and invisible to everyone until learned. WCAG 2.5.1 Pointer Gestures requires that anything done with a multipoint or path-based gesture can also be done with a single pointer without a path (a tap or click). 2.5.7 Dragging Movements, new in 2.2, extends that to dragging: reordering a list, moving a slider, dragging a card between columns must also be possible with taps or clicks, such as "Move up" and "Move down" buttons or a "Move to…" menu. 2.5.4 Motion Actuation says shake-to-undo and tilt controls need a regular control too and must be possible to turn off.

Switch control and voice control

Switch users operate a device with one or two buttons (or a head movement, a sip-and-puff tube, a blink). iOS Switch Control and Android Switch Access scan through the interface, highlighting groups and then items, and the user presses the switch to select. Every extra focusable element adds scanning time, so clean grouping and logical order matter enormously. Elements that only appear on hover or after a timeout are often unreachable.

Voice control users speak commands: "Tap Save", "Scroll down", "Show numbers" (which overlays a number on every control). iOS Voice Control, macOS Voice Control, Android Voice Access and Windows Voice Access all match spoken words against accessible names. If your visible button says "Send" but its accessible name is "Submit message form", saying "Tap Send" fails. That is WCAG 2.5.3 Label in Name: the accessible name must contain the visible label, ideally starting with it.

Time limits

Some people read slowly, type slowly with a head pointer, or need to look something up. WCAG 2.2.1 requires that time limits can be turned off, adjusted, or extended (with a warning and at least 20 seconds to respond), unless the limit is essential (like a live auction). 2.2.2 requires that moving, blinking or auto-updating content lasting more than five seconds can be paused. In practice: don't auto-dismiss toasts that contain actions (like Undo) in three seconds; don't auto-advance carousels; warn before a session expires and let people extend it with one tap; save drafts so a timeout never loses work.

Do

  • Use 44 pt / 48 dp targets on touch; at least 24 px on the web, with spacing.
  • Give every drag and multi-finger gesture a tap or menu alternative.
  • Match accessible names to visible labels.
  • Let people extend or turn off time limits; save drafts constantly.
  • Place destructive actions away from common ones and offer undo.

Don't

  • Use swipe-only actions with no visible equivalent.
  • Trigger actions on pointer-down; trigger on release so people can slide off to cancel (2.5.2).
  • Auto-advance carousels or auto-dismiss toasts with actions.
  • Rely on shake, tilt or long-press as the only way to do something.

Cognitive accessibility and neurodiversity

Taught by W3C COGA Task Force, with Steve Krug and the plain-language movement

Cognitive accessibility means reducing what people must understand, remember and decide at once. It helps people with learning disabilities, dyslexia, ADHD, autism, dementia, brain injury and anxiety, and it helps every tired, distracted human using your product.

Cognitive and learning disabilities are the largest and least visible group. They vary enormously: difficulty with reading, with numbers, with short-term memory, with sustained attention, with processing speed, with unexpected change, with ambiguity. WCAG 2.x covers only part of this territory, because many cognitive needs are hard to test as pass or fail. So the W3C's Cognitive and Learning Disabilities Accessibility Task Force (COGA) published a separate guide, Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities (2021), organised around eight objectives.

Plain language

Write at a level most people can read quickly when tired: common words, short sentences, active voice, one idea per sentence, the most important thing first. This is not "dumbing down"; it is respect for attention. Everything in Words applies with extra force here. Avoid idioms and metaphors that confuse literal readers and second-language readers ("ballpark figure", "circle back"). Explain numbers ("₹499 a year, about ₹42 a month"). Use the same word for the same thing everywhere.

Memory and predictability

Working memory holds only a few items. Every time your product asks people to remember something from a previous screen (a code, a choice, an instruction), some of them will lose it. Show what they need where they need it: keep earlier answers visible in a summary, let password managers fill fields, allow paste, offer "magic link" or passkey login as alternatives to memorised passwords (WCAG 3.3.8). Predictability matters too: WCAG 3.2.3 and 3.2.4 ask for consistent navigation and consistently named components; 3.2.6 asks for help to be in a consistent place. Unexpected changes of context, such as a form that submits when you pick a dropdown option, disorient everyone and can stop some people entirely (3.2.2).

Attention, distraction and anxiety

For people with ADHD, distraction is not a minor annoyance; it can end the task. For anxious users, countdowns ("Offer ends in 04:59"), scarcity messages and aggressive red warnings raise stress and cause mistakes. Calm products are cognitively accessible products: one primary action per screen, no auto-playing motion, notifications that are few and useful, and reassurance at moments of risk ("You can change this later", "Nothing is charged until you confirm"). Respect prefers-reduced-motion (see Motion & Feel): parallax and large animated transitions can cause nausea for people with vestibular disorders, and flashing more than three times a second can trigger seizures (WCAG 2.3.1).

Reading differences: dyslexia

Dyslexia affects decoding written words. What helps is well established and simple: left-aligned (not justified) text, generous line spacing (around 1.5), comfortable line lengths, sans or humanist fonts with clear letter shapes, no long blocks of italics or capitals, and the ability to change font size and spacing. Specially designed "dyslexia fonts" are popular, but controlled studies have generally not found that they improve reading speed or accuracy compared with ordinary clear fonts; what matters more is spacing, size and the reader's own preference. Offer choice (as Kindle and many reading apps do) rather than one prescribed font. Text-to-speech and read-aloud support are often more helpful than any font.

Do

  • Use plain language and explain unusual words.
  • Keep navigation, labels and help in consistent places.
  • Show progress and earlier answers in multi-step flows.
  • Prevent errors (good defaults, forgiving formats) and offer undo.
  • Let people control motion, text size, spacing and notifications.

Don't

  • Use countdown timers or fake scarcity.
  • Change context unexpectedly (auto-submit, auto-redirect).
  • Require memory tests at login (no paste, CAPTCHAs without alternatives).
  • Fill screens with competing calls to action and badges.

  • PasskeysApple, Google, Microsoft, 2022 onwards

  • Apple Assistive AccessiOS 17, 2023

Semantic HTML first, ARIA last, native APIs always

Taught by W3C (Using ARIA), with Heydon Pickering, Apple and Google accessibility docs

Native elements and platform controls come with roles, names, states, focus and keyboard behaviour built in. ARIA only changes what assistive technology is told; it adds no behaviour. So use native first, and reach for ARIA only to fill gaps.

The W3C's Using ARIA document states the first rule of ARIA use: if you can use a native HTML element or attribute with the semantics and behaviour you need already built in, do that instead of repurposing an element and adding an ARIA role, state or property. The WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices Guide puts it more bluntly: no ARIA is better than bad ARIA. WebAIM's annual scans support this: home pages that use ARIA tend to have more detected errors, on average, than those that don't, because ARIA is so often used to patch over the wrong element.

The five rules of ARIA, in practice

  1. Prefer native. button, a href, input, select, textarea, details/summary, dialog, fieldset/legend, label, table, h1–h6, nav, main, ul/ol.
  2. Don't change native semantics unless you really must. Don't put role="button" on a heading; put a button inside the heading.
  3. All interactive ARIA controls must be keyboard operable. If you add role="tab", you owe the user arrow keys.
  4. Don't hide focusable elements with aria-hidden="true" or role="presentation".
  5. Every interactive element needs an accessible name.

The ARIA you will actually use most is small: aria-label and aria-labelledby (names), aria-describedby (hints and errors), aria-expanded, aria-pressed, aria-checked, aria-selected, aria-current="page" (states), aria-hidden="true" for decorative icons, aria-live or role="status" for announcements, and aria-invalid for fields in error.

Component patterns: dialog, menu, tabs, combobox

Taught by WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices Guide (APG), with Scott O'Hara and Adrian Roselli

A few composite components cause most keyboard and screen-reader failures. Each has a known contract (roles, states, and which keys do what) published in the W3C's Authoring Practices Guide. Learn the contract, or better, use a well-tested implementation.

The APG is a W3C resource that documents design patterns with roles, states and keyboard interaction, plus working examples. It is guidance rather than a standard, and its examples are not production code, but it is the best shared definition of how components should behave. Below are the four you are most likely to build, with their contracts reduced to what you must remember.

Modal dialog

Contract: role="dialog" (or the native <dialog> opened with showModal()), aria-modal="true", a name from its heading. On open, focus moves inside. Tab and Shift+Tab cycle within it. Escape closes. On close, focus returns to the element that opened it. The rest of the page is inert (not focusable, not read).

Menu button

Contract: a button with aria-haspopup="menu" and aria-expanded. Enter, Space or Down Arrow opens the menu and focuses the first item; Up Arrow can focus the last. Inside, arrow keys move, Home and End jump, typing a letter jumps to the matching item, Enter activates, Escape closes and returns focus to the button. Items use role="menuitem" inside role="menu". Important nuance: ARIA menus are for application-style action menus. Site navigation is not a menu; use a list of links in a nav, with a disclosure button if it collapses.

Tabs

Contract: role="tablist" containing role="tab" elements, each with aria-selected and aria-controls pointing to a role="tabpanel". Only the selected tab is in the Tab order (roving tabindex). Left and Right Arrow move between tabs; Home and End go to first and last. Tab moves from the tab list into the panel. Selection can follow focus (automatic) if panels load instantly, or wait for Enter or Space (manual) if they are slow.

Combobox (autocomplete)

Contract: a text input with role="combobox", aria-expanded, aria-controls pointing at a role="listbox" of role="option" items, and aria-activedescendant naming the highlighted option while focus stays in the input. Down Arrow opens and moves through options, Enter selects, Escape closes (a second Escape may clear). Announce the number of results with a polite status message ("5 suggestions"). Comboboxes are the hardest of these to get right across screen readers; if a native <select> or <datalist> will do, use it, and if not, use a well-tested library component.

Testing accessibility on your own

Taught by Deque (axe), UK GDS, with WebAIM

Automated tools catch a meaningful but limited share of accessibility problems. A repeatable manual routine (keyboard, screen reader, zoom, grayscale, contrast) catches most of the rest, and testing with disabled people catches what you would never think of.

How much do automated tools find? Estimates vary with what is counted. In a 2017 experiment, the UK Government Digital Service built a page with 142 deliberate accessibility barriers and ran it through 13 automated tools; even the best found well under half of them. Deque, which makes axe, reported in 2021 that across its audits, axe-core's automated rules found about 57 percent of issues by volume, because the most common issues (like contrast) are also the most automatable. A fair summary: tools reliably find missing names, contrast failures, missing labels, invalid ARIA and some structure problems; they cannot judge whether alt text is meaningful, whether focus order makes sense, whether a flow is understandable, or whether a custom widget actually works with a keyboard.

The solo audit routine (about 90 minutes)

  1. Automated scan, 10 minutes. Run axe DevTools or Lighthouse on each main screen of your web app; run Xcode's Accessibility Inspector audit on iOS and Google's Accessibility Scanner on Android. Fix everything they report; they are cheap wins.
  2. Keyboard pass, 15 minutes. No mouse. Tab through each main task. Check: reachable, visible focus, sensible order, no traps, Escape closes things, focus returns after dialogs, nothing hidden under sticky headers.
  3. Screen-reader pass, 25 minutes. Run the script below with VoiceOver (Mac or iPhone), TalkBack or NVDA.
  4. Zoom and text size, 10 minutes. 200% text size; 400% browser zoom or a 320 px window; iOS largest accessibility size. Look for clipping, overlap, truncation and sideways scrolling.
  5. Colour and contrast, 10 minutes. Grayscale filter on; check every status and chart. Spot-check muted text, placeholders, borders and focus rings with a contrast checker in both themes.
  6. Motion and time, 5 minutes. Turn on Reduce Motion; check that large animations calm down. Wait out every timer and toast; check you can still act.
  7. Log and prioritise, 15 minutes. Write each issue with the criterion, the user it blocks, severity (blocker, serious, minor) and the fix.

Languages, scripts, directions and names

Taught by W3C Internationalization, with Patrick McKenzie ("Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names") and the Unicode CLDR

English is a short, left-to-right language with a small alphabet and simple names. Design as if it were the exception: leave room for longer text, mirror for right-to-left scripts, support every script your users write, and never assume a name or an address has a fixed shape.

Text expansion

Translated text is usually longer than English. As a rough rule, long paragraphs grow by 20 to 40 percent in languages like German, French or Hindi, and short UI labels can double or triple ("Save" becomes "Speichern"; "Add" becomes "Hinzufügen"). Some languages are shorter in characters but taller: Indic scripts like Devanagari and Tamil have marks above and below the line and need more line height; Chinese and Japanese are compact but need larger sizes to stay legible. Layouts that fit English exactly will break.

Right-to-left layouts

Arabic, Hebrew, Persian and Urdu are read right to left. In an RTL interface the whole layout mirrors: navigation starts on the right, the back arrow points right, progress fills from right to left, list chevrons point left, and text aligns right. Some things do not mirror: phone numbers, most numerals in many contexts, media playback controls (play points right in most RTL interfaces), logos, and images of real objects like clocks. On the web, set dir="rtl" and use CSS logical properties (margin-inline-start, padding-inline-end, inset-inline-start, text-align: start) so one stylesheet works both ways. iOS and Android mirror standard layouts automatically if you use leading and trailing rather than left and right.

Names and addresses

Patrick McKenzie's 2010 essay "Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names" lists assumptions that break real people: that everyone has exactly one first name and one last name, that names don't change, that names are written in one script, that names have no spaces or punctuation, that a name field can be required to have at least two characters. Many people in India use a single given name with an initial standing for a father's name or village; many people across South and Southeast Asia have no family name at all; Spanish and Portuguese naming uses two surnames; East Asian names usually put the family name first.

Do

  • Leave 30 to 50 percent extra room in labels; let buttons grow and wrap.
  • Use CSS logical properties and leading/trailing constraints from day one.
  • Use a single "full name" field unless you truly need parts.
  • Format numbers, dates and currency with locale APIs.
  • Set the language of the page and of passages in other languages (lang) so screen readers switch voices.

Don't

  • Concatenate translated strings ("You have " + n + " notes"); use message formats with plurals.
  • Put text in images.
  • Use flags to represent languages (languages cross borders).
  • Validate names or addresses against one country's pattern.

Cheap phones, slow networks, costly data

Taught by Alex Russell (performance inequality), with Google's Android Go and the "Lite" app generation

Performance is accessibility. A product that needs a flagship phone and fast Wi-Fi excludes most of the world, including most of India, and an app that freezes is unusable with a screen reader no matter how well it is labelled.

Your development machine is probably among the fastest computers your users will ever touch your product on. Much of the world, and a large share of India's hundreds of millions of smartphone users, is on budget Android phones with modest RAM and slower processors, on mobile networks that vary from good 4G and 5G to patchy 3G and 2G in trains, basements and rural areas, often on prepaid data with daily limits. Engineer Alex Russell has written for years about this "performance inequality gap": the gap between the devices developers use and the median device, which has kept JavaScript-heavy sites slow for most people.

What to design for

Low RAM and CPU: keep bundles small, avoid heavy animation libraries, virtualise long lists, don't keep many large images in memory. Android Go, Google's edition for phones with 2 GB of RAM or less, exists because this is a mass market. Slow and flaky networks: show useful content fast (server-rendered HTML, cached shells), make every action resilient to dropping connections (queue and retry, offline-first storage), and show honest progress. Expensive data: compress images, lazy-load, let users choose lower quality, and never auto-download large media on mobile data. Small storage: keep your install size small; people delete big apps first when storage fills.

Age, situation, identity, culture and time

Taught by Kat Holmes, with W3C WAI (older users) and Sara Wachter-Boettcher (Technically Wrong)

Inclusion means noticing the defaults that quietly assume one kind of person: young, alone, at a desk, in one time zone, with a gender that fits two boxes, and a culture where red means danger and Sunday starts the week.

Older adults

Ageing brings several changes at once: reduced near focus and contrast sensitivity, slower reaction and less precise fine motor control, some hearing loss, and often less familiarity with newer interaction conventions (hidden gestures, icon-only navigation). None of these are dramatic alone; together they make many apps quietly hostile. The fixes are the ones in this guide: larger default text with scaling, strong contrast, big targets, visible labels, no hidden gestures, undo, and patience in time limits. Older adults are also often targets of fraud, so clear, consistent security messaging matters.

Situational limits

Gender and identity in forms

First ask whether you need to know at all. Most products don't need gender. If you do (for example, for medical or legal reasons), explain why, make it optional where possible, offer more than two options, and allow people to self-describe. Ask for pronouns or a form of address only if you use them. Titles (Mr, Ms, Dr) are often unnecessary. Let people change their name and email without support tickets; names change for marriage, transition, safety and many other reasons. Never show deadnames in old receipts or shared histories once someone has updated their profile.

Culture, colour and symbols

Colours and symbols carry different meanings in different places. In many Western contexts white is associated with weddings; in parts of India and East Asia it is associated with mourning. Red signals danger or loss in Western finance apps, but in Chinese stock markets red often marks prices going up. A thumbs-up, an owl, a number or a hand gesture can be friendly in one culture and rude or unlucky in another. You cannot learn every meaning; you can avoid relying on colour or symbol alone (Chapter 6 again), use words with icons, and check key symbols with people from your target markets.

Time, dates and calendars

Your users live in different time zones (India uses a single +5:30 zone, which surprises software that assumes whole-hour offsets), start their weeks on different days (Monday in much of Europe and India, Sunday in the US, Saturday in parts of the Middle East), write dates in different orders (03/04 is 3 April in India and 4 March in the US), and some use other calendars alongside the Gregorian one (Islamic, Hebrew, Indian national, Chinese lunar, Vikram Samvat for festivals). Show dates unambiguously ("3 Apr 2026", or relative "Tomorrow, 9:00"), store times in UTC with the user's zone, use locale formatting, and let users choose their first day of the week.

Do

  • Place primary actions within thumb reach on phones.
  • Ask only for personal data you use, and say why.
  • Let people edit names, emails and pronouns themselves.
  • Use locale formatting for dates and times; support half-hour time zones.
  • Pair colours and symbols with words.

Don't

  • Require gender, title or full birth date by default.
  • Assume the week starts on Sunday.
  • Use culturally loaded imagery without checking.
  • Design only for a young, expert, two-handed user.

Wrap-up

Accessibility is not a separate discipline bolted onto design. It is design done with a wider picture of who is on the other side of the screen. You now know the mechanism (the accessibility tree, contrast, reflow, focus), the standard (WCAG 2.2 AA and its key numbers), the methods (native first, APG contracts, a 90-minute audit), and the wider frame (language, device, age, identity). The rest is repetition until it becomes taste.

Ten things to remember

  1. Disability is a mismatch between a person and a design. You make mismatches, so you can remove them. Solve for one, extend to many.
  2. WCAG 2.2 AA is the yardstick most laws point to. Memorise the numbers: 4.5:1, 3:1, 200%, 320 px, 24 px, 20 seconds.
  3. Contrast: body text 4.5:1, large text and UI parts 3:1. Calm comes from restraint, not faint text. Use APCA as a second opinion, not a standard.
  4. Never let colour carry meaning alone. Add a word, a shape, a pattern or a position.
  5. Screen readers read the accessibility tree: role, name, state, order. Visual hierarchy must exist in code as headings and landmarks.
  6. Alt text does the image's job in context. Decorative images get empty alt.
  7. Everything works by keyboard, focus is always visible, and focus goes somewhere sensible after every change.
  8. Big targets, gesture alternatives, matching labels for voice control, and no races against the clock.
  9. Native first, ARIA last. Learn the APG contracts for dialog, menu, tabs and combobox, or use a tested library.
  10. Test in layers: scan, keyboard, screen reader, zoom, grayscale, motion, and real disabled users. And design for languages, cheap phones, slow networks, older users and different lives.

Practice assignment for the next two weeks

The goal is to turn this guide into a habit and a measurably more accessible product.

  1. Days 1 to 2: baseline. Run the full 90-minute audit (Exercise 15.1). Publish or update your accessibility statement.
  2. Days 3 to 4: tokens and focus. Fix contrast at the token level in both themes and automate the check. Add a global :focus-visible style, a skip link, and scroll-padding for sticky headers.
  3. Days 5 to 6: names and structure. Replace every clickable non-interactive element. Add names to every icon button, real headings and landmarks to every screen. Add the accessibility linter to CI.
  4. Days 7 to 8: input alternatives. Give every gesture a tap alternative and custom action; enlarge small targets; fix timers and toasts.
  5. Days 9 to 10: text and layout. Fix every break at 200% text and 320 px; add large-text and narrow previews to your daily setup; pseudo-localise once.
  6. Days 11 to 12: words and memory. Rewrite errors and instructions in plain language; remove one memory demand from sign-in or onboarding (paste, passkeys, magic links).
  7. Days 13 to 14: real people. Book one paid session with a screen-reader user or another disabled tester. Re-run the audit and compare your issue log to day 1.

Reflection

At the end of the two weeks, write a paragraph in your design journal: which change surprised you most, which one made the product better for you, and which group of users you had not been thinking about before. Then put a recurring 90-minute audit on your calendar before every release.

  • Mismatch: How Inclusion Shapes Design

    Microsoft, 2016 · Free; the source of the permanent, temporary, situational framing. Read it in an hour.

  • Inclusive Design Patterns
    Heydon Pickering, Smashing, 2016 (and his Inclusive Components site) · Component-by-component, semantic-first thinking from a designer who codes, like you.

    Sarah Horton and Whitney Quesenbery, Rosenfeld Media, 2014 · Accessibility through personas and UX practice, not checklists.
  • Accessibility for Everyone
    Laura Kalbag, A Book Apart, 2017 · A short, friendly overview for designers and developers.

    Adam Silver, Smashing, 2018 · Accessible, inclusive forms in depth, from someone who worked on GOV.UK.
