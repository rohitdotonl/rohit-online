The Human Mind. Design for the brain people actually have.

Every interface is read by a perceptual system that groups and guesses, steered by an attention that is narrow, held in a memory that holds about four things, and acted on by a hand that obeys physics. This guide teaches the psychology under the "laws of UX": what each one says, where the evidence is strong or shaky, and exactly how to use it in the software you build alone.

Warm-up: what do you already believe?

Before the theory, test your intuitions. Psychology is the one design subject where everyone already has strong opinions, and many popular ones are half right. Answer quickly; you will meet each of these again later, and noticing where you changed your mind is how the learning sticks.

Diagnostic 1 of 3

A colleague says "Miller proved people can only handle seven menu items, so cap the nav at seven." What is the best response?

Visible options are read, not remembered, so the working-memory limit does not set a menu size. Cowan's later estimate of about four chunks is real, but it applies to what you must hold in mind, which is why it matters for multi-step flows, not for a list on screen. Chapter 9 untangles this.
Diagnostic 2 of 3

Your "Save" button is 24×24 px in the top-right of a 1440 px wide window, and people use it constantly. Which change makes it fastest to hit?

Pointing time depends on distance and target size (Fitts's law, chapter 13). Color and motion help people find a target, but once found, acquisition time is physics. The shortcut removes pointing altogether.
Diagnostic 3 of 3

A study shows that offering 24 jams instead of 6 made shoppers ten times less likely to buy. What should you conclude for your app's pricing page?

The famous jam study is real, but later meta-analyzes found the average effect close to zero, with overload appearing only under specific conditions. Chapter 16 covers what those conditions are.

Part one

Perception: what the eye hands the brain

Before anyone reads a word of your interface, their visual system has already grouped it, sorted figure from background, and flagged what stands out. This happens in a fraction of a second, without effort and without consent. Design that works with these automatic processes feels obvious. Design that fights them feels confusing even when every label is correct.

1

Seeing is guessing

Taught by Hermann von Helmholtz, with Richard Gregory and Colin Ware

The eye does not photograph your screen. It samples a tiny sharp spot, fills the rest with prediction, and builds meaning from edges and contrast, so your layout is understood mostly by what the brain expects.

Hold your thumb at arm's length. The nail covers roughly the part of the world you see in sharp detail: the fovea, about 1 to 2 degrees of visual angle. On a laptop at normal distance, that is a patch only a few words wide. Everything else is peripheral vision: blurry, poor at color, but very good at motion and at big shapes. Your eyes jump three or four times a second (saccades) and the brain stitches those snapshots into the illusion of a stable, sharp page.

Helmholtz called perception "unconscious inference" in the 19th century, and the psychologist Richard Gregory built on it: what you see is the brain's best hypothesis about what is out there, given the sparse input and everything it has seen before. That is why people "see" a button that is just a colored rectangle, why a hairline under text reads as a link, and why users miss a warning written in the place they never look.

Edges and contrast come first

Early vision is built to find edges: places where brightness changes. Your retina and visual cortex respond to differences much more than to absolute values. That is why the same gray looks lighter on a dark background (simultaneous contrast), and why a border and a change of background tone are equally good at saying "this is a separate thing". It is also why low-contrast interfaces fail: when the luminance difference between text and background is small, the edge-detecting machinery has less signal, reading slows, and the page feels foggy. Contrast ratios and their exact thresholds belong to Accessible & Inclusive and Visual Design; here the point is the mechanism: luminance contrast is the raw material of every edge, shape and group.

Expectation is part of the input

Because perception is inference, prior experience changes what people see. A blue underlined phrase is a link before it is read. A gray rounded rectangle at the bottom of a phone screen is a home indicator, not a control. An "X" in the top-right corner closes things. When you break these expectations, you are not just being original: you are asking the brain to override a confident prediction, which costs time and causes errors. This is the perceptual root of Jakob's law (chapter 18).

Do

  • Put what must be read where the eye already goes (near the last thing they acted on).
  • Use luminance contrast, not only hue, to make edges and groups.
  • Lean on learned visual conventions for controls: filled buttons, underlined links, chevrons for "more".
  • Check every screen in both light and dark mode, because contrast is relative.

Don't

  • Assume people see the whole screen because it fits in the window.
  • Rely on subtle 3–5% tone differences to separate areas.
  • Style actions as plain text "for minimalism" when they are primary.
  • Put important status changes in the far periphery and expect them to be read.

For the solo creator

You know your interface too well to see it fresh: your brain predicts it perfectly. Two cheap tricks restore a stranger's eyes. Blur a screenshot (a 6–8 px Gaussian blur) and ask "what are the three things I notice, and are they the right three?". Then switch to grayscale and check that every group and button still has an edge. Both take two minutes and catch most perceptual problems before anyone else sees them.

Common mistakes

Designing at arm's length, reviewing at nose length

You inspect your mockups zoomed in, so every detail is foveal and clear.

Review at real size and distance, and with the blur test, so you see what peripheral vision sees.

Hue-only distinctions

Two colors look different to you, but have nearly the same lightness, so their edge is weak (and invisible to many color-blind users).

Check groups and states in grayscale. If they vanish, add a lightness difference, a border or an icon.

Breaking a convention to look fresh

Originality feels like design. But the brain predicts the old pattern and the new one has to fight it.

Be original in the places people do not need to predict (tone, typography, details), conventional in controls.

Check yourself

Users of your note app keep missing the small "Synced" status that changes to "Offline" in the bottom-left corner while they type in the center. What is the best explanation and fix?

Peripheral vision cannot read small text, and a color change of a tiny label is easy to miss. Move the signal toward the locus of attention, or make it a large, persistent cue (for example a change in the editor's own frame). Red in the same corner helps a little, but the location is the real problem.

Gestalt I: how things become groups

Taught by Max Wertheimer, with Kurt Koffka, Wolfgang Köhler and Stephen Palmer

People do not see twenty elements; they see four groups. Nearness, likeness, shared containers and connecting lines decide which groups, and your layout means whatever those groups say.

The Gestalt psychologists in Germany in the 1910s and 1920s showed that perception organizes before it identifies. Wertheimer's 1923 paper laid out "laws of organization": rules by which the visual system decides what belongs together. A century later these are among the most reliable findings in all of design psychology, because you can demonstrate them on anyone in seconds. Stephen Palmer added two more in the 1990s, common region and uniform connectedness, that turn out to be the strongest of all for interfaces.

Why it matters: grouping is how people answer "what is related to what?" without reading. If a label is closer to the wrong field, people attach it to the wrong field. If two buttons look the same, people assume they do the same kind of thing. The structure your code has (which div contains what) is invisible. Only the perceived grouping exists for the user.

Proximity: spacing is meaning

Proximity is the cheapest grouping tool you have, because it uses empty space instead of ink. The rule of thumb for forms: the gap between a label and its own field should be clearly smaller than the gap between that field and the next label. A 4 px label gap against a 20–24 px gap between fields (roughly 1:5) reads unambiguously. Equal gaps (say 12 px everywhere) make every label float between two fields.

Similarity: same look, same job

Things that share color, shape, size or type style are seen as belonging together, and, crucially, as doing the same kind of thing. That is a promise. If every tag is a gray pill and one gray pill is actually a button that deletes, you have broken the promise. Similarity is also why consistency matters so much: when two buttons that do different things look identical, people assume they are equivalent.

Common region and connectedness: the strong glue

Palmer showed in 1992 that elements inside a shared boundary group together even when proximity says otherwise, and with Irvin Rock (1994) that elements joined by a line group more strongly still. In interfaces these are the card, the panel, the inset grouped list, the line between steps. They are powerful, so they are also noisy: a screen where everything sits in a bordered box has many regions and therefore no hierarchy. Calm interfaces such as iA Writer, Things and Apple's Settings mostly use proximity and a few large regions, not a box around every element.

Do

  • Make inner spacing clearly smaller than outer spacing (a ratio of at least 1:2, often 1:4).
  • Give items that do the same thing the same appearance, everywhere.
  • Use a few large regions (sidebar, content, inspector) rather than many small boxes.
  • Use connecting lines only where a sequence or relationship truly exists.

Don't

  • Use the same 16 px gap between everything "for consistency".
  • Style a destructive or different action exactly like its harmless neighbours.
  • Nest cards inside cards inside panels.
  • Let a label sit equally between two fields.

In the wild

  • iOS SettingsApple, inset grouped lists
    Related settings sit in rounded regions separated by generous gaps and small uppercase headers. Common region plus proximity organizes hundreds of settings without a single border line around a row.
  • Things 3Cultured Code, 2017

  • Stripe CheckoutStripe

Common mistakes

The uniform spacing token

A design system with one spacing value feels tidy, so everything gets 16 px.

Use a scale (4, 8, 16, 24, 32, 48) and assign by relationship: tight within a group, loose between groups.

Borders as a substitute for structure

Drawing a box is easier than deciding what belongs together.

Decide the groups first, express them with space, and add a region only when space is not enough.

Accidental similarity

You reused a component (a pill, a chip) for something that behaves differently.

Different behavior gets a visibly different form. Same behavior gets the same form.

Check yourself

In your invoice app, users keep editing the "Tax" field when they mean to edit "Discount". Both fields are in a single row with 12 px gaps, and each label sits 12 px above its field and 12 px below the row before. What will fix it with the least visual noise?

The labels are ambiguous because proximity is equal in both directions. Fixing the spacing ratio makes each label belong to one field and adds no ink. Borders would also work but add noise; color would create false similarity groups.

Gestalt II: shape, depth and motion

Taught by Max Wertheimer, with Edgar Rubin and Kurt Koffka

The visual system completes broken shapes, follows smooth paths, separates a figure from its ground, binds things that move together, and always prefers the simplest reading. Each of these is a lever for making an interface feel obvious.

Chapter 2 was about which things belong together. This chapter is about which shapes, layers and movements the brain constructs. These principles explain why a half-visible card invites a swipe, why a modal over a dimmed page feels like it is "on top", and why a selected batch of files feels like one object when you drag it.

Continuity and alignment

The eye prefers to follow smooth, continuous lines and edges. In layout, that makes alignment a grouping force: elements whose left edges sit on one invisible line are read as a column, and one element that is 3 px off breaks the line and looks like a mistake. It also explains horizontal scrolling rows: when cards continue off the edge of the screen, the eye follows the row and infers there is more.

Closure: what the brain fills in

Given a partial outline, people perceive the complete shape. This is why icons can be drawn with very few strokes, and why the "peek" works: in a horizontal carousel, a card cut off at the right edge is completed by the mind, which also tells it "there is more here, scroll". When every card fits exactly, nothing hints at more, and people may never swipe.

Figure-ground: what is on top

Edgar Rubin's 1915 vase/faces figure shows that the brain must decide which region is the object and which is the background, and it cannot see both at once. Smaller, enclosed, higher-contrast and lower regions tend to become figure. Interfaces use this constantly: a modal on a dimmed page, a popover with a shadow, a selected row with a filled background. If the ground is not pushed back (the page behind a dialog at full brightness), people are unsure which layer they are in, and click the wrong one.

Common fate: things that move together

Elements that move in the same direction at the same time are grouped, overriding even color and proximity. This is why animation can explain structure: when you collapse a section and its children slide up together, the user learns that they belonged to it. When you drag five selected files and they gather into a stack under the cursor, they become one object. Motion design builds on this in Motion & Feel.

Prägnanz: the simplest reading wins

"Prägnanz" (roughly "conciseness") is the Gestalt umbrella law: the brain resolves ambiguity in favor of the simplest, most regular interpretation. Designers use it two ways. First, give people simple forms so they do not have to resolve anything. Second, when your layout has an accidental simple reading (two unrelated panels that happen to align into a single block), people will see that reading, not yours.

Myth

"Gestalt principles are just design theory." They are among the best-replicated phenomena in perception research, studied with reaction times and neural recordings for a century. What is less exact is their relative strength in any given layout: that depends on how big the spacing, color or size differences are, so you still have to look.

Do

  • Align to a small number of strong edges so continuity does the grouping.
  • Let one card peek past the edge in any horizontal scroller.
  • Dim or blur the background behind a modal so figure and ground are unambiguous.
  • Animate grouped items together when they change state together.

Don't

  • Leave elements 2–4 px off the alignment line.
  • Fit carousel items exactly to the screen width.
  • Open dialogs over a full-brightness page that still looks interactive.
  • Animate unrelated elements on the same timing; people will assume they are related.

In the wild

  • App Store "Today" and shelf rowsApple
    Horizontal shelves size items so the next one is visibly cut at the edge. Closure tells you there is more without arrows or "see all".
  • macOS FinderApple
    Dragging several selected files shows them gathered under the pointer with a count badge. Common fate turns many items into one draggable object.
  • iOS sheetsApple, iOS 13 and later
    A sheet slides up while the page behind scales back slightly and darkens. Figure-ground made physical: you know which layer you are in.

Common mistakes

Near-miss alignment

Elements are positioned by hand or by different components with different paddings.

Use one layout grid and shared padding tokens; check with a vertical guide line across the screen.

Weak modal ground

A transparent overlay at 10% looks subtle in a mockup.

Make the backdrop clearly darker (or blurred) and block clicks on it; the ground must look inactive.

Decorative motion that groups by accident

A staggered entrance animation moves the sidebar and the content at the same time.

Only animate together what belongs together, and keep entrance motion short or absent.

Check yourself

Your reading app shows "Continue reading" as a horizontal row of book covers. Analytics show almost nobody scrolls the row. Covers are 100 px wide and three fit exactly on a 360 px screen with gutters. What is the most likely fix?

When the row fits exactly, closure completes it and the brain concludes it ends. A peeking item is the cheapest, quietest signal. A label works but adds noise; auto-scroll steals attention and is hostile to readers.

Pop-out: preattentive attributes and visual search

Taught by Anne Treisman, with Christopher Healey, Colin Ware and Jeremy Wolfe

A few visual features (color, size, orientation, motion, and some others) are detected in parallel across the whole screen in a fraction of a second. Anything that differs by one of them pops out. Anything that differs only by a combination must be searched for, one item at a time.

In 1980 Anne Treisman and Garry Gelade published feature integration theory. Their experiments showed two kinds of search. When the target differs from distractors by a single feature (a red item among blue ones), search time stays roughly flat no matter how many distractors there are: the target "pops out". When it differs only by a conjunction (a red circle among red squares and blue circles), search time grows with the number of items, as if attention must visit each one. The features that pop out are called preattentive: they are processed before focused attention, in roughly 200–250 ms.

For design, this is a budget. Pop-out is the most powerful tool you have for guiding the eye, and it only works when one thing differs. Make five things pop and none of them do. Try it yourself below.

Key idea

Salience is relative. A color is loud only on a quiet background. Spend your pop-out budget on one or two things per screen.

Do

  • Keep the baseline quiet so one difference can pop.
  • Make a pop-out cue redundant: color plus lightness, or color plus a shape or icon.
  • Sort or group lists so people can search a subset instead of everything.
  • Reserve motion for things that truly need to interrupt.

Don't

  • Color-code six categories and expect any of them to be findable at a glance.
  • Signal the important item by a combination of two subtle features.
  • Use animated badges for routine counts.
  • Make "new", "beta", "pro" and "sale" badges compete on one screen.

Common mistakes

Rainbow categories

Color feels like organization, so every project, tag and status gets its own hue.

Use color for one dimension at most, and for at most 5–7 categories; use labels and position for the rest.

Everything bold

Each feature request asks for emphasis, and emphasis accumulates.

Per screen, decide the one thing that must pop. Demote everything else, and review emphasis whenever you add something.

Color-only status

Red and green look clearly different to you.

About 1 in 12 men has some color vision deficiency. Pair color with lightness, an icon or text (see Accessible & Inclusive).

Check yourself

Your habit tracker marks overdue habits with a red label, completed ones with a green label, scheduled ones with a blue label and paused ones with a gray label. Users say they "can't find what is overdue". What do you change?

With four colored states, overdue is just one color among several, so it does not pop. Quieting the rest restores pop-out; sorting reduces the search set. Animation would work but costs attention every time the screen is open.

What people miss: change and inattentional blindness

Taught by Daniel Simons, with Christopher Chabris, Ronald Rensink, Arien Mack and Irvin Rock

People do not notice changes they are not attending to, even large ones, and they do not see unexpected things while focused on a task. An update that happens silently on screen is, for most users, an update that never happened.

In 1997 Ronald Rensink, Kevin O'Regan and James Clark showed people two versions of a photo alternating with a brief blank screen in between. Large changes (a whole engine missing from an airplane) took many seconds to spot. Without the blank, the change popped out instantly, because the motion signal of the change drew attention. The blank (like a page reload, a blink or a scroll) erases that signal. This is change blindness.

In 1999 Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris asked people to count basketball passes in a video. About half failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene. This is inattentional blindness: when attention is committed to a task, unexpected things are not seen at all, even in the fovea. Mack and Rock's 1998 book named the effect after years of lab work.

Both effects are robust and replicate well. They demolish the designer's assumption that "it is on the screen, so they saw it".

Lab: spot the change
Press Play. Two versions alternate with a blank between. Find what changes.
With the blank, most people need several cycles. Switch to "No blank" and the change jumps out at once, because the change itself becomes a motion signal. If your system asks for reduced motion, Play is replaced by manual stepping; the flash rate stays under 3 per second either way.

Where change blindness bites in software

  • Full reloads after an action. The page blanks, re-renders, and the one line that changed ("Shipping: ₹ 120") is invisible.
  • Silent recalculation. A total updates when a coupon fails, in a corner the user is not looking at.
  • Validation errors far from the field, shown at the top of a long form after submit, while attention is at the bottom on the button.
  • Toasts and snackbars that appear in a corner while the user is focused on the content, and vanish in 4 seconds.
  • Auto-saved settings with no confirmation near the control.

Do

  • Update content in place rather than reloading the page.
  • Put feedback next to the control or the value that changed.
  • Use a brief highlight or transition to mark a change, then let it settle.
  • State important changes in words ("Shipping added: ₹ 120").

Don't

  • Rely on users noticing a recalculated number.
  • Show errors only in a summary at the top of the page.
  • Use 3-second toasts for things people must read.
  • Assume a design review where you knew what changed tells you anything about noticing.

In the wild

  • Google DocsGoogle
    Collaborators' cursors and selections appear in color with name labels right where they are editing. Changes made by others are made visible at the place of change.
  • Single-page web appsa pattern
    Inline validation that appears next to a field as you leave it replaced many full-page "submit and reload" forms. No reload, no blank, no change blindness.
  • Figma multiplayerFigma

For the solo creator

Because you build the feature, you always know what changed, so you can never test this on yourself. Use a "fresh eyes" trick: record your screen while doing a task, wait a day, then watch it at normal speed and note every change you fail to notice. Or ask one friend to do the task and say aloud what they think just happened after each click.

Common mistakes

"It's on the screen"

Designers review with full knowledge of what changes.

Assume nothing is seen unless it is at the locus of attention or announced. Test with someone who does not know.

Toast for consequential news

Toasts are easy to add and feel non-intrusive.

Use toasts only for confirmations that can be missed safely ("Copied"). Anything consequential belongs inline and persistent.

Check yourself

In your budgeting app, applying a filter reloads the whole table, and a summary line at the bottom silently switches from "All accounts" to "2 accounts hidden". Users get confused totals. Best fix?

The reload blanks the screen (change blindness) and the summary is far from attention. An in-place update keeps the motion cue, and a persistent chip near the table header puts the state where people look. Bold or a toast help briefly but the state must stay visible.

Part one recap: connect the ideas

A dashboard has twelve colored KPI tiles in equal boxes. When a value crosses a threshold, its tile turns red after the page auto-refreshes. Which combination of ideas from this part explains why people miss it?

Three effects stack: salience spent on everything, regions that do not differentiate, and a blank-screen refresh that erases the motion of the change. Quiet tiles, grouped by meaning, with in-place updates and a single alarm color, fix all three. Data display is covered in depth in Information & Data.

Attention: the narrow beam

Perception gathers everything; attention picks almost nothing. It is a single, narrow, easily captured beam, and every interface competes for it with the user's own goals, the operating system, other apps and the room they are sitting in. This part covers how attention is limited, how people actually scan screens, and what interruptions and flow do to the work people come to your product to do.

One beam: limited attention and the locus of attention

Taught by Jef Raskin, with Daniel Kahneman, Jan Panero Benway and Herbert Simon

People can consciously attend to one thing at a time. Your interface either supports that one thing or competes with it, and anything that looks like an ad, a promotion or a nag is learned to be invisible.

Jef Raskin, who started the Macintosh project at Apple, put it plainly in The Humane Interface (2000): we have one "locus of attention", the single object or idea we are consciously engaged with. You can walk and talk, but you cannot attend to two conversations. Skills that are fully automatic (typing for a fast typist) do not need the locus, which is why they can run alongside thought. Everything else queues.

Herbert Simon made the economic version of the point in 1971: "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention". Each element you add to a screen draws a little from the same fixed budget. Kahneman's Attention and Effort (1973) treated attention as a limited capacity that is spent by effortful tasks; when one task is demanding (composing a hard email), people miss things that would be obvious when relaxed.

Banner blindness: attention learns to ignore

In 1998, Jan Panero Benway and David Lane found that people searching a site failed to find information placed in a large, colorful banner at the top, even though it was exactly what they were looking for. Later eye-tracking by Nielsen Norman Group repeatedly showed users skipping anything that looks like an ad: boxes in the right rail, animated panels, and content styled with promotional colors or stock photos. The visual system learns which shapes are noise for the task and stops looking at them.

Banner blindness spreads beyond ads. Any element that repeatedly offers nothing useful becomes invisible: the "What's new" panel, the persistent upgrade bar, the tips sidebar. Worse, when you later put something important in that place or style, it inherits the blindness.

Designing for one locus

  • Put feedback where attention already is. Raskin's strongest practical advice: messages belong at the locus, not in a status bar people never read.
  • Avoid modes the user must remember. Raskin argued that modes cause errors because the mode indicator is never at the locus; you type expecting one behavior and get another (the Caps Lock problem).
  • Remove instead of styling. Each element costs filtering effort even when ignored. Removing a permanent banner frees more attention than making it subtler.

Do

  • Show status, errors and confirmations at the element the user just acted on.
  • Style important notices as content, in the flow, near the related action.
  • Remove persistent promotional elements; use one-time, contextual moments instead.
  • Make modes visible at the locus (a cursor that changes, a colored frame).

Don't

  • Put critical information in an ad-like box, right rail or carousel.
  • Use the same banner slot for promotions and for important warnings.
  • Rely on a status bar for anything people must notice.
  • Add "just one more" persistent element without removing another.

You want it noticed, so you make it loud.

Loud and ad-shaped is skipped. Make it relevant, close and plainly written instead.

The banner is already invisible through banner blindness. Escalating loudness becomes harassment (see Honest & Calm). Relevance and placement at the locus work better than volume.

How people scan: F, layer-cake, spotted and Z

Taught by Jakob Nielsen, with Kara Pernice and Steve Krug

People rarely read screens; they scan for words that match their goal. Where they scan depends on how the page is structured, and when the page gives them nothing to grab, they fall back to the worst pattern: the F.

Nielsen's 2006 eye-tracking study of web pages produced the famous F-shaped pattern: two horizontal sweeps across the top, then a vertical scan down the left edge. Nielsen Norman Group's 2017 follow-up, by Kara Pernice, made the important correction that is often lost: the F-pattern is a symptom of poor structure, not a goal. People fall into it when text has no headings, bold keywords or lists to anchor on. The same research described other patterns: layer-cake (eyes jump from heading to heading, reading body text only under relevant ones), spotted (skipping around for a specific word, number or link), and commitment (reading everything, when motivation is high).

What about the Z-pattern?

The Z-pattern (eyes go top-left to top-right, diagonally down, then across the bottom) and its cousin the Gutenberg diagram are layout heuristics for sparse pages with few elements, like a landing page hero or a poster. They are not strong eye-tracking findings in the way the F and layer-cake are. Use them as a composition idea (logo top-left, action bottom-right, a strong diagonal), not as a law. On dense, text-heavy screens, scanning follows content structure, and the Z disappears.

Watch out

All of these patterns are for left-to-right languages. In Arabic, Hebrew or Urdu interfaces the patterns mirror. And on phones, a single column turns most scanning into a vertical pass: headings and the first words of each line carry nearly all the weight.

Design for the layer-cake

  1. Write headings that carry meaning. "Export your notes as Markdown" beats "Export". A scanner reads only the heading; it must answer "is this for me?".
  2. Front-load. The first two words of every heading, list item and link do most of the work, because scanning eyes fixate on the left edge.
  3. Chunk body text short. Two to four sentences per paragraph, one idea each.
  4. Make the scannable things look scannable: bold key terms sparingly, numbers as digits ("3 days", not "three days"), lists for parallel items.
  5. Put the action next to the explanation, not in a different region, so the layer-cake reader finds it in the same chunk.

Writing for scanning is covered deeply in Words and Reading & Writing. Here the lesson is perceptual: the eye goes to anchors, and you decide where the anchors are.

Do

  • Give every screen of text descriptive headings people can scan alone.
  • Start labels, list items and links with the distinguishing word.
  • Use digits for numbers in UI.
  • Test by reading only headings and bold text: does the page still make sense?

Don't

  • Write clever headings that hide the topic ("A new chapter").
  • Start every list item with the same words ("Click here to…").
  • Assume the Z-pattern will guide eyes on a dense screen.
  • Bold half the paragraph.

In the wild

  • GOV.UKGovernment Digital Service, 2012–
    Short pages, plain descriptive headings, front-loaded link text, numbers as digits. A whole service built for the layer-cake reader.
  • Stripe DocsStripe
    Each section starts with a heading that names the task, followed by a short paragraph and a code block. Developers scan for their task, read one chunk, copy the code.

    Results show the setting name first and its location path in smaller gray text. Front-loading the distinguishing word supports spotted scanning.

Interruptions, notifications and flow

Taught by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, with Gloria Mark, Mark Weiser and Amber Case

Deep work happens in a fragile state called flow. Every interruption breaks it, and getting back costs far more than the interruption itself. A product that respects attention protects flow and batches everything else.

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi studied people at their best, painters, chess players, surgeons, rock climbers, and described flow in his 1990 book Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience: total absorption in an activity, loss of self-consciousness, distorted sense of time. Flow appears under specific conditions: a clear goal, immediate feedback, and a balance between challenge and skill. Too easy and people get bored; too hard and they get anxious.

The cost of an interruption

Gloria Mark at UC Irvine has studied interruptions in real offices since the early 2000s. Her field work found that people switch activities every few minutes and that interrupted work is often not resumed for a long time; the popular figure of "about 23 minutes to get back on task" comes from her research and interviews. In a 2008 lab study, Mark, Gudith and Klocke found something subtler: interrupted people completed tasks in about the same time, but by working faster, with significantly more stress, frustration and effort. Interruptions do not just cost time; they cost wellbeing.

Notifications are interruptions you design. Each one asks the user to move their locus of attention from their task to yours. The design question is always: is this worth breaking what they are doing right now?

Calm notification design

Mark Weiser and John Seely Brown at Xerox PARC proposed "calm technology" in 1995: technology that informs without demanding focus, moving easily between the periphery and the center of attention. Amber Case turned it into principles in her 2015 book Calm Technology. In practice for notifications:

Memory: the tiny desk and the huge library

Working memory is a small desk where you hold what you are thinking about right now: a few items, gone in seconds unless rehearsed. Long-term memory is a vast library of facts, habits and models, slow to write and sometimes hard to find. Good interfaces keep the desk clear and lean on what is already in the library.

Working memory: seven, four, and chunks

Taught by George A. Miller, with Nelson Cowan, Alan Baddeley and John Sweller

People can hold only about four meaningful chunks in mind at once, for a few seconds. Never make them carry information from one screen to another, and when they must handle long strings, chunk them.

In 1956 George Miller published "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two: Some Limits on Our Capacity for Processing Information" in Psychological Review. The paper is witty and careful. It reviewed two kinds of limits: how many categories people can reliably distinguish on one dimension (absolute judgment, around seven), and how many items people can repeat back in order (immediate memory span, also around seven). Miller pointed out that the coincidence might be "only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence". His most lasting idea was chunking: memory span is limited in chunks, not in raw bits, so recoding information into bigger meaningful units extends what we can hold.

In 2001 Nelson Cowan reviewed decades of later research in "The magical number 4 in short-term memory" (Behavioral and Brain Sciences). When you stop people from rehearsing and grouping, the true capacity of the focus of attention is about 3 to 5 chunks, typically 4. Seven was the span with rehearsal and chunking helping.

Myth

"Menus and navigation must have seven items or fewer because of Miller." No. Visible items are not held in working memory; they are on screen to be re-read. Miller's paper says nothing about menus. The legitimate design uses of working-memory limits are: what people must remember between steps, how many things they must compare in their head, and how long strings they must copy. A navigation bar with 9 clear items can be fine; a flow that asks you to remember a 6-digit code from another app is not.

Chunking: making four go further

A chunk is a unit that is meaningful to the person. "9 8 7 6 5 4 3 2 1 0" is ten digits but one chunk ("counting down"). An unfamiliar 10-digit string has ten. Formatting helps people build chunks: grouping digits by 3 or 4, using separators, keeping known structure (country code, area code) visible. This is why phone numbers, credit cards and IBANs are printed in groups.

Cognitive load: three kinds

John Sweller's cognitive load theory (from 1988, developed through the 1990s) divides the mental effort of a task into three kinds. Intrinsic load comes from the task itself: splitting a bill among five people with different shares is inherently harder than splitting it equally. Extraneous load comes from how the task is presented: hunting for a field, decoding jargon, remembering a value from a previous screen. Germane load is effort that builds lasting understanding (learning how the tool's model works). Later versions of the theory folded germane load into intrinsic, but the design rule survives: you cannot remove intrinsic load, you must ruthlessly remove extraneous load, so people's limited working memory goes to their actual problem.

Do

  • Carry information forward: show the values from step 1 on step 3.
  • Format long strings in groups (3–4 characters) and allow pasting with or without spaces.
  • Autofill codes, addresses and payment details wherever the platform allows.
  • Keep comparisons side by side instead of across screens or tabs.

Don't

  • Ask people to remember a code, number or name from another screen or app.
  • Cite "7±2" to justify cutting a visible menu.
  • Split one decision across several modal steps when its inputs belong together.
  • Reject input because the user typed spaces or dashes in a chunked number.

Recognition over recall, and mental models

Taught by Don Norman, with Jakob Nielsen, Kenneth Craik and Endel Tulving

Recognizing something you see is far easier than recalling it from nothing. And people operate your product through a mental model, a private simplified theory of how it works, which your interface either teaches correctly or teaches wrong.

Ask someone to name all the menu items in their email app and they will struggle. Show them the menu and they will instantly know which one they want. Recognition needs only a match between what you see and what you know; recall must reconstruct the item from scratch with only a vague cue. This is why graphical interfaces beat command lines for occasional use, and why Nielsen made "recognition rather than recall" one of his ten usability heuristics (1994).

Recognition patterns you can use

  • Visible options instead of hidden commands: menus, toolbars, a command palette that lists actions as you type.
  • Recent and frequent items: recent files, recent searches, recently used emoji.
  • Autocomplete and previews showing actual content (file thumbnails, link previews).
  • Keyboard shortcuts shown next to menu items, so recognition slowly trains recall for those who want speed.
  • Breadcrumbs and titles that tell people where they are, so they do not have to remember how they got there.

Long-term memory: kinds that matter for design

Endel Tulving distinguished episodic memory (events you lived) from semantic memory (facts you know); researchers also describe procedural memory (skills and habits). For interfaces: semantic memory holds conventions ("trash can deletes"), procedural memory holds motor habits ("Cmd-S saves"), and episodic memory holds the experience ("last time this app lost my draft"). You build on the first, must not break the second (chapter 11), and are judged by the third (chapter 17).

Mental models: the theory in the user's head

The Scottish psychologist Kenneth Craik suggested in 1943 that the mind builds "small-scale models" of reality to predict what will happen. Don Norman applied this to design in The Design of Everyday Things with three models: the designer's model (how you think it works), the system image (what the product shows: UI, words, behavior, docs), and the user's model (what they infer). You never talk to the user directly. Everything they learn comes through the system image, so if the system image is unclear or inconsistent, the user builds a wrong model, and then makes "errors" that are perfectly logical given that model.

Habit, position and the unfinished

Taught by Jef Raskin, with Hermann Ebbinghaus, Bennet Murdock and Bluma Zeigarnik

Repeated actions become automatic habits that run without attention, so moving a control breaks people in a way no amount of explanation fixes. Memory also favors the first and last items in a sequence. And unfinished tasks pull at us, though less magically than the internet claims.

Habituation and muscle memory

Raskin argued that every interface eventually becomes habitual, and that this is a good thing: habits free the locus of attention for the real work. A writer who saves with Cmd-S does not think about saving. But habits run on location and sequence, not meaning. If you move "Save" or reorder a tab bar, the habit fires anyway and lands on the wrong thing. People do not just get slower; they make errors, feel stupid, and blame the product.

Habits also defeat confirmation dialogs. When "Are you sure?" appears every time, the click on "Yes" joins the habit, and the dialog stops protecting anything. Raskin's alternative: make actions undoable instead of confirmable.

Serial position: first and last stick

Ebbinghaus noticed it in the 1880s and Bennet Murdock measured it cleanly in 1962: when people try to recall a list, they remember the first few items (primacy, which had more rehearsal and reached long-term memory) and the last few (recency, still in working memory) much better than the middle. The effect is robust. Try it.

In a short tab bar, the first and last positions are both the most memorable and the easiest to reach. In a long settings list, the middle is where rarely-needed items belong. In a pitch or a landing page, the opening and the final summary carry the most weight.

The Zeigarnik effect, honestly

In 1927 Bluma Zeigarnik, working in Kurt Lewin's lab in Berlin, reported that people remembered interrupted tasks better than completed ones. Her colleague Maria Ovsiankina (1928) found a related effect: people tend to spontaneously resume interrupted tasks. Product writing often cites "Zeigarnik" to justify progress bars, incomplete profile meters and cliffhangers.

The evidence is weaker than that story suggests. Replications over the decades were mixed, and a 2025 meta-analysis by Ghibellini and Meier found no general memory advantage for unfinished tasks, while the urge to resume them (the Ovsiankina effect) held up better. The safe design reading: people do feel a pull to complete open, visible tasks, especially ones they chose and that are nearly done (see goal-gradient in chapter 17). Do not rely on a memory effect to make people return.

Do

  • Keep positions of frequent controls stable across versions.
  • When you must move something, leave a signpost at the old location for a while.
  • Offer undo instead of habitual confirmation dialogs.
  • Place the most important items first and last in lists and sequences.
  • Show unfinished work the user chose to start ("Continue your draft").

Don't

  • Put a new feature in the slot of an old, frequently used one.
  • Reorder menus by usage automatically (adaptive menus break habits).
  • Rely on "Are you sure?" for destructive actions people do often.
  • Manufacture fake unfinished tasks ("Your profile is 60% complete!") to nag.

Hick's law: more choices, slower decisions (sometimes)

Taught by William Edmund Hick, with Ray Hyman and Thomas Landauer

The time to choose among equally likely options grows with the logarithm of how many there are. It is a real law, but only for a narrow kind of choice; for scanning lists and making real-life decisions, other things matter more.

In 1952 W. E. Hick and in 1953 Ray Hyman showed that when people must respond to one of several lights by pressing the matching key, reaction time rises by a constant amount each time the number of equally likely alternatives doubles. The usual form is T = a + b · log₂(n + 1). Going from 2 to 4 options adds about as much time as going from 4 to 8, or 8 to 16. The log means the cost grows slowly: doubling the options does not double the time.

When Hick's law applies, and when it does not

Hick's experiments measured a specific situation: options are equally likely, the person knows them all, and the mapping from stimulus to response is learned. Interfaces often differ:

  • Unfamiliar lists are searched, not decided. When you do not know where an item is, you scan, and scan time grows roughly linearly with the items you must look at. Sorting, grouping and search cut this.
  • Familiar, ordered lists become log-like again. Landauer and Nachbar (1985) found menu selection in well-ordered menus (alphabetical or numeric) fit a log relationship: you can jump to roughly the right place.
  • Practice flattens it. Experts with a fixed layout pick by location from memory.
  • Unequal likelihoods change everything. If one option is chosen 80% of the time, making it the obvious default removes the decision for most people.

So the practical reading is not "fewer options are always better". It is: reduce the number of options that must be considered at each moment, by defaults, grouping, progressive disclosure and good ordering, while keeping everything findable.

Lab: find it in the menu
Press Start, then click the requested item.
Do a few trials at 4, 16 and 32 items in each order. With alphabetical order, time grows slowly (you jump to the letter). With random order, it grows much faster, because you are scanning one by one. That gap is the honest lesson: order and grouping often matter more than the count.

Do

  • Order long lists predictably (alphabetical, chronological, by frequency) so people can jump.
  • Promote the one or two most likely choices; tuck the rest behind "More".
  • Group options under meaningful headings.
  • Offer search when a list exceeds about 15–20 items.

Don't

  • Cut useful options just to hit a small number.
  • Present a long, unsorted list where every item has equal weight.
  • Quote Hick's law to justify hiding things people need often.
  • Ask people to decide at moments when a default would do.

Fitts's law: bigger and closer is faster

Taught by Paul Fitts, with I. Scott MacKenzie, Bruce Tognazzini and Steven Hoober

The time to hit a target depends on how far away it is and how big it is. Make frequent targets large and close, use screen edges and corners, and put touch targets where thumbs can reach.

Paul Fitts, a US Air Force psychologist, published the law in 1954 from experiments where people tapped back and forth between two metal plates. Movement time rises with an "index of difficulty" based on the ratio of distance to width. The formulation most used in HCI today is MacKenzie's (1992): MT = a + b · log₂(D/W + 1), where D is distance to the target's center and W is the target's width along the direction of movement. It is one of the most robust laws in human-computer interaction; it holds for mice, trackpads, fingers, styluses and even eye-gaze pointing, with different constants.

Lab: Fitts click test
Click Start, then click the yellow target as fast as you can. Each trial randomizes size and distance.
Do 15 to 20 trials. The log groups your times by difficulty (easy, medium, hard). Hard targets (small and far) should take clearly longer, and you will miss them more. That is why a 16 px icon button in a far corner of a big window feels sluggish even though it is "right there".

Edges and corners: infinitely large targets

On a desktop, the pointer stops at the screen edge. A target that touches the edge is effectively infinitely deep in the direction of movement: you can throw the mouse at it without aiming. Corners are best of all, capped in two directions. Bruce Tognazzini, Apple's early human interface evangelist, popularized this, calling the four corners the easiest targets on screen after the pixel under the cursor. It is why the Mac menu bar sits at the very top edge of the screen, and why hot corners work. A window-attached menu bar a few pixels below the edge loses the effect.

Thumbs: Fitts on a phone

On touch screens, distance is measured from where the thumb rests, and target width is limited by the finger's contact area. Steven Hoober's 2013 observation study of 1,333 people using phones in public found about 49% using one hand, 36% cradling the phone, and 15% two-handed. Josh Clark's "thumb zone" diagrams (in Designing for Touch, 2015) mapped what one-handed users can reach comfortably. Today's phones are tall, so the top of the screen is the hardest place to reach, which is why many apps have moved key actions and even search fields toward the bottom.

Time: 0.1, 1 and 10 seconds, and the 400 ms threshold

Taught by Robert B. Miller, with Jakob Nielsen, Walter Doherty and Ahrvind Thadani

Below 0.1 s a response feels instant. Up to 1 s the user's flow of thought survives. Around 10 s, attention leaves. Your job is to keep real latency low and, where you cannot, to make waiting feel short and understood.

In 1968, Robert B. Miller at IBM published "Response time in man-computer conversational transactions", estimating how long people tolerate different kinds of waits. Jakob Nielsen distilled it in Usability Engineering (1993) into three limits that have survived every hardware generation because they are about people, not machines:

  • 0.1 second: the limit for feeling that the system reacts instantly, as if you are manipulating the object directly. Typing, dragging, toggles, hover states must be here.
  • 1 second: the limit for the user's flow of thought to stay uninterrupted. They notice the delay but do not lose the thread. No special feedback strictly needed, though a light busy indicator after ~300–500 ms helps.
  • 10 seconds: the limit for keeping attention on the dialogue. Beyond it, people start doing other things, so show percent-done progress, let them work elsewhere, and tell them when it finishes.

The Doherty threshold

In 1982 Walter Doherty and Ahrvind Thadani at IBM published "The Economic Value of Rapid Response Time", reporting that when computer response time dropped below about 400 ms, user productivity rose sharply, as people and computer settled into a fast conversational rhythm. Be honest about the source: it is an industry paper from the mainframe era, measuring programmers' transactions, not a modern controlled experiment. The precise 400 ms number should not be treated as a law. The direction it points has been confirmed many times since: faster responses keep people engaged and productive, and every extra few hundred milliseconds on a frequent action adds up.

Lab: feel the delay
Press Save note.
Try 300 ms, 1,500 ms and 5,000 ms with each feedback mode. With no feedback, even 1.5 s feels broken and you will want to press again. An instant pressed state makes short waits acceptable; a spinner that appears only after 400 ms avoids flicker on fast saves; optimistic UI makes the wait vanish (but needs a plan for failure).

Perceived speed

When you cannot make it faster, you can still make it feel faster and more trustworthy:

  1. Acknowledge within 0.1 s. The button visibly presses, the row highlights. This is the most important rule; it tells people their action registered.
  2. Delay the spinner. Show busy indicators only if the wait passes about 300–500 ms, so fast actions do not flash.
  3. Optimistic updates. Show the result immediately and sync in the background, with a clear and recoverable failure state.
  4. Skeletons and progressive loading for content screens, so structure appears before data.
  5. Determinate progress for long tasks: a bar that moves steadily feels shorter than a spinner. Tell people what is happening ("Importing 1,240 notes").
  6. Let people leave. Above 10 s, run it in the background and notify on completion.

The craft of loading motion, skeletons and timing curves is in Motion & Feel.

Parkinson's law and time limits

C. Northcote Parkinson's 1955 essay in The Economist opened: "Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." It was satire about bureaucracy, not science. As a design heuristic it suggests that people take as long as a task seems to allow: an open-ended form invites over-thinking, while a clear, small scope ("Add a title, you can change it later") gets people moving. Use it gently: real time limits (countdowns, expiring offers) add stress and are a common dark pattern.

Do

  • Keep direct manipulation (typing, dragging, toggling) under 100 ms.
  • Acknowledge every action instantly, even if the work takes longer.
  • Show determinate progress and let people continue working for anything over ~10 s.
  • Set a performance budget for your three most frequent actions.

Don't

  • Show a spinner that flashes for 80 ms on fast connections.
  • Block the whole screen with a modal spinner for a background save.
  • Use an endless spinner for a task that takes minutes.
  • Add fake countdowns to create urgency.

Fast and slow: satisficing and the active user

Taught by Daniel Kahneman, with Amos Tversky, Herbert Simon, Steve Krug, John Carroll and Mary Beth Rosson

People mostly run on a fast, automatic mode of thinking and switch to slow, deliberate thought only when they must. They pick the first option that seems good enough, and they would rather start doing than read instructions. Design for that person, not for an ideal careful one.

System 1 and System 2

In Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011), Daniel Kahneman popularized a distinction from psychologists Keith Stanovich and Richard West: System 1 is fast, automatic, effortless and associative (reading a familiar word, recognizing a face, knowing that 2 + 2 = 4). System 2 is slow, effortful, deliberate and limited (computing 17 × 24, comparing two insurance plans). System 1 is always on and generates impressions; System 2 is lazy and only steps in when something feels wrong or hard.

Kahneman described these as useful fictions, characters in a story about the mind, not brain regions. The book also included some priming studies that later failed to replicate, which Kahneman publicly acknowledged. The core two-mode idea, and the heuristics and biases research with Amos Tversky, are on much firmer ground.

Satisficing: good enough wins

Herbert Simon, who won the 1978 Nobel Prize in economics, coined "satisficing" (satisfy + suffice) in 1956: real decision-makers with limited time and information do not optimize; they pick the first option that meets their threshold. Steve Krug turned this into one of the best-known observations in web usability in Don't Make Me Think (2000): users do not read pages and choose the best link; they scan, pick the first reasonable option, and if it is wrong, press Back. Guessing is cheap and usually works, so it is rational.

Design consequence: the first plausible thing people see will be clicked. Make it the right thing, and make recovery (Back, Undo) cheap when it is not.

The paradox of the active user

John Carroll and Mary Beth Rosson (1987) studied people learning office software and found that they skipped manuals and tutorials to start doing real work immediately, even when reading first would have saved time. They called it a paradox: people would be more productive if they invested in learning, but their motivation to get something done right now wins. They also tend to stick with the methods they first learned, even inefficient ones ("production bias" and "assimilation bias").

Do

  • Make the first plausible option the right one for most people.
  • Let people do real work in the first minute; teach in context.
  • Make wrong guesses cheap with Back and Undo everywhere.
  • Add deliberate friction only to rare, dangerous actions, to wake System 2.

Don't

  • Rely on people reading instructions before acting.
  • Put a tutorial carousel between install and the first useful action.
  • Present a careful comparison where a sensible default would do.
  • Use friction everywhere; it trains people to click through.

In the wild

  • GitHub "delete repository"GitHub
    Asks you to type the repository's name to confirm. Deliberate friction that forces System 2 for one rare, destructive action.
  • Apple Notes, iA WriterApple; Information Architects
    Open straight into a blank document ready for typing. The active user gets to work instantly; there is nothing to learn first.
  • Figma's interactive file onboardingFigma
    Has used playground files where you learn tools by doing small tasks rather than reading. Learning by doing matches the active user.

Choices people make: defaults, anchors, frames and losses

Taught by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, with Eric Johnson, Daniel Goldstein, Sheena Iyengar and Richard Thaler

How you present a choice changes what people choose: the default, the first number they see, the wording, and whether outcomes look like gains or losses. You cannot design a neutral choice, so design an honest one.

Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein called this "choice architecture" in Nudge (2008): whoever designs the form shapes the decision. As the designer of your product, you are a choice architect whether you like it or not. This chapter covers the effects with the best evidence, plus one famous one with shaky evidence.

Defaults and status quo bias

William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser (1988) showed that people disproportionately stick with the current state of affairs. Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein's 2003 paper "Do Defaults Save Lives?" compared organ-donor consent across European countries: in countries where people were donors unless they opted out, effective consent rates were close to 100%; in countries requiring people to opt in, they were far lower (Germany around 12%, against Austria's near-universal rate). Same people, same values, different default.

Defaults win for three reasons: they take no effort (System 1), they look like a recommendation ("the designer chose this for a reason"), and changing them feels like taking a risk you could regret. For software, this means your defaults are the product for most people. Most users never open settings.

Anchoring

In Tversky and Kahneman's 1974 paper "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases", people spun a wheel of fortune rigged to land on 10 or 65, then estimated the percentage of African countries in the UN. Those who saw 65 gave much higher estimates. An arbitrary first number pulls later judgments toward it. In software: the first price people see sets their sense of what is expensive, the first suggested value in a field (a tip, a donation amount, a goal) pulls choices toward it, and the "was ₹ 2,999" next to a sale price works on anchoring. Honest uses: suggest sensible starting values. Dishonest uses: invented "original" prices.

Framing

Tversky and Kahneman's 1981 "Asian disease" problem described the same programs as "200 people will be saved" or "400 people will die" (out of 600). People preferred the sure option in the gain frame and the gamble in the loss frame. Same facts, different choice. In interfaces, "95% uptime" and "down 18 days a year" describe the same thing; "Free for 14 days" and "₹ 499 charged on 15 March" describe the same trial. Honest framing gives people the frame that helps them decide well, usually both numbers.

Loss aversion

Prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979) found that losses feel roughly twice as heavy as equivalent gains. Some researchers (Gal and Rucker, 2018) argue the effect is smaller and more context-dependent than usually claimed, but loss-framed messages do reliably catch attention. This is why "You'll lose your 120-day streak" is so powerful, and why it is so easy to abuse. Use loss framing to warn about real losses (unsaved work, data deletion), not to manufacture anxiety.

Choice overload: the jam study and what came after

Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper's 2000 paper described a tasting booth in a California supermarket with either 24 or 6 jams. More people stopped at the large display, but of those who stopped, about 30% bought a jar when there were 6 options, against about 3% with 24. It became one of the most cited findings in design.

Then the replications came. Benjamin Scheibehenne, Rainer Greifeneder and Peter Todd's 2010 meta-analysis of 50 experiments found an average choice-overload effect of virtually zero, with large variation between studies. Alexander Chernev and colleagues' 2015 meta-analysis found the effect does appear, but reliably only under certain conditions: when the choice set is complex, the decision task is difficult, people are unsure of their preferences, or they want to minimize effort. So the honest lesson is not "fewer options sell more". It is: make options easy to compare, help people who do not know what they want, and do not make a hard decision harder with clutter.

Watch out

Every effect in this chapter is also the engine of a dark pattern: pre-ticked boxes (defaults), fake discounts (anchoring), "confirmshaming" (framing), streak guilt (loss aversion). The line is simple to state: are you helping people get what they want, or getting them to do what you want against their interest? Deep coverage in Honest & Calm.

Do

  • Choose every default as if it were the only setting most users will ever have.
  • Use privacy-protective and cost-protective defaults.
  • Show real reference numbers (monthly vs yearly) rather than invented ones.
  • Give both frames for important numbers ("₹ 249/month, billed ₹ 2,499 yearly").
  • Make options comparable: same attributes, same order, a clear recommendation.

Don't

  • Pre-check paid add-ons, marketing consent or data sharing.
  • Show fake "original" prices.
  • Shame people for declining.
  • Use loss framing for things that are not real losses.
  • Cite the jam study as proof that fewer options always sell more.

In the wild

  • App Tracking TransparencyApple, iOS 14.5, 2021
    Turned cross-app tracking from a buried default into an explicit, plain-worded choice per app. Changing a default and its framing changed behavior across an entire industry.
  • Retirement auto-enrollmentUK workplace pensions, from 2012
    Employees are enrolled by default and can opt out; participation rose sharply. The textbook case of a default in the user's own long-term interest.
  • Basecamp pricing37signals
    Has long used very few, simply described plans with flat pricing. Easy comparison removes the conditions under which choice overload appears.

Common mistakes

Review every default as a deliberate design decision; write down why it is the best for most users.

Shaping the experience: peaks, ends, progress and beauty

Taught by Daniel Kahneman, with Ran Kivetz, Joseph Nunes, Xavier Drèze, Hedwig von Restorff and Masaaki Kurosu

People remember an experience by its most intense moment and its end, not its average. They speed up as a goal gets close, push harder when they feel they have a head start, notice the one thing that differs, and judge good-looking things as easier to use.

The peak-end rule

In a 1993 study by Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier, people held a hand in painfully cold water (14°C) for 60 seconds, and in another trial for 60 seconds followed by 30 more seconds at a slightly warmer, still unpleasant 15°C. Asked which to repeat, most chose the longer trial: more total pain, but a better ending. A 1996 study by Redelmeier and Kahneman of colonoscopy patients found that memories of the procedure tracked the worst moment and the final moments, not its length ("duration neglect").

For products: the remembered experience of your app is built from peaks (the best and the worst moments) and endings (how a task, session or subscription ends). A smooth hour can be remembered as awful because of one lost draft. A frustrating setup can be redeemed by a delightful first result.

Goal-gradient and endowed progress

Clark Hull observed in 1932 that rats in a maze ran faster as they got closer to food. Ran Kivetz, Oleg Urminsky and Yuhuang Zheng (2006) found the same in people: customers with a coffee-shop loyalty card bought coffee more often as they got closer to the free drink. Joseph Nunes and Xavier Drèze (2006) then gave car-wash customers loyalty cards needing 8 stamps, or cards needing 10 stamps with 2 already stamped. The task was identical (8 more washes), but 34% of the "head start" group completed it against 19% of the others. This is the endowed progress effect: progress that feels already begun motivates.

The isolation (von Restorff) effect

Hedwig von Restorff reported in 1933 that an item that differs from the others in a list is remembered better. It is the memory side of preattentive pop-out (chapter 4). Designers use it to highlight the recommended plan, the primary button, the one new item. It only works when one thing differs, and the effect is stronger for things that are also meaningful.

The aesthetic-usability effect

Masaaki Kurosu and Kaori Kashimura (1995) asked people at Hitachi to rate 26 ATM interface layouts for beauty and for expected ease of use. The two ratings correlated strongly. Noam Tractinsky replicated it in Israel in 1997, finding the effect even stronger. Later work showed that attractive interfaces are judged more usable and that people tolerate small problems in them more. Two honest notes. First, perceived usability is not actual usability: beauty can hide problems from your testers. Second, the effect is about first impressions and goodwill; it does not rescue a product that fails at its job. Visual craft is covered in Visual Design.

Do

  • Find and remove the worst moments first (data loss, confusing errors).
  • End key flows with a clear, satisfying completion state.
  • Show real progress toward goals people chose, and credit steps already done.
  • Isolate one recommended option and say why.
  • Invest in visual craft, and test usability with tasks, not opinions.

Don't

  • Average your satisfaction data and ignore the worst incidents.
  • End a flow on a blank page or a raw redirect.
  • Fake progress (starting a bar at 20% for nothing).
  • Highlight several options "to be fair"; it cancels isolation.
  • Let a beautiful prototype stand in for a usability test.

In the wild

  • MailchimpIntuit Mailchimp
    After you send a campaign, the mascot's hand gives you a high five. Sending an email campaign is stressful; the ending is a small celebration. A designed positive end to an anxious task.
  • AsanaAsana
    Occasionally shows a celebratory creature flying across the screen when you complete a task (it can be turned off). A playful peak, made optional for those who find it distracting.
  • LinkedIn profile strengthLinkedIn
    A meter shows how complete your profile is and suggests the next step. Goal-gradient and endowed progress in one widget; useful when the goal is the user's, nagging when it is not.

Common mistakes

Polishing the middle

Average flows get the attention because they are where most time is spent.

Rank problems by peak severity, not by frequency alone; one lost document outweighs a hundred slightly slow screens.

Celebrations that interrupt

Delight is added to every action.

Celebrate rare milestones, keep it short, and let people turn it off.

Endings that just stop

After submit, the code redirects to the home screen.

Design a completion state: what happened, what is next, and a moment of closure.

Check yourself

Your app's satisfaction is fine on average, but reviews often mention "it lost my work once and I never trusted it again". You have one week. Where does the peak-end rule say to spend it?

Memory weights the worst moment heavily, and that moment destroyed trust. Remove the negative peak first; positive endings come after.

Laws of complexity and convention: Tesler, Postel, Jakob, Occam, Pareto

Taught by Larry Tesler, with Jon Postel, Jakob Nielsen, William of Ockham and Joseph Juran

Every task has complexity that cannot be removed, only moved, so move it into the software. Accept messy input and produce clean output. Follow the conventions people learned elsewhere. Prefer the simplest design that works, and focus on the few features that do most of the work.

These five are rules of thumb, not experimental findings. They earn their place because each names a trade-off that designers face weekly.

Tesler's law: conservation of complexity

Larry Tesler (who worked at Xerox PARC and Apple, and is credited with cut, copy and paste as we know them) argued in the mid-1980s that every application has an inherent amount of complexity that cannot be removed. The only question is who deals with it: the user or the developer. He argued that an engineer should spend an extra week to save each of millions of users a minute a day.

Postel's law: liberal in, conservative out

Jon Postel wrote the robustness principle into early internet protocol specifications (RFC 760 in 1980 and RFC 793, TCP, in 1981): "be conservative in what you do, be liberal in what you accept from others." For interfaces: accept phone numbers with or without spaces, dates in several formats, pasted text with stray whitespace, emails with capital letters; then store and display them in one clean format. A caution from the protocol world: being too liberal can hide errors and create security issues, so be liberal about format, strict about meaning (do not guess when the input is genuinely ambiguous).

Jakob's law: people spend most of their time elsewhere

Jakob Nielsen observed that users spend most of their time on other sites and apps, so they expect yours to work the same way. In perception terms (chapter 1), conventions are predictions the brain already has. Breaking them has a cost every time, and must buy something worth more. Conventions to keep unless you have a strong reason: logo links home, search is a magnifying glass, the shopping cart is top-right on the web, swipe-back on iOS, Cmd/Ctrl+Z undoes, Esc closes, a gear means settings. Platform conventions are covered in Systems & Platforms.

Occam's razor and Pareto

Occam's razor, from the 14th-century philosopher William of Ockham, says not to multiply entities beyond necessity: among designs that solve the problem equally, prefer the one with fewest parts. Each extra element, setting or mode adds a little perceptual, memory and decision load from every chapter of this guide. The Pareto principle, named by quality expert Joseph Juran after the economist Vilfredo Pareto's observation of income distribution, says that roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes. In products it is often true that a small set of features accounts for most use, and a few bugs cause most crashes. Treat the 80/20 as a shape, not a precise ratio.

Do

  • Spend engineering time to remove user effort on frequent tasks.
  • Accept common formats and normalize them.
  • Follow platform and web conventions for controls, gestures and shortcuts.
  • Remove features and settings that few people use and that add load for everyone.
  • Find your 20%: the features most use comes from, and make them excellent.

Don't

  • Push complexity onto users because it is easier to build.
  • Guess when input is genuinely ambiguous (03/04 could be March or April).
  • Reinvent standard controls for novelty.
  • Treat 80/20 as an exact law, or as a reason to neglect accessibility needs of the "20%".

And Occam's razor is your friend twice: every feature you do not build is a feature you never have to maintain, document or support.

The fixed complexity (currency, category, date) is being carried by the user every time. Defaults move it into the software, and the full form stays available for the exceptions.

Motivation: autonomy, competence, relatedness, and B = MAP

Taught by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, with BJ Fogg and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi

Lasting motivation comes from feeling in control, getting better, and being connected to others. A behavior happens when motivation, ability and a prompt meet at the same moment. Make the right action easy and well-timed; do not try to pump motivation with tricks.

Self-determination theory

Edward Deci and Richard Ryan developed self-determination theory from the 1970s onward (major statements in 1985 and 2000). It is one of the most researched theories of human motivation. It holds that people have three basic psychological needs, and that activities which satisfy them are pursued for their own sake (intrinsic motivation), while activities that thwart them feel like chores. A famous early finding by Deci (1971) was that paying people for an activity they already enjoyed could reduce their interest in it afterwards: external rewards can crowd out intrinsic motivation when they feel controlling.

AutonomyReal choices, customization, easy export, no lock-in, explaining whyForced flows, nagging, hidden settings, guilt, punishing breaks

Fogg Behavior Model: B = MAP

BJ Fogg at Stanford proposed in 2009 that a behavior (B) happens when three elements converge at the same moment: Motivation (M), Ability (A), and a Prompt (P; he originally called it a "trigger"). If any is missing, the behavior does not happen. Motivation and ability trade off: a very easy behavior needs little motivation, a hard one needs a lot. The prompt only works when the person is above the "action line". His 2019 book Tiny Habits applies it to personal habit building: make the behavior tiny, anchor it to an existing routine, celebrate.

The ethics of behavior design

The same model that helps someone build a reading habit can be used to keep people scrolling at 2 a.m. Several products built on persuasive design have been criticized for exactly that, and some of their designers have since spoken publicly against attention-maximizing design. A practical test for your product:

  • Whose goal is it? Prompts that help people do what they set out to do are help. Prompts that create new wants on your schedule are manipulation.
  • Would they thank you later? Imagine the user looking back at a month of your prompts. Would they say "that helped" or "that hooked me"?
  • Can they turn it off easily? If not, you do not believe they would choose it.

This is developed fully in Honest & Calm.

Do

  • Make the desired behavior tiny and easy (the first step under 10 seconds).
  • Let users set their own prompts (time, frequency, channel).
  • Give clear feedback on growing skill (competence).
  • Make it easy to leave, export and pause (autonomy).
  • Connect people to real people where it helps (relatedness).

Don't

  • Try to manufacture motivation with points, badges and pressure.
  • Punish breaks with lost streaks and guilt.
  • Send prompts when the user cannot act (above the action line or not).
  • Show fake social proof ("27 people are viewing this").

    Tiny lessons (ability), daily reminders (prompt), visible progress (competence), and streaks. It added "streak freezes" so a missed day need not erase progress. A strong behavior-design product, and a live debate on where streak pressure stops being helpful.
  • StravaStrava, 2009–

  • ObsidianObsidian, 2020–
    Notes are plain Markdown files on your own disk, and almost everything is configurable. Deep autonomy as a core value attracts deeply loyal users.
For the solo creator

You cannot out-gamify companies with behavior-science teams, and you should not try. Your strengths are autonomy (no lock-in, honest export), competence (a tool that makes people better at something), and relatedness (you, a real person, answering their emails). Those three are SDT's needs, and they are the reasons people love small, independent software.

Common mistakes

Gamifying intrinsic activities

Points feel like engagement.

If people already want to do it (write, read, learn), reward with feedback on their actual progress, not arbitrary points that can crowd out interest.

Prompting the unable

The reminder fires at 9 a.m. for everyone.

Let users pick the time, and make the action doable from the prompt itself.

Check yourself

Your journaling app sends a daily 9 p.m. reminder. Many users ignore it; some uninstall. Using B = MAP and SDT, what is the best change?

User-chosen timing respects autonomy and times the prompt to when they can act; a one-line entry raises ability; no punishment avoids the guilt that makes people leave.

Emotion: visceral, behavioral, reflective, and trust

Taught by Don Norman, with Noriaki Kano, BJ Fogg and Aarron Walter

People respond to a product on three levels at once: an instant gut reaction to how it looks, a feeling from how it works in use, and a considered judgment of what it means to them. Delight is real but fragile; trust is slow to build and quick to lose.

Norman's three levels

In Emotional Design (2004), Don Norman, who had been known for championing usability over beauty, argued that attractive things work better, and offered a model of three levels of processing:

  • Visceral: the instant, pre-conscious reaction to appearance, sound and feel. It is where the aesthetic-usability effect lives (chapter 17), and where your app icon, first screen and typography do their work. Studies by Gitte Lindgaard and colleagues (2006) found people form reliable impressions of web pages' visual appeal in as little as 50 milliseconds.
  • Behavioral: the pleasure and frustration of use: control, speed, predictability, the feel of scrolling and typing. This is most of this guide.
  • Reflective: conscious thought about the product: what owning or using it says about you, the story you tell others, memories of using it. It is shaped by brand, values and peak-end memories.

Delight versus satisfaction: the Kano pointer

Noriaki Kano's model (1984) sorts features by how they affect satisfaction. Basic features cause dissatisfaction when missing but no delight when present (sync that works, undo). Performance features increase satisfaction roughly in proportion (faster search). Delighters are unexpected and cause outsized pleasure, but people do not miss them when absent. Over time, delighters decay into expectations: what delighted in 2010 is basic in 2026. The practical rule: delight sits on top of a working foundation, never instead of it. Aarron Walter's Designing for Emotion (2011) expressed this as a hierarchy: functional, reliable, usable, then pleasurable. Kano is applied to prioritizing features in Problems Worth Solving.

Trust signals

Trust is a judgment about whether a product will act in your interest when you are not watching. The Stanford Web Credibility Project, led by BJ Fogg (2002), asked over 2,500 people to compare websites' credibility; the most frequently mentioned factor was the "design look" of the site. That is System 1 judging competence from polish. But the visual impression is only a first filter. Lasting trust comes from behavior over time:

Do

  • Design the first screen and icon for a calm, confident visceral impression.
  • Get basics flawless before adding delighters.
  • Match tone to the moment: playful in success, plain and specific in failure.
  • Show your name, your pricing and your data practices plainly.
  • Keep delight brief, rare and optional.

Don't

  • Rely on a beautiful surface to cover unreliable behavior.
  • Use jokes and mascots in error and loss moments.
  • Fake trust signals (invented user counts, stock-photo testimonials).
  • Make cancellation harder than sign-up.

Common mistakes

Law-washing

Naming a law makes a preference sound scientific.

State the observed problem and the evidence level; let the test decide.

Single-law thinking

One law is memorable.

Run the six-stage diagnosis; most real problems involve two or three stages.

Check yourself

In a test, a user says "I didn't know I could drag notes into folders". At which stage did it fail, and what is the likely fix?

The action has no signifier (perception) and relies on recall (memory). A visible alternative and clear drop feedback fix both; size and number of folders are irrelevant until people know dragging exists.

Wrap-up

You now have a working model of the person on the other side of the screen: a visual system that groups and predicts, an attention that holds one thing, a memory that holds about four, a hand that obeys Fitts, a decision-maker who satisfices and follows defaults, and a human who wants autonomy, competence and connection and remembers peaks and endings. Every later guide builds on this. Typography is perception applied to reading; motion is common fate and response time; words are recognition and framing; ethics is what you do with all of it.

Ten things to remember

  1. Perception is prediction. Conventions and clear edges let people's brains guess right.
  2. Group with space first, regions second, lines last. Spacing is meaning.
  3. Pop-out works only on a quiet background. Spend salience on one or two things.
  4. If a change is not at the locus of attention, assume it was not seen.
  5. People scan for anchors; give them meaningful headings and front-loaded words.
  6. Working memory holds about four chunks. Never make people carry information between screens.
  7. Habits are tied to position. Keep frequent controls where they are; prefer undo to confirmation.
  8. Bigger and closer is faster. Acknowledge every action within 0.1 s.
  9. Defaults, anchors and frames shape choices. Use them for the user, never against them.
  10. Remove the worst moment, design the ending, and build on autonomy, competence and relatedness.
