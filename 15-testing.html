Testing & Iteration. Find out what is true, then change the product.

You built it, so you cannot see it the way a stranger does. This guide teaches you how to borrow other people's eyes cheaply (usability tests, quick prototype tests, interviews), how to sharpen your own (heuristic reviews, walkthroughs, self-critique), how to read what the numbers say without fooling yourself, and how to turn all of it into a steady loop of small, confident changes. Everything here is sized for one person with a day job's worth of hours.

Warm-up: what do you already believe?

Before reading, answer three questions from instinct. Do not look anything up. The point is to surface the beliefs you carry into testing, because the most common reason solo creators never test is a quiet assumption that it needs a lab, a budget or a team. Your answers will be revisited at the end of Part two.

Diagnostic 1

You ask a friend to try your note app. She says "Looks great, I'd totally use this." What have you learned?

Opinions, especially from people who like you, are the weakest evidence there is. She was not lying; she was being kind, and predicting her own future behavior, which humans do badly. You learn about usability by watching someone try to do a real task. Chapter 1 builds the evidence ladder that makes this obvious.
Diagnostic 2

Your app gets 300 sign-ups a month. You want to know whether a new onboarding screen raises activation from 30% to 33%. What is the honest answer?

Detecting a 30% to 33% change reliably needs thousands of users per variant. At 300 a month, that is years. Small products learn mostly from observation, interviews and large, obvious changes, with numbers as a sanity check. Chapter 14 has a calculator so you can feel this.
Diagnostic 3

A participant in your test hesitates for ten seconds on the settings screen, then finds the toggle. Afterwards she says the app was "really easy." Which do you write down as the finding?

People rate experiences generously after the fact, and they forget struggles that ended in success. The hesitation is the data. Chapter 6 shows how to log behavior so it does not get lost in the participant's polite summary.
How to study this guide

Read one Part per sitting. Do each chapter's exercise on your own product before moving on; the guide is designed so that by the end of Part two you have actually run a test. The checklists save in this browser, so you can return to them every month.

Part one

Why test at all

One chapter on the root problem: you are the worst-placed person on earth to judge whether your product is clear. Not because you lack taste, but because you know too much. Everything else in this guide is a way around that.

1

You are not your user

Taught by Jakob Nielsen, with Elizabeth Newton, Chip & Dan Heath, Erika Hall

The person who built a product cannot un-know how it works, so their sense of "obvious" is broken; the only cure is to watch people who do not share that knowledge, and to trust what they do over what anyone says.

In 1990 a Stanford graduate student, Elizabeth Newton, ran a simple experiment. One person tapped the rhythm of a well-known song like "Happy Birthday" on a table; another person listened and tried to name the song. Before the listeners guessed, the tappers predicted how often they would succeed: about half the time. The real figure was 3 songs out of 120, about 2.5%. The tappers could hear the melody in their heads while tapping. The listeners heard knocking. Chip and Dan Heath popularized the study in Made to Stick (2007) as the clearest picture of the curse of knowledge: once you know something, you cannot imagine not knowing it.

You are the tapper. You hear the melody of your product every time you look at it. You know that the small grey icon top-right opens sync settings because you drew it. You know that "Collections" means tagged groups because you named the database table. You know that swiping left archives because you wrote the gesture handler. A first-time user hears knocking.

Why your intuition fails in a specific way

The curse of knowledge does not make you wrong about everything. It makes you wrong in a predictable direction: you underestimate how hard discovery is, how ambiguous your labels are, and how much people skim. You tend to be right about what is technically possible and wrong about what is noticeable. That is useful, because it tells you where to point your tests: at discovery (can they find it?), comprehension (do they know what it means?), and expectation (does it do what they thought it would?).

There are three other biases stacked on top. Familiarity: you have seen each screen hundreds of times, so problems feel normal. Effort justification: you spent a week on a feature, so you want it to be good. Confirmation: you notice the one user who loves the feature and forget the six who never opened it. None of these go away with experience. Senior designers test because they know they have them too.

The evidence ladder: do > say

Not all evidence is equal. Jakob Nielsen's blunt 2001 article "First Rule of Usability? Don't Listen to Users" made the point: what people say they do, and what they say they want, is a poor predictor of what they actually do. People are not lying. They are reconstructing, guessing and being polite. The ladder below ranks evidence for a solo creator by how much it should move your decisions.

Two refinements make the ladder practical. First, combine levels: numbers (level 6) tell you where people drop off; a usability test (level 5) tells you why. Second, past beats future. "Tell me about the last time you exported a document" (level 4) is far more trustworthy than "Would you use an export feature?" (level 3), because one is a memory and the other is a prediction. Rob Fitzpatrick's The Mom Test (2013) is built entirely on this rule.

Myth

"I'm my own target user, so I don't need to test." Being in the audience helps you pick the right problem (see Problems Worth Solving). It does not help you see your interface fresh. You are a user with perfect knowledge of the internals, which no one else will ever be.

Key idea

Your eyes are calibrated to the melody. Testing is how you hear the knocking.

Do

  • Treat every belief about clarity ("people will get this") as a hypothesis until someone outside your head has used it.
  • Ask about the past ("last time you…") instead of the future ("would you…").
  • Combine a number that shows where with an observation that shows why.
  • Write down your prediction before each test, so you can measure how wrong you were.

Don't

  • Demo the product and then ask for a reaction.
  • Count compliments as evidence.
  • Dismiss a confused user as "not the target audience" without checking.
  • Assume more experience will cure the bias. It only makes you faster at hiding it from yourself.

For the solo creator

You have no colleague to say "I don't get it." That makes you more exposed to the curse than a team is, not less. The fix is not a research department; it is a small, regular habit: one hour a month watching someone use your product, plus a written prediction you check against what happened. Parts two to four show exactly how.

Common mistakes

Testing with your partner or best friend only

They are available and kind. But they have heard you talk about the product for months, so they carry part of your knowledge, and they want you to succeed.

Use friends-of-friends who have never seen it. If you must use close people, give them a task and stay silent, and weight their findings lower.

Explaining before observing

You want them to "get it," so you give a tour first. The tour destroys the test: you have just transferred your knowledge.

Give a goal, not a tour. Explanation is allowed only after the session, and anything you needed to explain is itself a finding.

Arguing with the result

A participant misses something obvious and you think, "they just weren't paying attention."

Real users don't pay attention either. If one person in three misses it, assume a third of your users miss it.

Check yourself

You're deciding whether to build tags or folders for a reading app. Which piece of evidence should weigh most?

Specific past behavior (level 4) beats stated preference (a poll, level 3), your own opinion (level 1), and imitation (no evidence at all about your users). The stories also reveal the real job, which might be "find it again" rather than "organize," pointing to search instead of either.

2

Small, often: the five-user idea and its limits

Taught by Jakob Nielsen & Thomas Landauer, with Steve Krug, Laura Faulkner, Jared Spool

A handful of people doing realistic tasks reveals most of the big, common problems in a design, so many tiny rounds with fixes in between beat one large study; but "five is enough" is a rule about common problems in one flow, not a law.

In 1993 Jakob Nielsen and Thomas Landauer modeled how usability problems are discovered. If each problem has a chance L of being hit by any one participant, then after n participants you have found a share of 1 − (1 − L)n. Across the projects they studied, the average L was about 31%. Plug that in and five users find roughly 84% of the problems. The curve rises steeply and then flattens: user six mostly repeats what users one to five already showed you.

Nielsen's 2000 article "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users" turned this into a recommendation: rather than spend your budget on 15 users at once, run three rounds of five, fixing the problems between rounds. The second round tests your fixes and finds the problems the first set was hiding (you cannot discover that step three is confusing while everyone is stuck on step one).

Try L = 31% (Nielsen and Landauer's average), then drop it to 10%, which is closer to a complex product with many rare paths. Notice how the "five is enough" promise collapses when problems are rare, and how little the 12th user adds when they are common.

The limits of five

The formula assumes every problem has the same L, and that your five people are similar and doing the same tasks. Reality breaks all three. Laura Faulkner (2003) ran a study with 60 participants and drew random sets of five from them: some sets found most of the known problems, while unlucky sets found barely over half. Jared Spool and Will Schroeder (2001) tested large e-commerce sites and found that five users came nowhere near most problems, because each person took different paths through a huge site. The honest reading:

  • Five finds the common problems in the flow you tested. Those are the ones that matter most, so it is a great deal.
  • Rare problems need more people or different methods (analytics, support tickets, long-term use).
  • Different audiences need their own five. Writers and editors, new and returning, phone and desktop: each group is a separate pool.
  • Five is for finding problems, not measuring them. "3 of 5 failed" means "this is a real problem", not "60% of users fail." For percentages you need far larger samples.

Krug's monthly morning

Steve Krug's Rocket Surgery Made Easy turns this into a routine anyone can run: test with three people, one morning a month, debrief the same day, and fix the few most serious problems before the next round. He argues three is enough because you are looking for the problems that most people will hit, and because a fixed monthly date means testing actually happens. For a solo creator, the calendar is the method. A date on the calendar beats a plan to "test properly once the redesign is done."

  1. Week before. Pick the flow to test (usually whatever you just changed or whatever support complains about). Write 3 to 5 tasks. Recruit 3 people.
  2. Morning, 9:00 to 12:00. Three sessions of about 45 minutes, with 15-minute gaps to write notes while they're fresh.
  3. Lunch, 12:30. Debrief yourself: list everything you saw, pick the top three problems.
  4. Afternoon. Decide the smallest change that fixes each. Krug's advice is to tweak rather than redesign: the least you can do that solves it.
  5. Next month. Test again, including the fixed flow.

Do

  • Book a recurring date (first Saturday of the month, say) before you have anything "ready."
  • Run small rounds and fix between them.
  • Test each distinct audience or platform separately.
  • Use "3 of 5 struggled" as a signal of a real problem.

Don't

  • Wait for a large, perfect study.
  • Report "60% task failure" from five people as if it were a measurement.
  • Expect five users to find rare, deep or edge-case problems.
  • Keep testing the same broken flow without fixing it in between.

For the solo creator

Three people, two hours, one fix list, once a month: that is twelve rounds and thirty-six sessions a year, more than many funded startups manage. If even that is too much, do one session every two weeks. Consistency matters more than sample size.

Common mistakes

Treating five as a magic number

The rule is quoted without its assumptions.

Use five (or three) per audience per round, for finding common problems. Use other methods for rare issues and measurement.

Testing everything at once

You want to get your money's worth, so you write twelve tasks covering the whole app.

Focus each round on one or two flows. Depth on one flow reveals more than skimming ten.

Never retesting fixes

You fix the problem and assume it's solved.

Next round, include the fixed flow. Fixes often create new problems, or only half-work.

Check yourself

Your app has two very different audiences: students taking lecture notes and lawyers drafting documents. You have budget for six sessions this month. Best plan?

Different audiences hit different problems, so each needs its own small sample. Three per group finds each group's most common issues. Random mixing (a) risks five students and one lawyer. Waiting (d) produces nothing.

Recruiting cheaply

Taught by Steve Krug, with Erika Hall, Teresa Torres

You need people who can plausibly do the task and who do not know your product's internals; you rarely need perfect matches, so recruit loosely, screen for the few traits that change behavior, and build a standing list so next month is easy.

Krug's advice is to "recruit loosely and grade on a curve": unless your product requires specific domain knowledge, almost anyone who uses similar software will reveal the big problems, and you mentally adjust for people who are less experienced than your real users. What matters most is that participants are not you: not designers or developers who will critique instead of use, and not people who already know how the product works.

The traits worth screening for are the ones that change behavior: Do they do the job your product is for (take notes, track money, read long articles)? Which platform do they use daily? Have they used your product before (and do you want that)? Everything else, such as age or job title, matters far less than founders tend to think.

A screener in five questions

A screener is a short form that filters participants. Write it so the "right" answer is not obvious, or people will tell you what you want to hear.

Incentives and courtesy

Pay people, or give them something of real value. A gift card, a year of your product, or a donation in their name all work. Amounts vary by country and audience: a small voucher works for a 20-minute consumer session, while professionals (doctors, lawyers, engineers) expect considerably more. Paying is not only fair; it changes the relationship from a favor to a job, which makes people less polite and more honest. Send the thank-you within 24 hours.

For the solo creator

Build a test list once and you will never "recruit" again. Add one opt-in checkbox to sign-up or a line in your welcome email. Keep a simple sheet: name, contact, audience, platform, last tested date. Rest each person for at least three months so they do not become experts. Twenty names is enough for a year of monthly rounds.

Do

  • Screen on behavior in a recent time window.
  • Hide the target answer among distractors.
  • Pay or reward people promptly.
  • Keep a standing test list with "last tested" dates.

Don't

  • Use designers or developers for usability tests.
  • Over-specify demographics that do not change behavior.
  • Reuse the same three people every month.
  • Recruit only fans from your newsletter for first-run tests.

Writing tasks that don't give the answer away

Taught by Steve Krug, with Jakob Nielsen, Erika Hall

A good task gives a realistic goal and the motivation behind it, in the user's words, and never names the button, label or path you want them to find; a bad task is an instruction in disguise.

The task is the most powerful lever in a usability test, and the easiest to ruin. If your task says "Use the Collections feature to group your notes," you have told the participant the feature exists, what it is called and roughly what it does. They will scan for the word "Collections," find it, and succeed. You will learn that people can match words. You will not learn whether anyone would ever think to group notes, or where they would look.

A good task is a scenario: a short story with a goal and a reason, like real life. "You're preparing for three exams next week and your notes for each subject are mixed together. Get them organized so you can revise one subject at a time." Now the participant has to translate a real need into your interface, which is exactly what real users do.

Judge each task wording. Score: 0 of 6.

More rules for tasks

  • Plant the data. If the task is "find the article about sleep," make sure a test account contains it, among realistic decoys.
  • Order tasks like real life: sign up before you organize. But put a task you most care about early, in case time runs out.
  • Allow multiple right paths. Write down what counts as success before the session, not during.
  • Hand tasks over on paper or in chat so the wording is identical for each person and they can re-read it.
  • Let participants invent one task: "What would you normally do with an app like this? Show me." Their own task is often the most revealing.
  • Pilot the tasks on anyone (even a friend) before the real session, to catch ambiguous wording.

Do

  • Write a reason and an end state for every task.
  • Use the user's vocabulary, not your interface's.
  • Decide success criteria in advance.
  • Pilot once.

Don't

  • Name buttons, tabs, menus or features.
  • Ask "can you find…" or "try out…" with no goal.
  • Ask yes/no comprehension questions.
  • Write more tasks than fit in 30 minutes.

Common mistakes

The label leak

You think in your interface's vocabulary, so it slips into tasks.

After writing, highlight every word that also appears in your UI. Replace each with the user's word for the goal.

Tasks with no end

"Explore the dashboard" feels open and friendly.

Every task needs a moment when the participant (and you) knows it is done.

Fantasy scenarios

"Imagine you're a CEO with 400 employees…" makes people role-play, badly.

Pick scenarios that fit the participant's real life; screen for it, or let them adapt details.

Moderating without leading

Taught by Steve Krug, with Clayton Lewis, Jakob Nielsen, Erika Hall

Your job in the session is to make the participant comfortable thinking out loud, then get out of the way: neutral prompts, no hints, no defending, and every question answered with a question.

Thinking aloud is the core technique: you ask participants to say what they are looking at, what they are trying to do and what they expect, as they go. It was brought into usability work at IBM by Clayton Lewis in the early 1980s, and Nielsen has called it the single most valuable usability engineering method. It works because it externalizes the participant's mental model at the moment of action: "I'd expect this to be under the menu… no… maybe this gear?" is pure gold, and you would never get it from a later interview.

Thinking aloud is unnatural, so you have to teach it gently at the start and prompt it during the session without steering. The hardest discipline for a solo creator is silence. You built this. When they struggle, every instinct says help. Don't. The struggle is the finding.

Remote, in person and unmoderated

How: a video call with screen share (any tool you and they already use). For phone apps, ask them to share their phone screen, or join from the phone while a laptop camera points at it.

Recording and consent

For the solo creator

Synthesis: the rainbow spreadsheet

Prototype testing at every fidelity

Taught by Carolyn Snyder, with Bill Buxton, Jake Knapp

Match the fidelity of the prototype to the question you're asking: paper for structure and concept, clickable mockups for flow and labels, coded prototypes for feel, speed and real data; higher fidelity costs more and makes people critique polish instead of ideas.

Fidelity is not a ladder you must climb in order. It is a set of tools, each good at answering certain questions. Quick methods: first-click, five-second, preference, tree tests, card sorts

Taught by Bob Bailey & Cari Wolfson, with Donna Spencer, Jared Spool

Each quick method answers one narrow question with less effort than a full usability test; pick by question: where do they click first, what do they remember, which do they understand, where would they look, how do they group things.

Full sessions are rich but slow. Quick methods trade richness for speed and numbers: many of them run unmoderated, with 20 to 50 participants, in a day. They are best used to settle a specific design question before or between usability rounds.

First-click testing

Show a screen and a task ("Where would you click to read this article offline?"), record only the first click. Research by Bob Bailey and Cari Wolfson found that when people's first click was correct, they went on to complete the task about 87% of the time, against about 46% when the first click was wrong. The first click predicts the rest. That makes first-click tests a cheap check on labels and layout, especially navigation and home screens.

Five-second tests

Show a screen for five seconds, hide it, ask: "What was this page for? What do you remember?" It tests whether the purpose and the main hierarchy come through at a glance, which is exactly what a landing page or an empty home screen must do. If people remember your decorative illustration and not what the product does, your hierarchy is upside down (see Visual Design). It cannot test usability; nobody uses anything in five seconds.

Preference tests (with caveats)

Show two or more designs and ask which people prefer and why. Useful for tone and aesthetic direction (two icon styles, two onboarding illustrations). Dangerous for anything about usability: people prefer what looks familiar and attractive, and their preference does not predict which one they can use. Always ask a comprehension question first ("What do you think this button does?"), then the preference, and treat the "why" answers as more valuable than the vote.

Tree testing

Strip your navigation down to a text hierarchy (no visual design at all) and ask people where they would go to do a task. It isolates your information architecture from your layout. If people can't find "Offline reading" in a bare tree, no amount of icon polish will save it. Tools like Optimal Workshop's Treejack automate it; you can also do it with a nested list on paper. Watch two numbers: success (did they end in the right place?) and directness (did they get there without backtracking?).

Card sorting

Give people cards with your content or features on them and ask them to group the cards and name the groups. In an open sort they invent the groups, which reveals their mental model and vocabulary. In a closed sort you supply the group names and see whether your categories make sense. Donna Spencer's Card Sorting (2009) is the standard guide. Use open sorts early, when you're designing navigation; use tree tests afterwards to check the structure you built. For more on structure itself see Structure & Interaction.

9

Nielsen's ten heuristics, in depth

Taught by Jakob Nielsen & Rolf Molich, with Don Norman

Ten broad rules of thumb, distilled from hundreds of real usability problems, let you inspect any interface and catch many common problems without users; each one is a lens that makes a category of failure visible.

Jakob Nielsen and Rolf Molich introduced heuristic evaluation in 1990. In 1994 Nielsen refined the list by analyzing 249 usability problems and finding the set of principles that explained them best. The ten have barely changed since (NN/g refreshed the wording in 2020), because they describe how people work, not how technology looks. They also echo Norman's principles from The Design of Everyday Things: feedback, mapping, constraints, conceptual models (see Foundations and The Human Mind).

Nielsen also found that a single evaluator catches only a fraction of the problems (around a third, in his studies), and that several evaluators working independently find far more, because different people notice different things. You are one evaluator. That's a reason to use the heuristics as a structured lens, do several passes, and still test with users.

1 · Visibility of system status

The system should always tell people what is going on, through timely feedback. Every action needs an answer: "I heard you," "I'm working," "Done," or "That failed." Silence makes people tap again, wait anxiously, or leave. Rough timing guide: under 0.1 s feels instant; up to about 1 s keeps flow without a spinner; beyond a few seconds needs progress (see Motion & Feel).

2 · Match between the system and the real world

Speak the user's language: words, concepts and order that are familiar to them, not to your database. Internal names (entity, sync conflict, workspace object) leak in because you see them every day in code. Order should follow the real-world task: a recipe app lists ingredients before steps.

3 · User control and freedom

People act by mistake all the time. They need a clearly marked "emergency exit": undo, cancel, back, close, without a long process. Undo is better than confirmation dialogs, because people click through confirmations automatically but can always recover with undo (see Structure & Interaction).

4 · Consistency and standards

People should not wonder whether different words, positions or actions mean the same thing. Two kinds: internal consistency (your app agrees with itself) and external (it follows platform conventions, which Nielsen elsewhere calls Jakob's Law: users spend most of their time in other products). Follow the platform unless you have a strong reason not to (see Systems & Platforms).

5 · Error prevention

Better than a good error message is a design that prevents the error. Norman distinguishes slips (right goal, wrong action, like tapping Delete instead of Save) from mistakes (wrong goal from a wrong mental model). Prevent slips with spacing, constraints and good defaults; prevent mistakes with clear previews and explanations.

6 · Recognition rather than recall

Minimize what people must remember. Show options, recent items and examples instead of expecting them to remember commands, codes or what was on the previous screen. Working memory is small (see The Human Mind); every "remember this" is a tax.

7 · Flexibility and efficiency of use

Shortcuts, hidden from novices, speed up experts. The best designs let the same person grow: menus show keyboard shortcuts next to commands, so people learn them by using the menu. Command palettes (⌘K) serve both: novices search in words, experts type three letters.

8 · Aesthetic and minimalist design

Every extra unit of information competes with the relevant ones and reduces their visibility. This is not "make it pretty" or "make it empty": it is "make what matters win." Rams's "less, but better" and Tufte's data-ink ratio are the same idea (see Visual Design).

9 · Help users recognize, diagnose and recover from errors

Error messages should be in plain language, say precisely what went wrong, and suggest a fix, right where the problem is. Keep what the person typed. For the words themselves, see Words.

10 · Help and documentation

Ideally nothing needs explaining. When it does, help should be easy to search, focused on the person's task, short, and available in context, at the moment of need.

    Self-critique and fresh eyes

    Taught by Adam Connor & Aaron Irizarry, with Dieter Rams, Oliver Reichenstein

    Useful critique starts from an objective ("this screen exists so that…") and asks whether each element serves it; to see your own work freshly, change the conditions you see it under, and to get useful critique from others, ask narrow questions.

    Adam Connor and Aaron Irizarry's Discussing Design (2015) separates critique from feedback in general. Critique is analysis against objectives: "Given that the goal is X, this element does or does not help, because Y." Reaction ("I don't like the blue") is not critique; neither is a direct fix ("make the button bigger"), because it skips the why. As a solo creator you must critique yourself, which means writing the objective first, otherwise you will judge by mood.

    The objective-first critique

    Write it as a sentence with a person, a situation and an outcome. Then, for each element on the screen, ask the three critique questions: What is this for? Does it serve the objective? Is it the best way to serve it? The answers are keep, change, move, or cut. Pair this with the visual hierarchy test: is the most important element for the objective also the most visually prominent?

    Product metrics a designer should read

    Taught by Alistair Croll & Benjamin Yoskovitz, with Eric Ries, Andrew Chen

    Three questions cover most of what a small product needs to know: do new people reach the moment of value (activation), do they come back (retention), and how much do they use it (engagement); everything else is either a detail of these or a vanity number.

    A design change is a hypothesis about behavior. Metrics are how you see behavior at a scale no usability test can reach. But a dashboard with 40 charts is as useless as none, because you will stare at whatever moved and invent a story. Pick a small set, define them precisely, and look at them on a schedule.

    Activation
    The share of new users who reach the first moment of real value, defined as a specific action: "created a note and opened it again later," "imported a book and read 5 pages." Not sign-up. See Problems Worth Solving for designing that moment.
    Retention
    The share of a group of users (a cohort, usually by sign-up week) still active N days or weeks later. The single best sign that the product is valuable.
    Engagement
    How much retained users use the product: sessions per week, notes created, minutes read. Useful only alongside retention, and only when "more" is actually better for the user.
    Conversion
    The share of people who take a specific step: visitor to sign-up, trial to paid.
    Churn
    The share of users or subscribers who leave in a period; the mirror image of retention.

    Funnels: where people leave

    A funnel lines up the steps toward an outcome and shows how many people make it through each. Its job is to point you at the step with the worst drop, so that your next usability test focuses there.

    Cohort retention curves: the truest chart

    Group users by when they started, and plot what share is still active over time. Every product's curve falls; the question is whether it flattens. A curve that flattens means a core of people found lasting value: you have something. A curve that keeps sliding toward zero means people try it and leave, and no amount of acquisition will fix that. When newer cohorts' curves sit above older ones, your changes are working.

    Vanity metrics

    Eric Ries, in The Lean Startup (2011), called numbers that make you feel good but don't inform decisions "vanity metrics," contrasting them with "actionable" ones. Total sign-ups, total downloads and page views only go up; they can't tell you whether anything you did worked. The test: if this number changed, would I do something differently? If not, stop looking at it.

    For the solo creator

    Track three numbers, weekly, in a plain note: activation rate for last week's sign-ups, week-4 retention for the cohort from a month ago, and your North Star (next chapter). Write one sentence each week on what changed and what you shipped. Small numbers are noisy: at 50 sign-ups a week, activation can swing by 10 points by chance alone. Look at trends over a month, not a single week.

    Do

    • Define activation as a specific action that signals value.
    • Read retention by cohort and look for flattening.
    • Use funnels to choose what to usability-test next.
    • Annotate charts with what you shipped and when.

    Don't

    • Celebrate cumulative totals.
    • Equate sign-up with activation.
    • React to one week's wobble in small numbers.
    • Push engagement up with tricks when "more" isn't better for users (see Honest & Calm).

    In the wild

    • Facebook's early activation ruleoften-cited growth story
      Facebook's growth team is widely reported to have focused on getting new users to a certain number of friends within their first days, because users who did tended to stay. An activation metric names the behavior that predicts retention. Find yours by comparing who stayed with who left.
    • "Lean Analytics"Croll & Yoskovitz, 2013
      Introduces the "One Metric That Matters" for each stage of a business. Focus beats breadth: one number at a time is something you can actually move.

    Common mistakes

    Activation defined as sign-up

    It's the easiest event to track.

    Find the first action that predicts people coming back, and use that.

    Averaging retention across everyone

    A single "active users" chart is easy.

    Cohorts by sign-up week; otherwise growth hides churn.

    Story-telling from noise

    Every wiggle seems to mean something.

    Decide in advance which change you expect and how big; look at monthly trends.

    Check yourself

    Monthly active users rose 30% after your launch on a big forum. Week-4 retention for that cohort is 4%, versus 18% for earlier cohorts. What do you conclude?

    MAU mixes new and retained users, so a spike of visitors inflates it. The cohort shows those users left. Either they weren't your audience, or the first-run experience didn't serve them. That's a great usability-test question for next month.

    HEART, a North Star and honest instrumentation

    Taught by Kerry Rodden, Hilary Hutchinson & Xin Fu, with Sean Ellis, Teresa Torres

    Start from the user-experience goal, choose a signal that shows it, and only then a metric (Google's HEART goals–signals–metrics method); summarize the value you create in one North Star number; and measure with the minimum data, named clearly, collected respectfully.

    In a 2010 CHI paper, "Measuring the User Experience on a Large Scale," Kerry Rodden, Hilary Hutchinson and Xin Fu at Google described the HEART framework: five categories of user-centered metrics, Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention and Task success, plus a process, Goals → Signals → Metrics, for choosing them. The process matters more than the acronym: it stops you from measuring what's easy instead of what matters.

    A North Star for a small app

    A North Star metric is a single number that captures the core value users get, and that tends to lead to revenue and retention. It should count value delivered, not activity or revenue. For a reading app, "articles read to the end per week"; for a habit tracker, "users who checked in on 4+ days this week"; for a writing app, "documents edited on 3+ days in a week." A good one rises only when users are actually better off.

    Naming events

    Your events are a vocabulary you'll live with for years. A common convention is object + past-tense action: note_created, article_finished, export_completed. Keep a short tracking plan: event name, when it fires, properties, why you need it. If you can't write the "why," don't track it.

    Experiments, and what to do without traffic

    Taught by Ron Kohavi, with Evan Miller, Eric Ries

    An A/B test randomly splits people between versions so the only systematic difference is your change; it's the gold standard for causation, but detecting realistic improvements takes thousands of users per variant, so small products should mostly ship bold changes, roll out in stages and compare carefully.

    Ron Kohavi, who led experimentation at Microsoft and wrote Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments (2020, with Diane Tang and Ya Xu), has reported that most ideas tested at large companies fail to improve the metrics they targeted. That is humbling and useful: intuition about which change will help is unreliable even for experts. A/B tests protect against that. The catch is statistical power. Randomness means two identical groups will show different conversion rates by chance; to tell a real 10% improvement from noise, you need enough people that the noise is small.

    Sample size intuition

    A handy approximation (Lehr's rule, for 80% power at the usual 5% significance) is: users per variant ≈ 16 × p(1 − p) ÷ d², where p is the baseline rate and d the absolute difference you want to detect. Small baselines and small effects explode the number. Play with it.

    Start with 30% activation, a 10% relative lift (30% → 33%) and 75 new users a week: that's about 300 a month. Then try detecting a 50% lift. Big, bold changes are measurable at small scale; tweaks are not. This is an approximation for intuition, not a calculator for a real test.

    Rules if you do run one

    • Decide the sample size and the metric before you start, and don't stop early when it "looks significant." Evan Miller's essay "How Not To Run an A/B Test" (2010) shows how repeatedly peeking and stopping at the first significant result produces false winners far more often than the nominal 5%.
    • Run full weeks, because weekday and weekend users behave differently.
    • Check that the split is actually even; a lopsided split means a bug.
    • Watch a guardrail metric (retention, complaints, refunds) so a "win" on clicks doesn't hide harm.

    Feedback channels: what users tell you unprompted

    Taught by Teresa Torres, with Bob Moesta, Rob Fitzpatrick, Fred Reichheld

    Support emails, reviews, in-app feedback and churn are a free, continuous research stream; treat them as data to tag and count, not as a to-do list, and dig beneath each request for the problem behind it.

    Every message a user sends cost them effort, so it signals something real. But feature requests are proposed solutions from people who don't know your constraints or your other users. "Add folders" might mean "I can't find things," which search could fix better. The job is to collect, tag, count and then ask about the underlying problem.

    The channels

    In-app feedback. A quiet "Send feedback" item in the menu or settings that opens a plain text box (with an optional screenshot and app version attached automatically). Don't interrupt people with pop-ups; let them come to you. Reply to every message personally; a reply from the maker turns a complainer into a collaborator.

    Support tickets as research. Every "how do I…?" is a usability finding: someone failed a task in real life. Tag each ticket with the problem and the screen. Once a month, count. If ten people asked how to export, export is a discoverability problem, not ten support problems. A good test: could the product have answered this question itself?

    App reviews. Read them weekly, especially one- and two-star reviews with specifics. Both Apple's App Store and Google Play let developers reply publicly. On iOS, the system review prompt (SKStoreReviewController) is limited to three displays per user in a 365-day period, so ask after a moment of success, never after an error. Remember the sample is skewed toward people who feel strongly.

    Net Promoter Score. Fred Reichheld introduced it in a 2003 Harvard Business Review article: "How likely are you to recommend us to a friend?" on 0–10; promoters (9–10) minus detractors (0–6). Critics, including many UX researchers, point out that it asks for a prediction rather than behavior, throws away information by bucketing, varies by culture, and is very noisy at small sample sizes. If you use it, the follow-up "What's the main reason for your score?" is the useful part. A specific question about a recent experience usually teaches more.

    Churn and cancellation. When someone cancels, ask one optional question with a few common reasons and a free-text box, then let them go immediately (no guilt trips; see Honest & Calm). Email a few recent churners personally and offer a short call. Bob Moesta's "switch interview" approach asks about the timeline: what was happening when they first thought about leaving, what they tried, what they switched to.

    Interviewing existing users

    Talk to users regularly, not only when something breaks. Teresa Torres recommends weekly touchpoints with customers. The core rule is from Chapter 1: ask about specific past behavior. "Tell me about the last time you saved an article. Where were you? What happened next?" Story-based questions reveal context, workarounds and the real job. For interview technique in depth, see Problems Worth Solving.

    For the solo creator

    You are the support team, which is a superpower: every ticket lands on the designer's desk. Keep one "voice of user" log (a spreadsheet is fine): date, channel, quote, problem tag, screen. Spend 30 minutes on the first Monday of each month counting tags. Let the top theme pick next month's usability test.

    Do

    • Log every piece of feedback with a problem tag.
    • Reply personally, quickly.
    • Ask about the problem behind each request.
    • Make cancellation easy and ask one optional question.

    Don't

    • Build the most-requested feature literally.
    • Interrupt work with rating pop-ups.
    • Treat NPS as a goal in itself.
    • Guilt-trip people who leave.

    In the wild

    • Indie apps with a named makercommon pattern among small developers
      Many one-person apps sign their support replies and feedback screens with the developer's own name. Users write differently to a person than to a "team": more context, more patience.
    • Superhuman's segmentationRahul Vohra, 2018
      After the "very disappointed" survey, the team focused on what the users who'd be most disappointed loved, and on what held back people who were close to that. Not all feedback is equal; weigh it by who it comes from.

    Common mistakes

    The loudest-user roadmap

    One persistent user emails every week.

    Count across users. One person's ten emails count once.

    Reading reviews only after launches

    They feel painful.

    A weekly 10-minute review habit, logged with tags. Small doses.

    Survey fatigue

    Surveys are easy to send.

    At most one short survey per quarter; prefer one well-placed question after a real moment.

    Check yourself

    Twelve users this month asked for "a dark mode." What should you do first?

    The request is probably real, but the underlying need shapes the solution: following the system theme, a warm low-contrast night theme for readers, or reduced brightness. Two quick conversations make the build much better.

    Dogfooding, a diary of your own use, and a bug bash for one

    Taught by 37signals, with Linear and the indie-maker tradition

    Using your own product daily for real work finds problems no test will (the ones that only appear on day 40, with messy data, when you're tired); but you are a biased power user, so log your use deliberately, and complement it with tests of people who aren't you.

    "Eating your own dog food" became a common phrase in software after it circulated inside Microsoft in the late 1980s. Many of the best-loved tools are made by people who use them all day: Basecamp's team runs its company on Basecamp; Linear has said it builds Linear in Linear. Daily use reveals slowness, paper cuts, and what happens after months of accumulated data, which no 45-minute session can.

    But dogfooding has a blind spot shaped exactly like the curse of knowledge. You never experience first-run again; you know every shortcut; you forgive what you planned to fix. So turn it into a method: a diary, scheduled "beginner days," and a structured bug bash.

    A diary of your own use

    Keep a running note while you use your product for real work. Every time something annoys, slows or surprises you, write one line immediately: date, what you were trying to do, what happened, how it felt. Don't fix it on the spot (that breaks your work and biases you toward easy fixes). Review the diary weekly and move recurring items into your issue tracker.

    DateTrying toWhat happenedFelt
    MonFind last week's meeting noteSearch matched 40 notes, no dates shownannoyed (3rd time)
    TuePaste a long articleEditor froze ~2 s on 12,000 wordsworried
    ThuOpen app on trainSpinner, no offline contentgave up, used Notes

    Beginner days

    Once a month, use your product as a beginner would: delete the app and reinstall it, sign up with a new email, go through onboarding without skipping, on your oldest device, with no shortcuts allowed. Do the core task from scratch. It won't fully cure the curse, but it re-exposes you to first-run, which you otherwise never see after launch.

    A bug bash for one

    1. Timebox it. 90 minutes before each release, phone on silent.
    2. Change roles every 15 minutes. The beginner, the power user, the impatient one (tapping fast, double-tapping), the offline one (airplane mode), the accessibility user (screen reader, largest text size; see Accessible & Inclusive), the destroyer (paste huge text, emoji, right-to-left text, rotate, background the app mid-save).
    3. Log, don't fix. One line per issue with steps to reproduce.
    4. Triage with severity (0 to 4) at the end. Ship-blockers are 4s and data-loss risks.
    For the solo creator

    If you aren't a daily user of your own product, find a way to become one, even partially, or find two people who are and talk to them weekly. If you are, the diary is the cheapest research you'll ever do: 30 seconds per entry. Keep it in the app itself if you can; capturing friction inside the friction is poetic and practical.

    Do

    • Use your product for real work every day if you can.
    • Log friction immediately, fix later.
    • Schedule beginner days and bug bashes.
    • Test on old devices, offline, with big text and messy data.

    Don't

    • Treat your own use as user research.
    • Fix things mid-task and lose the thread.
    • Test only on your development machine.
    • Build only for your power-user self.

    In the wild

    • Basecamp37signals
      The company runs its own projects in Basecamp and has written about how this shapes the product. Deep daily use is a quality engine for the things you do yourself.
    • Linearissue tracker, 2019 onward
      The team has described building Linear in Linear, which helps explain its focus on speed and keyboard flow. Dogfooding pushes products toward what their makers value; make sure that's what your users value too.

    Common mistakes

    Power-user drift

    Daily use makes you want shortcuts and density; new users want clarity.

    Balance diary items against first-run test findings each month.

    The unlogged annoyance

    You notice friction, sigh, and forget.

    One-line diary entries, every time. Count them weekly.

    Check yourself

    You've used your own app daily for a year and can't find anything confusing anymore. What's the best interpretation?

    Familiarity hides confusion. Dogfooding still catches day-40 problems, but first-run and comprehension need fresh eyes: beginner days help a little, new users help a lot.

    Iteration: the loop, the log, and the courage to delete

    Taught by Eric Ries, with Ryan Singer, Teresa Torres, Dieter Rams

    Iteration is a disciplined loop: state what you expect, ship the smallest change that tests it, look at the evidence, and decide (keep going, change direction, or remove); writing each turn of the loop down is what turns activity into learning.

    Eric Ries's Build–Measure–Learn loop, from The Lean Startup (2011), describes startups as machines for validated learning: build the minimum needed to test an assumption, measure what happens, learn whether to persevere or pivot. Ries points out that you plan the loop backwards: decide what you need to learn, then what you'd measure, then what to build.

    The iteration log

    One row per change. Write the first three columns before you ship. This is the solo version of pre-registering an experiment: it protects you from your own hindsight.

    Persevere, change, or kill

    Ryan Singer's Shape Up (Basecamp, 2019) adds a useful discipline: set an appetite (how much time this problem is worth) before you start, and use a circuit breaker: if a project doesn't ship within its cycle, it doesn't get extended automatically; it has to be re-pitched. For a solo creator, that means "two weekends for this; if it isn't working, I stop and rethink."

    Deleting features

    Every feature has a cost after it ships: code to maintain, settings to explain, screens that dilute the core, tests to run, support questions. In most products, usage follows a long tail: a few features carry most of the value. Rams's "less, but better" applies to features as much as to form.

    1. Measure use and ask who. Low use by your most loyal users is different from low use by nobody.
    2. Count the cost. Bugs, support questions, settings, code complexity, design constraints.
    3. Announce early and honestly. Say what's going, why, when, and how to keep their data (export).
    4. Remove completely. Code, settings, docs, onboarding mentions. Half-removed features haunt you.
    5. Log it and watch. Note support volume and retention for a month after.
    Accessibility testing

    Every loop should include an accessibility check, because what isn't tested regresses. Screen reader passes, keyboard-only navigation, largest text sizes, contrast and reduced motion each have specific test procedures, covered in depth in Accessible & Inclusive. At minimum, add one role to your bug bash and one item to your design review.

    For the solo creator

    Your iteration speed is your advantage over big companies: no meetings, no approvals. Your risk is the opposite: nobody asks "did that work?" The log asks for you. Review it monthly; once a quarter, read the whole thing and write one paragraph on what you learned about your users. That paragraph is the most valuable document you own.

    Do

    • Write the expectation before you ship.
    • Set an appetite and a limit for each bet.
    • Give every feature a verdict.
    • Delete features that cost more than they give, honestly and completely.

    Don't

    • Ship and move on without looking back.
    • Rationalize any result as success.
    • Keep half-working features "for now" indefinitely.
    • Remove features silently or without an export path.

    In the wild

    • Instagramfrom Burbn, 2010
      Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger's first app, Burbn, was a check-in app with many features; they noticed photo sharing was what people actually used, and stripped the product down to it. Usage evidence can tell you which small part of your product is the real product.
    • Slackfrom the game Glitch, 2013
      Slack began as the internal chat tool of a team building an online game; when the game was shut down, the tool became the company. A pivot keeps what you learned and changes what you bet on.
    • Shape UpRyan Singer, Basecamp, 2019
      Six-week cycles with fixed time and variable scope, appetites set before work starts, and a circuit breaker on overruns. Fixed time with flexible scope forces the trade-offs iteration needs.

    Common mistakes

    Moving the goalposts

    The result disappoints, so you find another metric that went up.

    The "I expect" column is written before shipping and doesn't change.

    Iterating on polish, not on value

    Visual tweaks are satisfying and safe.

    Ask of each loop: what did I learn about users? If nothing, it was decoration.

    Never deleting

    Removing feels like admitting failure, and someone might complain.

    Schedule a quarterly "remove one thing" review. Every removal is a gift of focus to the rest.

    Check yourself

    You added a "graph view" two months ago. 3% of weekly users open it, mostly once. It generates frequent bug reports. What's the best next step?

    Evidence first (who and why), then an honest decision. Sunk cost (c) is not a reason. Promotion (a) treats low use as a marketing problem without evidence. Silent deletion (d) breaks trust.

    Wrap-up

    Testing isn't a phase; it's how you stay honest with yourself. The whole guide reduces to a monthly rhythm: watch three people, read your three numbers, count your feedback tags, review your diary, update your log, and make the few changes the evidence supports. An hour a week, sustained, will make your product better than most built by teams.

    Ten things to remember

    1. You can't un-know your product. Testing is how you borrow eyes that don't.
    2. Behavior beats opinion; past behavior beats predicted behavior.
    3. Three to five people per round find the common problems; many small rounds beat one big one.
    4. Tasks are scenarios with goals, never instructions with labels.
    5. In the session, be quiet: answer questions with questions, and save explanations for the debrief.
    6. Rate severity by frequency, impact and persistence; fix the top few with the smallest change that works.
    7. Heuristics, walkthroughs and self-critique find candidates; users confirm them.
    8. Activation, cohort retention and one North Star that counts real value are enough numbers for a small product.
    9. At small scale, A/B tests can only see big differences; make bold changes, roll out carefully, and watch trends.
    10. Write what you expect before you ship, give every feature a verdict, and delete without guilt.
