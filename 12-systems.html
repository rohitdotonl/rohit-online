Systems & Platforms. Make every decision once, then respect the ground you build on.

A design system is how one person ships like a careful team: you decide the color of an error, the height of a button and the way a dialog behaves once, write it down in code, and never think about it again. Platforms are the other half of the same idea. iOS, macOS, Android, Windows and the web each come with a system of their own, and your product feels right when it speaks their language.

Warm-up: how systematic is your product today?

Before any theory, a quick diagnostic. You already have a design system. Every product does. The only question is whether it is written down and deliberate, or scattered across a hundred files as accidents. Answer these honestly about the product you are building now (or the last one you shipped).

  1. Count your grays. Search your stylesheet or Swift code for color literals. How many distinct grays are there? Teams that have never audited usually find a dozen or more, and nobody chose most of them.
  2. Count your button styles. Open five screens. How many different paddings, radii and font weights do your buttons use?
  3. Try dark mode. If you flipped to dark mode today, how many files would you have to touch? One, or fifty?
  4. Ask the platform question. On your main platform, where does the back action live, where do settings live, and which side of a dialog does the confirm button sit on? If you hesitate, Part five is for you.

Why a system, when you are one person

Taught by Nathan Curtis, with Brad Frost and Massimo Vignelli

A design system is a set of decisions you make once and reuse forever. For a team it is about alignment; for one person it is about speed, consistency and protecting your limited attention from a thousand tiny choices.

Most writing about design systems is written for companies with fifty designers and three hundred engineers. Their problem is coordination: how do you stop twelve teams from inventing twelve date pickers? You do not have that problem. You have a different one, and it is just as real: every time you build a screen, you face dozens of micro-decisions. How much padding here? Which gray for this caption? Is this button 36 or 40 pixels tall? Should the error be red text or a red border? Each decision costs a little attention, and attention is the scarcest thing a solo creator has.

Worse, you make each decision slightly differently each time, because you made the last one three weeks ago and do not remember. The result is drift: four grays that are nearly the same, buttons that are 38, 40 and 42 pixels tall, and dialogs that put the confirm button on different sides. No single screen looks wrong. Together they make the product feel subtly cheap, the way a room feels off when the picture frames are hung at slightly different heights. People rarely name it, but they feel it, and it lowers their trust.

Three things a system buys you

Consistency. When the same thing always looks and behaves the same way, people learn it once. A red text link that deletes in one place and a red filled button that deletes in another make people re-learn. Consistency is a usability feature first and an aesthetic one second (it is one of Jakob Nielsen's ten usability heuristics: "consistency and standards").

Speed. Once you have a Button, a TextField, a Sheet and a ListRow that you trust, a new screen is assembly, not design. Many solo creators report that their second year of building is several times faster than the first for exactly this reason.

Fewer decisions. Decision fatigue is real enough at the end of a long building day. A system turns "what padding?" into "space-4", which is not a decision at all. You will make better product decisions with the attention you save.

The minimum viable system

The danger for a solo creator is the opposite of the team's: building a system for its own sake. A beautiful Figma library with 140 components and seven documentation pages is a hobby, not a tool, if your product has nine screens. The minimum you need is small and fits on a page or two of code:

  1. Tokens. Named values for color, type, spacing, radius, shadow and motion. Perhaps 40 to 80 of them. Part two is about these.
  2. About a dozen components. Button, icon button, text field, select or picker, checkbox and switch, list row, card or section, sheet or dialog, menu, toast, empty state, navigation bar. That covers most of the screens of most small products.
  3. A handful of patterns. How you do forms, confirmation, undo, errors, loading, empty states and search. A pattern is a recipe made of components; see Structure & Interaction for the patterns themselves.
  4. Voice. Ten rules for how your product speaks: sentence case, verbs on buttons, no blame in errors. Covered in Words.
A design system isn't a project. It's a product, serving products.Nathan Curtis, title of an essay on EightShapes' Medium publication, 2016

Curtis wrote that for organizations, but it lands differently for you. Your system is a product whose only customer is you, six months from now, tired, at 11 pm, trying to add a feature quickly. Design it for that person: obvious names, few options, no cleverness.

Myth

"A design system will make my product look generic." A system decides how consistently you apply your choices, not which choices you make. iA Writer, Things and Linear are among the most distinctive products of their eras, and each is extremely systematic. Distinctiveness comes from a few strong decisions applied everywhere, which is exactly what a system enforces.

For the solo creator

Do not stop building to "make a design system". Extract it. Build two or three screens, notice what repeats, and pull the repeated values into tokens and the repeated pieces into components. A system extracted from real screens fits your product; a system designed up front fits an imaginary one. Budget one afternoon a month for this, forever.

Do

  • Treat the system as code your product imports, not a separate document.
  • Start with tokens, then the five components you use on every screen.
  • Extract from screens you have already built.
  • Keep the whole system small enough to hold in your head.

Don't

  • Spend a month on a component library before the product has users.
  • Copy a big company's full system; you would maintain 200 things you never use.
  • Add a component for something that appears once.
  • Confuse the Figma file with the system. The shipped code is the system.

In the wild

  • NYC Transit Authority Graphics Standards ManualUnimark (Vignelli, Noorda), 1970
    A ring binder that specified every sign in the subway: typeface, arrow shapes, color discs for lines, module sizes. Decades before software, it is a design system in the full sense: a few rules, applied relentlessly. A system is decisions plus documentation. The medium does not matter.
  • iA WriterInformation Architects, 2010–today
    Very few typefaces, one accent color, a tightly limited set of controls across Mac, iOS, Windows and Android. The product feels like one object on every device. A small system, held tightly, is a brand.
  • LinearLinear, 2019–today

Common mistakes

Building the system before the product

It feels like productive, grown-up work, and it postpones the scary part (finding out if anyone wants the thing).

Build three real screens first. Extract tokens and components from them in one afternoon. Grow the system only when something repeats a third time.

Treating the design file as the source of truth

Designers are taught that Figma is where design lives. For a solo builder, the design file and the code drift apart within weeks.

Make the code the source of truth. If you keep a design file, generate or update it from the code's token file, not the other way round.

"One-off" exceptions that never get folded back

A screen needs something slightly different, you hard-code it "for now", and now is forever.

Allow exceptions, but mark them with a comment like // SYSTEM-EXCEPTION: so a monthly search finds them. Either promote them to the system or remove them.

Check yourself

You are three weeks into a new note-taking app with four screens. Which is the best next move for your system?

Extraction (b) captures decisions you have already proven on real screens, costs an afternoon, and pays back immediately. (a) builds for imaginary screens. (c) lets drift compound until the cleanup costs weeks. (d) can be a fine starting point for components, but adopting everything unchanged means maintaining far more than you use and erasing your product's identity.

Atomic design, and where it breaks

Taught by Brad Frost, with critiques from the practice

Atomic design is a mental model: interfaces are made of small parts that combine into larger ones. It is a superb way to think about reuse, and a poor filing system if you take the chemistry literally.

In 2013 Brad Frost published a blog post, and in 2016 a book, both called Atomic Design. Borrowing from chemistry, he described five stages. Atoms are the smallest useful parts: a label, an input, a button, a color. Molecules are small groups that do one job together: a search form made of a label, an input and a button. Organisms are larger sections made of molecules: a header containing a logo, navigation and the search form. Templates place organisms into a page layout with placeholder content, showing the structure. Pages are templates filled with real content, which is where you discover whether the system actually holds up when a title is 80 characters long or a list is empty.

What atomic design gets right

The model's lasting gift is a habit of mind: look at any screen and see it as parts and wholes at the same time. When you design a settings screen, you are also designing a list row, a switch and a section header that every future screen will use. Frost was clear that the stages are not a step-by-step process:

Atomic design is not a linear process, but rather a mental model to help us think of our user interfaces as both a cohesive whole and a collection of parts at the same time.Brad Frost, Atomic Design, chapter 2, 2016

The second gift is the page stage. Many systems stop at components and never stress-test them with real, ugly content. The page stage says: put the 80-character title, the user with no avatar, the list with 2,000 items and the list with zero items into the layout, and see what breaks.

Where it breaks

Practitioners have criticized the literal taxonomy for years, and the criticisms are worth knowing because you will feel them yourself:

  • The boundaries are arbitrary. Is a card a molecule or an organism? Is a list row with an avatar, two labels and a switch a molecule? Teams lose hours to these debates, and the answer never changes what anyone builds.
  • The chemistry names mean nothing to anyone else. "Organism" does not tell future-you where the date picker lives. "Forms", "Navigation" and "Feedback" do.
  • Real systems are graphs, not ladders. A button appears inside a dialog, a toolbar, a card and a form. A dialog can contain a form that contains a list. Parts compose in every direction.
  • It says nothing about behavior. States, keyboard handling, accessibility and motion are where most component work actually goes, and the atomic model is silent about them.

What to keep, what to drop

Keep the habit of thinking in parts and wholes, and keep the page stage as your stress test. Drop the five folders. Organize your components by job, the way most published systems now do: Actions (button, icon button, link), Inputs (field, select, checkbox, switch), Feedback (toast, banner, progress, empty state), Navigation (tab bar, sidebar, breadcrumb), Containers (card, sheet, dialog, popover). Future-you will look for "the thing that shows a message after save" under Feedback, never under Molecules.

Do

  • See every screen as parts you are also designing for later reuse.
  • Test components inside real pages with worst-case content.
  • Group components by purpose: actions, inputs, feedback, navigation, containers.

Don't

  • Debate whether something is a molecule or an organism.
  • Name folders after chemistry.
  • Assume a component is done because it looks right with placeholder text.

In the wild

  • Pattern LabBrad Frost and Dave Olsen, 2013
    An open-source tool built to make atomic design concrete: you build atoms, molecules and organisms as templates and see them assembled into pages with real data. The page stage needs tooling that renders real content, not just a gallery.
  • Storybookopen source, 2016–today
    The most common way web teams view components in isolation, one "story" per state. It is organized by whatever folders you choose, and most teams choose purpose-based ones. Isolation is good for building; real pages are still needed for testing.

Common mistakes

Taxonomy over function

Classification feels like rigor.

Pick purpose-based folders once and move on. If a component could live in two, pick the one you would search first.

Never reaching the page stage

Components look perfect in isolation with "Lorem ipsum" and one-word labels.

Keep a "torture page" in your app (debug builds only) that renders every component with the longest, shortest, emptiest and most international content you can find.

Check yourself

Your reading app's article card looks perfect in your component gallery. In the real feed it breaks when a German headline has a 30-letter compound word. Which stage of atomic design would have caught this?

Templates show structure with placeholder content; pages put real content in, and that is exactly where long words, missing images and empty states show up. The fix (hyphenation, overflow-wrap, a maximum of lines) belongs to the component, but the discovery happens at the page stage.

Part two

Design tokens

Tokens are the atoms that are not even components: the named values every pixel of your product is made of. Get their layers and names right and theming, dark mode, high contrast and a future rebrand become an edit to one file.

4

Tokens in three layers

Taught by Nathan Curtis, with Jina Anne and the W3C Design Tokens Community Group

A design token is a named design decision. The power comes from layering: primitives say what values exist, semantic tokens say what each value is for, and component tokens say where it is used. Code only ever reads the upper layers.

The term "design tokens" was popularized at Salesforce around 2014, where Jina Anne and Jon Levine used it for the named values that fed the Lightning Design System across web and native platforms. The idea is older than the name: any time you wrote $brand-blue in Sass you were using a token. What changed is the discipline around them, and that discipline is mostly about layers.

Layer 1: primitives (also called global, base or core tokens)

Primitives are your palette and your scales: every gray, every hue step, every spacing step, every font size. Names describe the value, not the use: gray-600, blue-500, space-4, radius-2. They are the paint on your palette. Nothing in your interface should reference them directly, because the moment a screen says "gray-600" it has made a decision that a theme cannot change.

Layer 2: semantic tokens (alias or system tokens)

Semantic tokens name a purpose: color-text, color-text-secondary, color-bg, color-surface, color-border, color-accent, color-danger, space-inset-card, radius-control. Each points to a primitive. This is the layer your product mostly reads, and the layer that changes between themes: in light mode color-text points to gray-900; in dark mode it points to gray-50. Not a single component changes.

Layer 3: component tokens (optional for you)

Component tokens name a specific slot in a specific component: button-primary-bg, field-border-focus. Large systems need them so that one team can tweak the button without touching everything that uses color-accent. A solo creator usually does not. Add a component token only when a component genuinely needs to differ from the semantic default, for example when your primary button uses a slightly darker accent than links for contrast. Otherwise, components read semantic tokens directly and you have two layers, which is plenty.

/* tokens.css: the whole idea in 20 lines */
:root {
  /* 1 · primitives: values, named by what they are */
  --gray-900: #1C1D1F;  --gray-600: #5F6267;
  --gray-200: #D6D6D2;  --gray-50:  #F1F1EF;
  --amber-500: #E9A800; --red-600: #B03D2B;
  --space-2: 8px; --space-3: 12px; --space-4: 16px;

  /* 2 · semantic: purposes, named by what they are for */
  --color-text:           var(--gray-900);
  --color-text-secondary: var(--gray-600);
  --color-bg:             var(--gray-50);
  --color-border:         var(--gray-200);
  --color-accent:         var(--amber-500);
  --color-danger:         var(--red-600);
  --space-inset-card:     var(--space-4);

  /* 3 · component: only where a component must differ */
  --button-primary-bg:    var(--color-text);
  --button-primary-label: var(--color-bg);
}

If you ship on more than one platform, keep tokens in one neutral file (usually JSON) and generate CSS variables, Swift constants and Android resources from it. For the solo creator

Two layers are enough to start: primitives and semantic. Aim for about 12 to 20 semantic color tokens, not 200. A good starter set: bg, surface, surface-raised, border, border-strong, text, text-secondary, text-tertiary, accent, accent-text (text on accent), accent-soft, danger, success, warning, focus-ring, overlay. If you cannot say in one sentence when to use a token, delete it.

Do

  • Name primitives by value on a scale, semantic tokens by purpose.
  • Make components read semantic tokens, never primitives.
  • Order name parts consistently, general to specific.
  • Keep the semantic set small enough to memorize.

Don't

  • Put a hue in a semantic name (--color-blue-button).
  • Make a token for a value used once.
  • Create component tokens for every component "just in case".
  • Rename tokens casually; every rename is a migration.

Common mistakes

Semantic names that are really primitives

--color-primary-blue feels semantic because it says "primary".

Remove every hue word from semantic names. If the token would be wrong after a rebrand, it is a primitive wearing a costume.

Token explosion

Each new screen adds "just one more" token: --settings-row-gap, --profile-row-gap.

Before adding, ask whether an existing semantic token already describes the purpose. Most "new" tokens are an existing one with a new name.

Skipping the semantic layer

Primitives feel sufficient until dark mode arrives.

Introduce semantic tokens now, even if each points to one primitive. The indirection costs nothing today and saves the dark-mode rewrite later.

Check yourself

Your app's success toast uses --green-600 directly. You are adding a colorblind-friendly theme where success should be blue. What is the cleanest change?

A semantic token (c) names the purpose, so each theme can point it at a different primitive. Redefining --green-600 as blue (a) makes the primitive lie about itself and breaks everything else that wanted green. Theme checks inside components (b, d) spread theming logic across the codebase.

5

Scales: space, type, radius, shadow, motion

Taught by the 8-point grid tradition, with Material, Apple and Radix

Primitives are not a bag of values; they are scales. A scale with a clear rule (each step twice the last, or a fixed ratio) makes good choices easy and bad ones impossible.

Ask someone to choose "the right padding" and they will deliberate. Give them a scale of eight steps and they will pick in a second, and their picks will relate to each other visually. That is the whole trick. Scales turn a continuous space of options into a small menu where every item looks good next to every other item.

Spacing: a 4-point base

Most modern systems use a base unit of 4 or 8 points. Both divide evenly into common screen densities (1×, 2×, 3×), so edges land on whole pixels. A 4-point base with steps that grow faster as they get bigger works for almost everything: small steps for tight relationships inside a control, large steps for separating sections. The jump between steps should be visible; if you cannot see the difference between two steps, you have one too many.

Type: a modular scale, few steps

Font sizes are a scale too. A ratio of about 1.2 (a "minor third") to 1.25 (a "major third") gives sizes that feel related: 13, 15, 17, 20, 24, 30. Most products need five or six text styles, each a token bundling size, line height, weight and letter spacing: text-caption, text-body, text-body-strong, text-title, text-headline, text-display. On Apple platforms, use the built-in text styles (.body, .headline, .title2…) as your tokens, because they scale with Dynamic Type for free. The details of choosing the scale are in Typography.

Radius: few values, and the nesting rule

Three or four radii are enough: radius-small (about 4) for chips and small controls, radius-control (about 8) for buttons and fields, radius-container (about 12 to 16) for cards and sheets, and radius-full for pills and avatars. When one rounded shape sits inside another, the inner radius should equal the outer radius minus the padding between them. Otherwise the gap between the curves is uneven, and the corners look pinched.

Shadow and elevation: two or three levels

Shadows say "this is above that". A product needs very few heights: flat (most things), raised (cards, if you use shadows at all), overlay (menus, popovers) and modal (dialogs, sheets). Make each a token bundling offset, blur and opacity. In dark mode shadows almost disappear against dark backgrounds, so dark themes usually express elevation by making higher surfaces slightly lighter instead. That is one reason color-surface-raised is a separate semantic token.

Motion: durations and curves

Motion gets tokens too: three or four durations (for example duration-fast about 120 ms for hovers and toggles, duration-base about 200 to 250 ms for most transitions, duration-slow about 350 to 400 ms for large surfaces like sheets) and two or three easing curves (standard, enter, exit). Tokenizing motion also gives you one place to honor reduced-motion settings. The craft of timing is in Motion & Feel.

Dark mode is not inversion

Inverting light mode produces glaring white text on pure black, accents that vibrate, and shadows that vanish. A good dark theme follows a few rules that come up again and again in platform guidelines:

  • Dark gray, not black, for most surfaces. Very dark gray lets you show elevation with lighter surfaces and reduces the halo around bright text. (Pure black can be right for OLED-focused media apps, but it is a choice, not a default.)
  • Off-white text, and less contrast for secondary text than you think. Body text around 87 to 92% white is common.
  • Desaturate and lighten accents. Saturated colors on dark backgrounds vibrate; a lighter, slightly less saturated accent reads as the same brand.
  • Elevation by lightness. Raised surfaces get a lighter gray rather than a stronger shadow.
  • Check every pair again. Contrast ratios change; WCAG asks for at least 4.5:1 for body text and 3:1 for large text and UI component boundaries. See Accessible & Inclusive.

Component anatomy, variants and props

Taught by Nathan Curtis, with the Figma and SwiftUI API designers

Every component has named parts (its anatomy) and a small set of choices (its API). Design the API like a function signature: few parameters, clear names, no impossible combinations.

When you design a component you are designing two things at once. The first is what people see: a container, a label, maybe an icon and a focus ring. The second is what future-you sees: the options you can pass when you use it. The second is easier to get wrong, and harder to fix later, because every screen that uses the component depends on it.

Anatomy: name the parts

Draw the component and label every part. This sounds pedantic until you need to say "the leading icon in the text field gets the secondary text color, except in the error state". Named parts make specifications, tokens and bug reports precise.

Variants versus props

Figma calls them variants and properties; code calls them props, parameters or modifiers. Either way, a component's options come in two kinds:

  • Variants change what the component is: a primary versus a secondary versus a destructive button. Different jobs, different appearance. Keep them few and named by purpose.
  • Props configure an instance without changing its identity: the label text, an optional icon, the size, whether it is full-width, whether it is loading.

The trap is multiplying them. Four kinds × three sizes × seven states × with-or-without icon = 168 combinations. In Figma, teams sometimes draw all 168 as variants. In code, nobody draws them; they fall out of the logic. That is one of the strongest arguments for designing components in code as a solo creator (more in Chapter 11): code handles combinations by composition, while a drawing tool needs each one drawn.

Rules for a good component API

  1. Few options, named by purpose. kind: .destructive, not color: .red. A purpose can be restyled; a color is a promise you cannot keep.
  2. Make impossible states impossible. If a button cannot be both loading and disabled-with-reason, use one state enum instead of two booleans.
  3. Sensible defaults. The most common use should need the fewest arguments: Button("Save").
  4. Content is a prop, not a variant. "Button with icon" is not a different component; the icon is optional content.
  5. No escape hatches for style. A customColor prop is the start of drift. If you need a new look, add a new purpose-named kind.

Composition over configuration

When a component grows its fifteenth prop, it is usually trying to be several components. A Card with props for header, subtitle, image, badge, two buttons, a menu and a footer is a monster nobody can predict. Prefer small pieces that nest: Card contains a CardHeader, any content, and an optional CardActions that contains Buttons. SwiftUI and React both reward this style: views made of views.

Sizes

Most components need at most three sizes, and many need one. A common set: small for dense toolbars and tables (desktop only, pointer input), regular for almost everything, large for one primary action on a mobile screen or a landing page. Remember that on touch screens the hit area must stay at least 44 × 44 points (Apple) or 48 × 48 dp (Material) even when the visible control is smaller. Size is a prop, and the hit area is a rule the component enforces on its own.

For the solo creator

Write the component's call signature before you style it. If you cannot write Button(...) in one readable line for the five most common uses, the API is wrong, and no amount of visual polish will save it. Then build exactly the variants your screens use today.

Do

  • Draw and label the anatomy of each core component.
  • Name variants by purpose; keep them to three or four.
  • Use enums for mutually exclusive states.
  • Compose small pieces instead of adding props.

Don't

  • Draw every combination of every option.
  • Expose raw style props such as color or padding.
  • Create a new component for "button with icon".
  • Let one component grow past about eight props.

In the wild

    A Button holds an action and a label; how it looks is a separate ButtonStyle you apply, and roles like .destructive and .cancel describe purpose. Separate what a component does from how it looks.
  • Radix DialogWorkOS, Radix Primitives
    A dialog is built from parts: Root, Trigger, Portal, Overlay, Content, Title, Description, Close. You compose exactly what you need. Composition makes complex components flexible without props soup.

The state matrix

Taught by every platform's interaction guidelines, with WCAG

A component is not finished until every state is designed: default, hover, focus, pressed, disabled, loading and error, in every variant. Missing states are where products feel broken.

Designers draw the default state, because that is what appears in mockups. Users live in all the others. They hover with a mouse before clicking, tab with a keyboard, press and hold on a phone, wait while something loads, and hit errors. Each of those moments needs a deliberate answer, and the answer should be the same everywhere in your product.

Default (rest)
The component waiting for input. Everything else is a change from this.
Hover
A pointer is over it. Pointer devices only; touch screens have no hover, so hover must never be the only way to discover something.

It will receive keyboard input. Must be clearly visible: WCAG 2.2 adds requirements for visible focus that is not hidden by other content. Show the ring for keyboard focus (:focus-visible), not necessarily for mouse clicks.
Pressed (active)
Being clicked or touched right now. On touch screens this is the main feedback that a tap registered, so it must appear within about 100 ms.
Disabled
Exists but cannot be used now. Lower contrast is allowed (WCAG exempts inactive controls from contrast rules), but people should be able to find out why.
Loading
The action is in progress. Keep the component's size, show progress, prevent double submission.

Rules that make states consistent

  • One recipe per state across components. Hover always mixes about 10 to 20% of the text color into the background. Focus is always the same 2 px ring in color-focus with a 2 px offset. Pressed always darkens a little more than hover.
  • State never changes size. A loading button keeps its width; an error field does not jump down the page. Layout shifts during interaction feel broken and make people misclick.
  • Never color alone. Error adds an icon and a message; selected adds a checkmark or weight change. This is required for people with color vision deficiencies and helpful for everyone.
  • Disabled is a last resort. A disabled button tells people "no" but not "why". Often it is better to keep the button enabled and explain on press what is missing (see the next chapter).
  • Focus order follows reading order. The state matrix says how focus looks; your layout decides where it goes next.

  • Signal errors with red alone.

Deep dive: the button

Taught by Apple HIG, Material and GOV.UK, with Polaris content guidelines

The button is the smallest complete design problem in software: hierarchy, size, label, states, placement and accessibility in one rectangle. Get it right and the rest of your system inherits the care.

Hierarchy: one primary per view

Buttons come in a ladder of emphasis. The primary button is the action most people want next, filled with your strongest color. There should be at most one per view (a screen, a dialog, a section of a long page). Secondary buttons are alternatives: outlined or tinted. Tertiary or quiet buttons are text-only, for low-stakes options like "Skip" or "Learn more". Destructive is not a level of emphasis but a purpose: it uses the danger color, and is usually not primary, because the most likely action should rarely be destroying something.

Size and hit area

Visible size and touchable size are different numbers. Apple's guidance is a minimum hit target of 44 × 44 points; Material's is 48 × 48 dp. WCAG 2.2's minimum target size criterion (2.5.8, level AA) asks for 24 × 24 CSS pixels or enough spacing around smaller targets. On desktop with a mouse, a compact 28 to 32 px button is fine in toolbars; on touch, use 44 or more. A small icon button can have a visually small glyph inside an invisible, larger hit area.

Labels: verbs that predict the result

A button label should say what will happen, as a verb, in sentence case: "Save note", "Send invite", "Delete 3 photos". Avoid "OK", "Yes", "Submit" and "Confirm", which make people re-read the question to know what they are agreeing to. In a dialog, the confirming button repeats the verb from the title: "Delete draft?" → "Delete". Polaris and GOV.UK both document this; the full craft is in Words.

Loading without jumping

Placement

Where buttons go is a platform convention more than a personal choice (Part five covers this in detail). Some rules hold everywhere: put the primary action where the eye ends its journey through the content (usually the bottom or bottom-right of a form or dialog); keep the destructive action away from the most-pressed spot; on phones, consider a full-width primary button within thumb reach at the bottom of a long form; and keep button order identical across your whole product.

Patterns, and documentation for future-you

Taught by GOV.UK, with Nathan Curtis and Massimo Vignelli

Components are nouns; patterns are sentences made of them. Document both briefly, in the codebase, for the one reader who matters: you, months from now, having forgotten why.

From components to patterns

A pattern is a recurring solution to a user task, assembled from components: "confirm a destructive action" uses a dialog, a title, a body, a destructive button and a cancel button, plus rules about when to use a dialog versus an undo toast. Your product needs a handful of these, and you will reuse them on every new feature. The patterns themselves (forms, validation, empty states, lists, search, undo, onboarding) are taught in Structure & Interaction. Here the point is simpler: write down your version of each, once.

The one-page component doc

Big systems have long documentation sites. You need one page per component, ideally a comment block at the top of the component file or a Markdown file beside it, so it is updated in the same commit as the code. Every page answers the same questions in the same order:

Key idea

The 1970 New York subway standards manual is still readable half a century later because each page shows the rule, the measurements and the example together. Aim for that: rule, numbers, example, reason.

For the solo creator

Documentation that lives in a separate wiki will be out of date within a month. Keep it in the same file as the component, or a Markdown file next to it, and update it in the same commit. When you work with an AI coding assistant, these comment blocks double as instructions it can read, so it uses your components the way you intended (see Designing with AI).

Do

  • Document each component in eight short sections, beside the code.
  • Record decisions with a date and a reason.
  • Write each pattern as a short recipe: when, which components, which rules.
  • Link to the torture page for states.

Don't

  • Build a documentation website for a product with one maintainer.
  • Document what the code already says (prop types) at length.
  • Skip "when not to use"; it prevents most misuse.
  • Leave decisions in your head or in chat history.

Designing in code versus Figma

Taught by the practice of designer-engineers, with Figma, Tailwind and SwiftUI

A solo creator should keep one source of truth, and it should usually be code. Use a drawing tool to explore, then promote what you decide into tokens and components in code, where it can no longer drift.

This is not an argument against Figma or sketching. Drawing tools are faster than code for divergent thinking: trying ten layouts in ten minutes, laying screens side by side, making a flow you can see at once. Code is better for convergent work: real data, real states, real text sizes, real devices, and above all, a system that is impossible to violate because the product is built from it. The question is not which tool, but where each decision finally lives.

One source of truth across platforms

If you ship to more than one platform, put tokens in one neutral file and generate the rest. A workflow that works for one person

  1. Sketch to diverge. Paper or Figma, fast and ugly, using your real tokens if your tool has them. Ten options, not one.
  2. Pick one, build it in code. Use existing components only. If something new is needed, write it as a local, unstyled piece first.
  3. Review on the device. Real data, both themes, largest text size, keyboard, touch.
  4. Promote. If the new piece appears a second or third time, move it into the system: tokens, component, doc block.
  5. Regenerate the design file only if you still use one for exploration.
For the solo creator

Conventions, and Jakob's law

Taught by Jakob Nielsen, with Jon Yablonski and Don Norman

People spend most of their time in other products, so they expect yours to work like those. Every convention you follow is something they already know; every convention you break is something they must learn.

Users spend most of their time on other sites.Jakob Nielsen, on what is now called Jakob's Law of the Internet User Experience, 2000

Nielsen was writing about websites, but the idea generalizes to every platform. Jon Yablonski's Laws of UX made it widely known as "Jakob's law": users prefer your product to work the same way as all the others they already know. Your app is one of perhaps eighty on someone's phone. Their thumb has learned where back is, what a swipe does and which side of a dialog confirms, from all the others. Norman's term for this is a conceptual model people bring with them; conventions are shared conceptual models.

What counts as a convention

A convention is any behavior people predict without being told: tapping a back chevron goes back; ⌘Z undoes; a red button destroys; a blue underlined phrase on the web is a link; swiping left on an iPhone list row reveals actions; settings on a Mac live under the app menu. Conventions live at every level: gestures, shortcuts, placement, iconography, words ("Settings" on most current platforms; older macOS used "Preferences" until 2022), and timing.

When to break one

Break a convention only when all three are true: the convention measurably hurts your core task, your alternative is clearly better for that task, and you can teach the new behavior in the moment it is needed. Loren Brichter's pull-to-refresh in Tweetie 2 (2009) is the classic case of a broken convention that became one; it is famous precisely because such successes are rare. For everything outside your core innovation, be boring. Spend your novelty budget where it matters.

iOS and iPadOS essentials

Taught by Apple's Human Interface Guidelines

iPhone design is built on a few strong structures: navigation stacks you can swipe back through, a tab bar for top-level places, sheets for focused tasks, and text and symbols that scale with the user's settings. Learn these and most screens design themselves.

The essentials, briefly

Navigation bar and large titles
The top bar shows where you are and holds the back button and a few actions. Large titles that collapse as you scroll work well for top-level screens; detail screens usually use inline titles.
Tab bar
Top-level destinations at the bottom, always visible on iPhone. Each tab remembers its own navigation stack. Do not hide the tab bar on drill-down unless the task is immersive.
Sheets and full-screen covers
Sheets present a focused task (compose, edit, filter) with Cancel and Done; detents let a sheet sit at medium height. Full-screen covers are for immersive tasks (a camera, a reader).
Swipe back
A left-edge swipe pops the stack, interactively. People use it constantly; breaking it makes an app feel cheap.
SF Symbols
Apple's icon set of thousands of symbols, designed to align with San Francisco text and to scale and change weight with it. Using them keeps icons consistent with the system and with Dynamic Type.
Dynamic Type
People choose their text size, from smaller than the default (17 pt body) up to accessibility sizes around three times larger. Use text styles, let layouts reflow (stack horizontally arranged items vertically at large sizes), and test at the largest size.
Swipe actions and context menus
Rows can reveal actions on swipe; long-press shows a context menu with a preview. Both are shortcuts, so the same actions must also be reachable another way.
Haptics
System controls give subtle haptic feedback (toggles, pickers). Add your own sparingly, for confirmations that matter (see Motion & Feel).

iPadOS: not a big iPhone

On iPad, the tab bar often becomes a sidebar, and split views show a list and its detail side by side (the classic master–detail layout). People use iPads with keyboards, trackpads and Apple Pencil, so hover effects, keyboard shortcuts and drag and drop matter. Apps also run in resizable windows next to other apps, so your layout must adapt to many widths, not just "iPad portrait" and "iPad landscape" (Chapter 18). Recent iPadOS versions moved further toward Mac-like windowing and added a menu bar, which makes the Mac conventions in the next chapter increasingly relevant on iPad too.

For the solo creator

SwiftUI's NavigationStack, TabView, NavigationSplitView, .sheet with .presentationDetents, SF Symbols and text styles give you nearly all of this for free, including next year's visual changes. Your design system on iOS is mostly tokens, a few ButtonStyles and ViewModifiers, and the discipline not to replace system structures with custom ones.

Do

  • Use a tab bar for 3 to 5 top-level places on iPhone.
  • Keep the left-edge swipe back working everywhere.
  • Use text styles and test at the largest Dynamic Type size.
  • Use SF Symbols for system-like actions.
  • Adapt to a sidebar and split view on iPad.

Don't

  • Hide top-level navigation behind a hamburger.
  • Put custom actions in the top-left corner.
  • Fix text sizes in points that ignore Dynamic Type.
  • Stretch the iPhone layout across an iPad screen.

macOS: the native feel

Taught by Apple's Human Interface Guidelines, with the long tradition of Mac indie developers

A Mac app is a keyboard-and-pointer tool that lives in windows, with every command in the menu bar. "Native feel" is not a look; it is dozens of small behaviors people expect without thinking.

Mac users are famously particular. They notice when ⌘, does not open settings, when a text field does not support the standard emacs-style shortcuts, when a window does not remember its size, or when the menu bar is empty. Each is small. Together they decide whether your app feels like a guest or a resident.

The behaviors that make it feel native

  1. A complete menu bar. Every command your app has should appear in a menu, with its shortcut shown. The menu bar is how Mac users discover features and learn shortcuts, and it is searchable from Help.
  2. Standard shortcuts, untouched. ⌘N new, ⌘O open, ⌘W close window, ⌘Q quit, ⌘, settings, ⌘Z / ⇧⌘Z undo and redo, ⌘F find, ⌘C/⌘V copy and paste. Never reassign them.
  3. Settings in their own window. App menu → Settings…, opened with ⌘,. Usually a small window with a toolbar of tabs. Changes apply immediately; there is no Save button.
  4. Windows that behave. Multiple windows where documents warrant them, remembered size and position, full-screen support, and correct behavior when the last window closes.
  5. Native text. Text fields that support system spelling, standard editing shortcuts, services and the system font panel where relevant. Re-implementing text editing is where most cross-platform apps fail.
  6. Right-click everywhere. Context menus on every object, mirroring commands that also exist in the menu bar.
  7. Density for pointers. A mouse is precise, so controls can be smaller and denser than on touch: regular controls are typically compact compared with iOS.
  8. Drag and drop, in and out. Files, text and images should drag into your app and out to Finder and other apps.
For the solo creator

If you build with SwiftUI, a Settings scene gives you the settings window and the ⌘, menu item; .commands adds your menus; .keyboardShortcut wires shortcuts and shows them in menus. If you build with a web technology (Electron, Tauri), budget explicit time for a real menu bar, standard shortcuts, native context menus and window state; these are the details Mac reviewers mention first.

Do

  • Put every command in the menu bar with its shortcut.
  • Open settings with ⌘, in a separate window; apply changes immediately.
  • Support multiple windows when people work on several things.
  • Use native text controls and context menus.

Don't

  • Ship an iPad layout unchanged on the Mac.
  • Override standard shortcuts.
  • Put a Save button on settings.
  • Use touch-sized controls everywhere on a pointer platform.

Android, Material 3, and Windows briefly

Taught by Material Design 3, with Microsoft Fluent 2

Android has its own grammar: a system back gesture, a bottom navigation bar, a floating action button for the main creative action, and colors that can come from the user's wallpaper. Respect it instead of shipping your iPhone app in disguise.

The essentials

System back
Android has a system-wide back action (a swipe from either edge in gesture navigation, or a button). Your app must handle it correctly at every level: closing a sheet, leaving a screen, or exiting at the root. Predictive back (introduced across Android 13 to 15) shows a preview of where back will go as the user swipes, so your navigation must be honest about its history.
Top app bar
Title, navigation icon (up arrow or menu) and a few actions, with an overflow menu for the rest. It can collapse on scroll.
Navigation bar, rail and drawer
Bottom navigation bar for 3 to 5 destinations on phones; a navigation rail on medium widths (tablets, unfolded foldables); a navigation drawer for many destinations or large screens.
Floating action button (FAB)
One prominent button for the primary creative action of a screen. Not every screen needs one.
Snackbars
Brief messages at the bottom, often with a single action such as "Undo". The Material way to confirm an action without interrupting.
Dynamic color
Since Android 12 (2021), Material You can generate the color scheme from the user's wallpaper. Apps that use Material color roles adapt automatically; you can opt in fully, partially, or keep your brand colors.
Edge to edge
Recent Android versions draw apps behind the status and navigation bars by default (enforced for apps targeting Android 15). Handle window insets as you would iOS safe areas.

Windows and Fluent, briefly

If you ship on Windows, Microsoft's Fluent 2 design system and the WinUI guidance are your HIG. A few conventions matter most: Segoe UI Variable as the system typeface; materials like Mica that tint the window background with the desktop wallpaper; rounded corners (8 px for top-level windows, 4 px for controls in Windows 11); a navigation view with settings at the bottom of the pane; dialogs with the primary button first (left) and Close last; full keyboard access with Alt-key access keys; and respect for the user's contrast theme. Many solo creators reach Windows through a cross-platform framework; the same rule applies as on the Mac: budget time for the keyboard, the title bar and the system's dialog order.

For the solo creator

With Jetpack Compose and Material 3 components, most conventions come built in: Scaffold, TopAppBar, NavigationBar, FloatingActionButton, SnackbarHost and the navigation library's back handling. Your job is to map your tokens onto the Material color scheme and type scale and decide how much of dynamic color to accept. A good compromise for a branded product: keep your accent, let surfaces follow the system.

Do

  • Handle system back correctly at every level.
  • Use a navigation bar on phones and a rail on medium widths.
  • Use snackbars with Undo for reversible actions.
  • Map your tokens onto Material roles.
  • On Windows, follow Fluent's dialog order and keyboard rules.

Don't

  • Put an iOS-style "‹ Previous" back button on Android.
  • Use a FAB for navigation or for minor actions.
  • Ignore insets in edge-to-edge layouts.
  • Ship the same screenshots to the Play Store as to the App Store.

The web, and the small screens: watch and widgets

Taught by Tim Berners-Lee's web, with Ethan Marcotte, Steven Champeon, Alex Russell and Frances Berriman

The web's conventions are older and deeper than any app platform's: URLs, links, the back button, text you can select and zoom. A web product that breaks them feels like a trap. One that honors them feels trustworthy.

URLs are part of the interface

On the web, every meaningful view should have its own URL. That single rule gives people back and forward, bookmarks, sharing, opening in a new tab, and restoring after a crash. Single-page apps often break it by changing the screen without changing the URL. Put the state people would want to return to in the URL: the screen in the path, filters and view modes in the query, a position in the hash.

The web's other conventions

  • Links navigate, buttons act. Use <a href> for anything that goes somewhere, so ⌘/Ctrl-click and middle-click open new tabs. Use <button> for actions. Do not make clickable divs.
  • Text is text. People select, copy, translate, search with ⌘/Ctrl-F, and zoom. Do not render text as images or disable selection on content.
  • Responsive by default. Ethan Marcotte's "Responsive Web Design" (A List Apart, 2010) established fluid grids, flexible images and media queries. Today container queries let components respond to their own space too (Chapter 18).
  • Progressive enhancement. A term coined by Steven Champeon in 2003: start with HTML that works, then layer CSS and JavaScript on top. A form that submits without JavaScript, links that work before the app loads. It makes products sturdier on bad networks and old devices.
  • Respect the browser's settings. Use rem units so the user's default font size applies; never block pinch zoom; honor prefers-color-scheme, prefers-reduced-motion and prefers-contrast.
  • Progressive web apps. The term was coined in 2015 by Alex Russell and Frances Berriman for web apps that can be installed, work offline (via service workers) and send notifications. With a manifest and a service worker, your web product can live on a home screen or dock; on iOS, home screen web apps gained web push in iOS 16.4 (2023). A PWA should still behave like the web inside: URLs and all.

The small screens: watch and widgets

Watch apps and widgets are not small versions of your app. They are glances: one piece of information or one action, available in a second or two. Apple's guidance for watchOS emphasizes brief interactions; widgets on iOS, iPadOS, macOS and Android show timely content and open the app (or, since iOS 17, run a simple action with a button or toggle) when tapped.

For the solo creator

The web is the cheapest platform to ship and the easiest to get subtly wrong. Use a router that maps URLs to screens from day one, semantic HTML for links and buttons, and a native <dialog> or a proven accessible library for overlays. Only add a watch app or widgets when you can name the one glance they answer; a single excellent widget beats three mediocre ones.

Do

  • Give every meaningful view a URL; put view state in the query.
  • Use real links and buttons.
  • Build with progressive enhancement and rem units.
  • Design widgets and watch views as single glances.

Don't

  • Hijack the back button or scrolling.
  • Disable zoom or text selection on content.
  • Make the PWA behave like a closed native app.
  • Squeeze your whole app into a widget.

In the wild

  • Linear and GitHub URLsLinear; GitHub
    Issues have short, stable, readable URLs that people paste into chat and documents. The URL is a product feature. A good URL is a shareable unit of your product.
  • GOV.UKGovernment Digital Service
    Built with progressive enhancement: pages and forms work before or without JavaScript, which matters for people on old phones and poor connections. Sturdy beats clever when everyone must be able to use it.
  • Apple Fitness ringsApple Watch, 2015–today
    Three rings readable in under a second, the canonical example of a glanceable complication. The smaller the screen, the fewer the questions.

Responsive and adaptive: sizes and inputs

Taught by Ethan Marcotte, with Material window size classes and Apple size classes

Design for ranges, not devices: a layout that works from 320 to 1,600 points and reflows at a few meaningful widths. And design for inputs, not screens: a finger, a mouse, a keyboard and a pen ask for different things on the same screen size.

Breakpoints from content, size classes from platforms

A breakpoint is a width where the layout changes. Do not choose them from device lists, which change every year; choose them where your content breaks: when a line of text becomes too long (more than about 75 characters, see Typography), when two columns become cramped, when a sidebar crowds the content. Platforms give you named ranges to anchor on. Material defines window size classes: compact (under 600 dp wide, most phones in portrait), medium (600 to 839 dp, tablets in portrait and many unfolded foldables) and expanded (840 dp and up, tablets in landscape and desktops), with larger classes added for very wide windows. Apple uses size classes, compact or regular in each dimension.

Lab: resize the window, change the pointer
Drag the width through 600 and 840 to watch the navigation move from a bottom bar to a rail to a sidebar, and the detail view appear beside the list. Then switch the input: with a fine pointer, rows get denser and a hover hint appears (hover over a row); with touch, rows grow to 44-point targets and nothing depends on hover. Width and input are independent: an iPad with a trackpad is wide and fine; a phone in landscape is wide-ish and coarse.

Input is a separate axis

Screen size does not tell you how someone is pointing. A 13-inch touchscreen laptop, an iPad with a Magic Keyboard and a phone with a Bluetooth mouse all break the "small means touch" assumption. On the web, query the input directly: @media (hover: hover) and (pointer: fine) for precise pointers, (pointer: coarse) for touch. On native platforms, use the system's controls, which already adapt (iPadOS shows pointer effects, macOS uses denser controls).

Layout shifts, desktop windows and foldables

  • Reserve space. Images without dimensions, late-loading banners and fonts that swap cause content to jump while people are reading or about to tap. Give media an aspect ratio, reserve space for async content, and keep component sizes stable across states (Chapter 8).
  • Desktop windows are any size. People tile windows side by side, so your Mac or web app may be 500 points wide on a 5K display. Treat window width, not screen size, as the input, and define a sensible minimum window size.
  • Container queries. Supported in all major browsers since 2023, they let a component respond to the width of its own container. A card can switch from horizontal to stacked whether it lives in a sidebar or a main column.
  • Foldables. A device can change size while your app is open: folded phone, unfolded tablet, half-open "tabletop" posture with a hinge across the middle. Preserve state through the change (the scroll position and the open note must survive), avoid putting critical content across the hinge, and use the medium and expanded layouts you already have.
For the solo creator

Write your three layouts (compact, medium, expanded) as the only breakpoints in your token file, and make components fluid inside them with container queries or flexible stacks. Test with real window resizing, not just device presets: drag the browser or Mac window slowly from narrowest to widest and watch for the moment anything looks wrong. That moment is a breakpoint or a bug.

Do

  • Pick breakpoints where your content breaks; anchor on platform size classes.
  • Treat input type as independent from screen size.
  • Reserve space to prevent layout shifts.
  • Preserve state across resizes and folds.

Don't

  • Design for device names.
  • Gate actions behind hover.
  • Assume desktop means a big window.
  • Stretch a phone layout to 1,400 points wide.

    Switches from a single list with a bottom bar on phones to a navigation rail and list-detail layout on tablets and unfolded foldables. The same app, three canonical layouts.
  • The Boston Globe responsive site2011, with Ethan Marcotte and Filament Group
    One of the first major responsive news sites, often cited as proof that a single fluid design could serve phones to desktops. Responsive design was a design decision before it was a technique.

Design debt, and keeping a system alive for years

Taught by Brad Frost (the interface inventory), with semantic versioning and the GOV.UK team

Every shortcut you take in the interface is a loan. Design debt compounds quietly until a simple change takes a week. Pay it down on a schedule: inventory, consolidate, version, and deprecate gently.

Engineers know technical debt: code that works but makes future changes expensive. Design debt is its twin: the third gray nobody chose, the dialog that puts the buttons on the other side, the screen that uses its own spacing, the component with a customColor prop. Each one was a reasonable choice under time pressure. The trouble is interest. Every inconsistency makes the next change slower (which version do I follow?), makes theming leakier, and teaches users that your product is unpredictable.

The interface inventory

In 2013 Brad Frost described the interface inventory: screenshot every instance of every kind of UI element in your product and paste them into one document, grouped by type. All buttons together. All headings. All form fields. All dialogs. All icons. The power is in the side-by-side view. Inconsistencies that are invisible across fifty screens become embarrassing on one page, and the embarrassment is useful: it creates the motivation to consolidate.

Running an inventory alone, in one afternoon

  1. Capture. Walk through every screen in both themes and screenshot each instance of: buttons, text styles, form fields, icons, dialogs and sheets, empty states, colors (use a color picker on screenshots).
  2. Group. Paste into one canvas or document, one row per type. Do not judge yet.
  3. Count. Write the count of distinct variants per row: "buttons: 10 styles".
  4. Decide. For each row, choose the keepers (usually the ones closest to your tokens) and map every other instance to one of them.
  5. Consolidate. Replace instances in code with the system component. Do the most-used screens first.
  6. Prevent. Add a search, lint rule or code review habit that catches new literals and one-off styles.

Versioning your system

Even for one person, a version number on your system is useful because it marks moments of change you can look back on, and it lets you migrate gradually. Use the familiar rules of semantic versioning (major.minor.patch, from semver.org): a patch fixes something without changing how you use it (a contrast tweak); a minor version adds something backward-compatible (a new component, a new token); a major version breaks something (a token renamed or removed, a component API changed).

Wrap-up

Ten things to remember

  1. A design system is decisions made once and encoded in code. For one person, its value is speed, consistency and saved attention.
  2. Start small: tokens, about a dozen components, a handful of patterns, and voice. Extract from real screens; never build for imaginary ones.
  3. Atomic design is a way of seeing parts and wholes. Keep the page stage as a stress test; organize components by purpose, not by chemistry.
  4. Tokens come in layers: primitives (values), semantic (purposes), and optionally component tokens. Screens read only purposes.
  5. Names outlive values. No hues in semantic names; order name parts from general to specific.
  6. A theme is a new mapping from semantic tokens to primitives. Dark mode is not inversion; high contrast is a real theme.
  7. A component is finished only when its anatomy, API, all seven states, sizes, content rules and accessibility are decided and written down beside the code.
  8. Keep one source of truth, almost always code. Drawing tools are for exploring, not for specifying.
  9. Follow each platform's conventions for behavior; share brand, words and the data model. Adapt, don't copy.
  10. Design for width ranges and input types, not devices, and pay down design debt a little every month.

  • Atomic Design
    Brad Frost, 2016 (free to read online) · The model in full, plus the interface inventory and how to keep a pattern library alive. Read chapters 1, 2 and 4.
  • Design Systems
    Alla Kholmatova, Smashing Magazine, 2017 · The best book on the human side of systems: shared language, functional versus perceptual patterns, strict versus loose systems.
  • EightShapes essays on tokens and systems
    Nathan Curtis, Medium, 2016–today · Especially "Naming Tokens in Design Systems". The most precise thinking available on token architecture.
  • Expressive Design Systems
    Yesenia Perez-Cruz, A Book Apart, 2019 · How to keep a system from flattening your product's personality.
  • Refactoring UI
    Adam Wathan and Steve Schoger, 2018 · Practical, visual advice on scales for spacing, type, color and shadow, from the makers of Tailwind.
  • Apple Human Interface Guidelines
    Apple, continuously updated · Read the pages for navigation, sheets, alerts, menus, typography and the platform considerations for each device you ship.
  • Material Design 3
    Google, continuously updated · Read color roles, window size classes and canonical layouts, even if you never ship on Android.

    Government Digital Service · The model for documenting components and patterns with research and "when not to use".

    Jon Yablonski, O'Reilly, 2020 (and the website) · Jakob's law and its relatives, with the psychology behind them.

    Ethan Marcotte, A Book Apart, 2011 (from his 2010 A List Apart article) · The origin of designing for ranges instead of devices.

    Jeremy Keith, 2016 (free online) · Progressive enhancement and the web's long history of sturdy conventions.
