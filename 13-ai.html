Designing with AI. Probabilistic tools that people can trust, steer and switch off.

Every app you use is getting an AI feature, and most of them are a chat box bolted to the side. This guide teaches you to do better: to decide when a model is the right material at all, to place it inside the work instead of beside it, and to design for the moments when it is slow, wrong or overconfident. It ends with the other half of the topic: how a one-person studio can use AI to design faster without designing blander.

Warm-up

You already have strong intuitions about AI features, because you use them daily: autocomplete in your editor, smart replies in mail, a chat assistant in a browser tab. Some of those features feel like magic and some feel like a nag. Before reading, answer three quick questions. Do not look anything up. The point is to notice what you already believe so the chapters can confirm or correct it.

Diagnostic 1

A note-taking app adds "Summarize with AI". In testing, summaries are right about 90% of the time. What is the most important design question left?

At 90% accuracy, a person who summarizes five notes a day meets a wrong summary every other day. Accuracy work matters, but the design job is making errors cheap to notice and cheap to fix. That is the theme of Parts 1 and 3.
Diagnostic 2

Your expense app needs to convert "₹1,250.00" typed in a field into a number. A friend suggests sending the text to a language model. What do you do?

When a deterministic rule can do the job, it wins on speed, cost, privacy and predictability. Models earn their place when the input is genuinely open-ended. Chapter 3 gives you a test for this.
Diagnostic 3

An AI feature rewrites a 600-word paragraph. Which presentation lets the user trust the result fastest?

People can only trust what they can check. A diff turns "read 600 words again" into "review six changes". Chapter 11 has a working diff you can try.
How to study this guide

Read one Part per sitting. Each chapter has a demo, a visual or a mockup: do not skim past them, they carry half the teaching. Part one

A new material

A language model is not a smarter database. It is a material with different properties: it bends where code is rigid and breaks where code is reliable. Part one is about those properties, the research that already tells us how to design for them, and the first decision you make with any AI feature: whether to use it, and how much to let it do.

1

What changes when the system is probabilistic

Taught by Google PAIR, with Saleema Amershi and Lisanne Bainbridge

Traditional software does the same thing every time; AI features do something plausible every time. Designing for AI means designing for variation, for error as a normal state, and for time and money spent on every call.

Everything you learned about interaction design assumes a deterministic machine. Press Save and the file is saved. Type 2+2 and you get 4. The user forms a mental model once (see The Human Mind) and the system keeps honouring it. A language model breaks four of those assumptions at once, and each broken assumption becomes a design problem.

Property 1: the same input can give different outputs

Ask a model to summarize the same note twice and you can get two different summaries. Sampling settings can reduce this, and structured output can constrain it, but you should design as if variation is always possible. The consequence is that users cannot build a stable mental model from one try. They learn the feature as a distribution: "it is usually good at this, sometimes odd at that." Your interface has to help them learn the shape of that distribution quickly, and has to make a second try cheap.

Property 2: errors are normal, not exceptional

In classic software an error is a bug, and you fix it. In an AI feature, a wrong answer is a known rate: 3%, 10%, 25% depending on the task. You can push the rate down, but not to zero, and the errors are not flagged. A hallucinated date looks exactly like a real one. So the design job moves from "prevent errors" to "make errors visible, cheap and recoverable". Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 paper Ironies of Automation warned about the same thing in factories and cockpits: the more reliable automation gets, the less practised and attentive the human who must catch its rare failures.

Property 3: it is slow

A database lookup returns in milliseconds. A model call typically takes from under a second for a short completion to tens of seconds for a long answer, and minutes for a multi-step agent. That puts most AI features above the 1-second line where people lose the feeling of direct manipulation, and many above the 10-second line where they lose attention (the classic Nielsen response-time limits; see Motion & Feel for perceived speed). Latency is not an engineering detail you fix later. It shapes which patterns are even possible: ghost text needs a fast, small model; a research report can take a minute if it runs in the background.

Property 4: every call costs money

A button in normal software is free to press a thousand times. An AI button has a marginal cost per press, paid by you, in tokens. A feature that runs automatically on every keystroke or every page load can cost a hundred times more than the same feature run on request. As a solo creator on a ₹500 or $8 monthly plan, this is a design constraint as real as screen size. Chapter 16 turns it into a calculator.

Key idea

With AI, the happy path is the easy part. Design the wrong path, the slow path and the expensive path first.

Do

  • Write down the expected error rate of the feature before you design it.
  • Label generated content as generated, at the point of use.
  • Make a second try one click away.
  • Decide early which calls run on request and which run automatically, because that decides cost.

Don't

  • Present model output with the same visual authority as data the user entered.
  • Design only from your best demo outputs.
  • Assume latency will "get fixed" by faster models later.
  • Run expensive calls on page load "in case" the user wants them.

For the solo creator

You are also the support desk. Every unflagged AI error becomes an email you answer. Ten minutes spent designing a "this looks wrong" path saves hours of support later, and it is the cheapest form of quality control you can buy.

Common mistakes

Designing from the demo

You test with three inputs that work beautifully and design the screen around them.

Run the feature on 30 real inputs before designing. Print the worst five and design for those.

Hiding the probabilistic nature

You want the feature to feel polished, so you remove every hint that it can be wrong.

Polish the recovery path instead. A visible "Edit" and a source link feel more professional than false certainty.

Ignoring cost until the bill arrives

During development you are the only user and calls feel free.

Multiply calls per user per day by your expected users and by price per call, on day one. See Chapter 16.

Check yourself

Your reading app extracts the author and date from saved articles using a model. It is right 95% of the time. Users never edit these fields. What does that most likely mean?

Silence is not the same as correctness. With fluent errors, people do not notice, and if correction is hidden they cannot act even when they do. Look at a sample of real outputs and test whether a user can find the edit control in five seconds.

The rulebooks that already exist

Taught by Saleema Amershi et al. (Microsoft), with Google PAIR and Apple's Human Interface Guidelines

You do not need to invent AI design from scratch. Three free, well-researched sources already cover most of it: Microsoft's 18 Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction, Google's People + AI Guidebook, and Apple's machine learning guidance. Learn their shape, then use them as checklists.

Much of the AI design advice you will read online in 2026 is a rediscovery of work published in 2019. That year a Microsoft Research team led by Saleema Amershi published Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction at CHI, the main academic conference on human-computer interaction. They collected more than 150 recommendations from two decades of research and industry, distilled them to 18, and tested them against 20 real AI products with 49 designers. The same year Google's People + AI Research group (PAIR) released the People + AI Guidebook, a practical workbook organized around the product process. Apple's Human Interface Guidelines have long had a section on machine learning, covering feedback, corrections, confidence and limitations.

These were written before large language models went mainstream, and that is exactly why they are valuable: they describe the human side, which has not changed. People still need to know what a system can do, how well it does it, how to correct it and how to turn it off.

Microsoft's 18 guidelines, by moment

The guidelines are grouped by when they apply in the user's relationship with the feature: at first contact, during each interaction, when the system is wrong, and over time. That grouping is the useful part. It reminds you that a feature has a life, not just a screen.

Six guidelines you will use every week, drawn

Eighteen is a lot to hold in your head. These six do most of the work in small products. Here they are as before-and-after mockups.

The other three you will meet constantly: G9 efficient correction (edit the output in place, do not re-prompt from scratch; Chapter 11), G15 granular feedback (a thumbs-down that asks "what was wrong?" in one tap; Chapter 13) and G17 global controls (one setting to turn the feature off or change how it behaves everywhere; Chapter 14).

Google's People + AI Guidebook

Where Microsoft gives you promises to keep, PAIR gives you questions to work through, in six chapters that follow the product process:

  1. User needs and defining success. Is AI adding unique value here? Should it automate or augment? What does a good outcome look like, and what is the cost of each kind of error?
  2. Data collection and evaluation. What data shapes the behavior, and how will you know if it is good?
  3. Mental models. What will users believe the system can do, and how do you set that belief accurately from the start?
  4. Explainability and trust. How much explanation does a user need to calibrate trust, not maximize it?
  5. Feedback and control. How do users teach and steer the system, and what do they get back for it?
  6. Errors and graceful failure. What kinds of failure exist, and how does the user recover from each?

Two PAIR ideas are worth stealing outright. First, calibrated trust: the goal is not that users trust the AI as much as possible, but that their trust matches its actual reliability, high where it is good and low where it is weak. Second, the cost of false positives versus false negatives: a spam filter that hides a real job offer (false positive) is worse than one that lets a spam mail through (false negative). Decide which error is worse for your feature, then tune and design for that.

Apple's machine learning guidance

Apple's HIG treats machine learning as a set of decisions about the role of the feature (is it critical or complementary? proactive or reactive? visible or invisible?) and about inputs and outputs. On the input side it distinguishes explicit feedback (the user tells you, like a "Not interested" button) from implicit feedback (you infer from behavior, like skips), and asks you to be careful with implicit signals because people did not knowingly give them. On the output side it covers showing multiple options, confidence, attribution ("Because you read…") and limitations. The tone is typical Apple: quiet, low-drama features that blend into the task. Its newer guidance on generative AI adds expectations about transparency and responsible content. Read the current pages directly; they change between OS releases.

  • Use the 18 guidelines as a review checklist for every AI feature before release.
  • Decide which error type (false positive or false negative) hurts more and design for it.
  • Aim for calibrated trust, and say so in your design notes.

Don't

  • Treat these sources as outdated because they predate chat models.
  • Check off a guideline because "the user could figure it out".
  • Treat implicit behavior as consent for the system to learn anything it likes.

Common mistakes

Reading the guidelines once and filing them away

They feel obvious when you read them, so they never become a habit.

Turn the 18 into a review ritual: before shipping any AI change, walk the four boxes and write one line per guideline, even if the line is "not applicable because…".

Explaining to maximize trust

Explanations feel like a trust-building tool, so you add long, reassuring ones.

Explain to calibrate. Short, checkable reasons ("Because you starred 3 posts from this site") beat long, unverifiable ones ("Our advanced AI analyzed your preferences").

Check yourself

Your app auto-tags bookmarks with topics. Users complain the tags are "random". Which guideline would you apply first?

"Random" is a symptom of an unexplained system. A short reason either reveals a sensible pattern (trust goes up) or reveals a bad one (the user can correct it, and you learn). Friendlier names do not touch the problem.

3

When to use AI, and when not to

Taught by Google PAIR, with Ben Shneiderman and the jobs-to-be-done tradition

Start from the problem, not the model. Use AI where the input is open-ended and an imperfect answer is still useful; use plain code wherever a rule can do the job, because rules are instant, free, private and always right in the same way.

There is pressure in 2026 to add AI to everything. Investors ask about it, competitors announce it, and it is genuinely fun to build. But the best AI features in shipping products are narrow, and most of the best features in any product are not AI at all. iA Writer's focus mode, Things' Quick Entry and Linear's keyboard shortcuts are deterministic, and they are loved for being predictable. Before you reach for a model, run the problem through a short test.

Good fits and poor fits

Models are strong at turning messy, unstructured input into something structured (a pasted receipt into fields), at producing a first draft people will edit (a reply, a summary), at matching meaning rather than exact words (search that finds "invoice" when you typed "bill"), and at transforming text between forms (shorter, friendlier, translated). They are poor at exact arithmetic over many numbers, at facts that must be correct with no checking step, at tasks where a person's own voice is the point, and at anything where the rule is known and simple.

Automation versus augmentation

PAIR's guidebook asks a sharp question: should this feature automate the task (do it so the user does not have to), or augment it (help the user do it better)? People want automation for tasks that are boring, repetitive or that they cannot do well, such as filing receipts or transcribing audio. They want augmentation for tasks they enjoy, feel responsible for, or that express who they are, such as writing, designing or choosing what to read. Ben Shneiderman's Human-Centered AI (2022) adds that the two are not one slider: the best systems give high automation and high human control at the same time, like a modern camera that auto-focuses and still lets you pick the subject.

Myth

"AI features differentiate my product." A feature that every competitor can add with one API call is not a moat. What differentiates is your understanding of a specific job, and the craft of fitting AI into it so well that nobody notices the AI.

Do

  • Write the problem statement without the word "AI" first.
  • Try a rule-based version and measure where it fails; use AI only for those cases.
  • Ask users how they feel about the task: chore or craft?
  • Prefer hybrid designs: AI for interpretation, code for everything after.

Don't

  • Start from "what can we do with this model?"
  • Automate tasks people enjoy or feel responsible for.
  • Use a model for arithmetic, sorting, validation or anything with a known rule.
  • Add a sparkle button to signal modernity.

For the solo creator

Every AI feature you ship becomes a dependency on a vendor's pricing, rate limits and model changes. A deterministic feature you write once keeps working for a decade. Spend your AI budget on the one or two places where open-ended input is the core of the job, and keep the rest boring.

Common mistakes

The solution looking for a problem

The model is exciting, so you look for places to put it.

List your users' top five frustrations first. Only then ask whether any of them has open-ended input.

Automating the craft

A task looks slow, so you automate it, not noticing that users value doing it.

Interview or observe three users doing the task. If they talk about it with pride, augment rather than automate.

Skipping the checking-cost question

The output looks impressive, so you assume it saves time.

Time a user checking the AI output versus doing the task. If checking is not at least twice as fast, rethink the feature.

Check yourself

Streaks are arithmetic (code). Writing the reflection removes the value of reflecting. Choosing habits for someone is high-stakes and personal. Parsing a casual sentence into checkmarks is open-ended input, easy to verify, and cheap to fix.

Levels of autonomy

Taught by Thomas Sheridan and Raja Parasuraman, with Ben Shneiderman

"How much should the AI do on its own?" is a dial, not a switch. Four settings cover most products: suggest, draft, act with approval, act and report. Pick the level from the cost of a mistake and the ease of undoing it, and let it rise only as trust is earned.

Automation researchers have studied this question for decades. Sheridan and Verplank's 1978 scale described ten levels of automation, from "the computer offers no help" to "the computer decides everything and ignores the human". Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens (2000) added that automation can sit at different levels for different stages: gathering information, analyzing it, deciding, and acting. For software products you can simplify this to four useful levels.

How to choose the level

Two questions decide it. How bad is a wrong action? And how easy is it to undo? A spam filter can act on its own because a misfiled email is mildly bad and fully reversible (it is in the spam folder). Sending an email on the user's behalf is moderately bad and irreversible: once sent, it is gone. Paying an invoice is severe and irreversible. Map your action on these two axes and the level follows.

That last point is the most practical trick in the chapter. You can often raise autonomy safely by making an action more reversible. Gmail's "Undo send" holds a message for a few seconds before it really leaves. A soft delete with a 30-day trash makes deletion reversible. Once an action is reversible, the system can be allowed to do it with less ceremony.

Let autonomy rise with trust

A good pattern is to start a feature one level lower than you think it can handle and let users raise it. Email clients that learn filing rules often begin by suggesting a folder ("Move to Receipts?") and only offer "Always do this" after the user has accepted the suggestion a few times. That is Microsoft's G13 and G14: learn from behavior, adapt cautiously. The setting should be per action type, not global, because a user may happily let the AI file receipts while never letting it reply to clients.

Watch out

Higher autonomy shifts the user from doer to supervisor, and supervising is hard. Bainbridge's irony applies: if the AI is right 98% of the time, people stop checking, and the 2% slips through. At levels 3 and 4, make the review step fast and specific (show the diff, highlight the unusual bits) instead of asking for general vigilance.

Do

  • Choose the level per action, from severity and reversibility.
  • Make actions reversible to unlock higher autonomy safely.
  • Offer "Always do this" only after repeated accepted suggestions.
  • At level 4, provide an activity log with undo on every entry.

Don't

  • Use one global "AI autonomy" setting for unrelated actions.
  • Let an AI take irreversible actions without explicit approval.
  • Ask for approval so often that people click "Yes" without reading.
  • Hide what the system did on its own.

For the solo creator

Level 4 features generate support load when they go wrong ("your app deleted my stuff"). Ship at level 2 or 3, log what users accept, and only add an "always do this" option once your logs show a high acceptance rate for that specific action.

Common mistakes

Jumping to agentic

Agents are fashionable, so the first version acts on its own.

Ship the draft version first. If users accept 95% of drafts unchanged, you have evidence for more autonomy.

Approval fatigue

You ask for confirmation on everything to be safe.

Ask only for irreversible or unusual actions. Batch the rest into one reviewable summary.

No log at level 4

The point of automation is not having to look, so you show nothing.

Keep a quiet, searchable log of every automatic action with undo. Users rarely open it, but knowing it exists is what lets them relax.

Check yourself

Your invoicing app could let AI send payment reminders to clients automatically. Users like the idea but worry. What is the best first version?

An email to a client is permanent and affects a relationship: top-right quadrant. Drafting removes most of the effort while the human keeps the send. Per-client automation can come later, earned by evidence. A modal at login creates approval fatigue.

The chat box is not the interface

Taught by Amelia Wattenberger, with Maggie Appleton and Jakob Nielsen

A chat box asks the user to describe their work instead of doing it. For most tasks inside a product, AI belongs in the work itself: in the text, on the selection, in the field, where the context already is and the result lands without copying.

Chat became the default AI interface by accident: it was the simplest way to expose a general-purpose model to the public in 2022. It is great for open exploration, for questions you cannot phrase as a button, and for learning what a model can do. But as a feature inside a product, chat has three costs. In her 2023 essay Why Chatbots Are Not the Future, Amelia Wattenberger argued that a text box gives no hint of what the system can do, puts the whole burden of expression on the user, and pulls them out of the work they were doing. Maggie Appleton made a similar case in her language model sketches the same year, sketching interfaces where AI appears as margins, lenses and annotations instead of a conversation.

Jakob Nielsen framed the deeper shift: classic software is command-based (you tell the computer how), while AI is intent-based (you tell it what outcome you want). Intent is powerful, but writing intent in prose is hard. Many people are not good at articulating what they want in words, and they should not have to be in order to use your product.

A catalogue of interaction patterns

Taught by shipping products, 2018–2026, with Emily Campbell's Shape of AI collection

Most good AI features are one of about eight patterns. Each has a natural latency budget, a tolerated error rate and a place in the interface. Choose the pattern from the task, then tune the details.

Patterns are useful because users already know them. Once someone has learned that grey text means "press Tab to take this", every product that uses ghost text gets that knowledge for free. Inventing a new AI interaction is expensive for you and for users; reach for a known pattern first.

Two more patterns get their own chapters: agents with plans and checkpoints (Chapter 7) and chat (Chapter 5). And one quiet pattern deserves a mention: ambient AI, where a model improves something without any UI at all, such as better search ranking, smarter defaults or auto-cropped thumbnails. It needs no sparkle; it needs a way to override it.

Pattern by pattern: the details that matter

Use for: continuing what the user is already producing: code, email, short form fields. Details: show only when confident; never more than a line or two at a time in prose; accept the whole suggestion with Tab and offer word-by-word acceptance where it helps (some editors use Ctrl or Cmd plus the right arrow); typing matching characters should "type through" the suggestion; typing different characters should kill it instantly. Debounce: wait until the user pauses for a beat (often 150 to 300 ms) so suggestions do not flicker during fast typing. Accessibility: suggestions must be announced to screen readers politely, and there must be a setting to turn them off.

Use for: rewriting, summarizing, translating, changing tone of a selected chunk. Details: keep the verb list short (four to seven) and concrete ("Shorter", not "Improve"); show the result as a diff or side-by-side preview, never a silent replacement; offer "Try again" and "Insert below" as well as "Replace"; keep the original reachable through undo. Apple's Writing Tools in iOS 18 and macOS Sequoia follow this pattern: select text, choose Proofread, Rewrite, a tone, or a summary form, and review before keeping it.

Use for: finding and running features by describing them, filtering and querying data in plain language. Details: deterministic matches always come first and instantly; the AI interpretation appears as one row below, labeled as AI; when the AI maps words to a command, show the command it built ("Filter: Done · older than 7 days") so the user learns the real feature and can edit it. Raycast is a good model: its root search lets you hand a query to AI, while keeping normal commands instant.

Use for: creative generation inside a canvas: images, layouts, slides, illustrations. Details: generate several options (three is a common number) because the user is choosing, not accepting; put results on a separate, non-destructive layer; keep the prompt editable next to the result; mark generated content with provenance metadata where your platform supports it. Adobe's Generative Fill in Photoshop (2023) set the template: select an area, optionally type a prompt, get variations on a new generative layer.

Use for: turning messy input (a pasted email, a receipt photo, a meeting transcript) into your product's own data. Details: ask the model for a fixed schema (JSON with named fields) so the result renders in your normal components; validate it with code before showing it; mark low-confidence fields (Chapter 10); never save without a review step for important records. This is the most underrated AI pattern, because the output looks like the rest of your product and users already know how to edit it.

Use for: hands-busy or eyes-busy contexts (driving, cooking, walking), quick capture, accessibility. Details: show a live transcript so people can see what was heard; show the interpretation separately from the words; make both editable; confirm before acting on anything irreversible; handle accents, code-switching (Hinglish is a real test) and background noise gracefully, and offer to type instead. Never make voice the only way in.

Agents: plans, checkpoints and permissions

Taught by coding agents and research agents of 2024–2026, with Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens

An agent is an AI that takes several steps on its own toward a goal. Design it like delegating to a new assistant: agree on a plan first, set what it may do without asking, check in at risky moments, and leave a clear record of what it did.

Agents are the most autonomous pattern and the hardest to design. They run for minutes, touch real things (files, calendars, inboxes, code), and fail in new ways: they can go confidently in the wrong direction for ten steps before anyone notices. The design job is to keep the human oriented and in control without making them watch every step.

The four surfaces of an agent

  1. The brief. Where the user states the goal. Help them be specific with fields, examples or a short clarifying question from the agent before it starts.
  2. The plan. A visible list of intended steps the user can read, edit, reorder or reject before work begins. A plan is the cheapest place to catch a misunderstanding.
  3. The run. Progress through the plan, with the current step, what has been done, and checkpoints where the agent pauses for approval before risky or irreversible actions.
  4. The report. What was done, what changed (as a diff), what failed, what needs attention, with undo or rollback where possible.

Permissions: scope, not just yes or no

Before an agent acts, users need to understand what it is allowed to touch. Good permission design borrows from operating systems: name the resource, the action, and the duration. "Allow once", "Allow for this task" and "Always allow for this folder" are different promises, and the user should choose. Destructive or external actions (delete, send, pay, publish) deserve their own prompts even when other actions are pre-approved.

Interrupting and steering

Long runs need a way in. Users should be able to pause, stop, and add guidance mid-run ("skip the newsletters") without starting over. Stopping must be safe: the agent finishes or rolls back the current atomic step and reports where it stopped. If the agent hits ambiguity, it should ask a short, specific question with options instead of guessing, and keep working on unrelated steps while it waits.

Watch out

Agents that read untrusted content (web pages, emails, documents from others) can be manipulated by instructions hidden in that content, an attack known as prompt injection. As a designer, your defence is structural: keep high-risk actions behind explicit approval, show the user exactly what will be sent or changed, and never let content the agent read silently widen its permissions.

Do

  • Show a plan before running, and let users edit it.
  • Put checkpoints before irreversible or external actions.
  • Name action, resource and duration in every permission request.
  • Run long tasks in the background with notifications at checkpoints and at the end.
  • End with a report: done, changed, failed, needs you.

Don't

  • Show a spinner for three minutes and then a wall of results.
  • Ask for broad permissions up front "to be safe".
  • Let Stop leave things half-changed with no report.
  • Let content the agent reads change what it is allowed to do.

In the wild

  • Claude CodeAnthropic, 2025
    A terminal coding agent that can show a plan, keeps a visible to-do list as it works, asks before running commands or editing files unless the user has allowed them, and lets the user interrupt to redirect. Plan, progress, permissions and interruption as first-class UI.
  • Deep research modesOpenAI, Google, Perplexity, 2024–2025
    Several assistants added research modes that may ask clarifying questions first, then work for minutes and return a cited report. Clarify the brief before a long run; make the wait worth it with sources.
  • LinearLinear, 2025
    Lets AI agents be assigned issues in the same way as teammates, so their work shows up in the familiar issue, comment and activity views. Agents fit best when their work flows through surfaces people already review.
For the solo creator

You probably do not need a general agent. A "batch job with a preview" covers most cases: the AI proposes all changes at once, you show them as a reviewable list, the user approves some or all, and code applies them. That is an agent's plan-and-report without the open-ended loop, and it is far easier to build and support.

Common mistakes

Invisible progress

The agent works in a loop and the UI shows only "Thinking…".

Render the plan as a checklist with states. Even rough steps ("Reading 214 articles") reduce anxiety and let users catch wrong turns.

Checkpoint everywhere

You add approvals to every step to feel safe.

Checkpoint only irreversible, external or unusual actions. Batch the rest into the final report.

No report

The agent's job is "done", so the UI just closes.

End every run with a summary that lists changes, failures and anything left for the user, with undo.

Check yourself

Your agent cleans up a user's calendar. Halfway through, it finds two events it is unsure whether to delete. What should it do?

Ambiguity on a destructive action calls for a checkpoint, not a guess. Blocking the whole run wastes the user's time, and silent skipping leaves them with an inaccurate picture of what was done.

Setting expectations

Taught by Google PAIR (mental models), with Joseph Weizenbaum and Don Norman

People decide how to use an AI feature in their first few tries. Show them what it is for, what it is bad at, and what good input looks like, through examples rather than promises, and keep its personality modest so they judge it as a tool.

Norman's idea of the conceptual model (see The Human Mind) matters more with AI, because the feature's abilities are invisible. A button labeled "Save" explains itself. A text box with a sparkle explains nothing. Users fill the gap with whatever they have heard about AI: either that it is magic (and then they over-trust it) or that it is useless (and then they never try it twice). Your job is to replace both myths with an accurate, specific picture.

Three tools for setting the model

Show, do not tell. One concrete example of input and output teaches more than a paragraph of capability claims. Prompt starters (clickable example requests) do double duty: they teach scope and lower the blank-box anxiety. Make them specific to the user's own data when you can ("Summarize the 3 notes you edited today" beats "Summarize notes").

State the limits plainly. Microsoft's G2 asks you to make clear how well the system does what it does. A short line under an input ("Works best with English meeting notes under 1 hour. May miss names.") is honest and protects trust when the feature fails exactly as warned.

Teach at the moment of need. A tour on first launch is forgotten. A tip that appears the first time a user selects text ("Tip: press ⌘J to rewrite this") arrives when it is useful.

Personality: less is more

In 1966 Joseph Weizenbaum built ELIZA, a simple program that mirrored users' statements back as questions. He was disturbed by how quickly people attributed understanding and empathy to it; this became known as the ELIZA effect. Modern models are vastly more fluent, so the effect is much stronger. Human names, faces, "I feel", exclamation marks and fake typing pauses all push people to treat a probabilistic tool as a person who understands them. That raises trust beyond what the system has earned and can encourage emotional dependence.

This does not mean AI text must be robotic. It means: write like a good tool (see Words), not like a friend. Plain, polite, brief, first person only where it helps clarity ("I couldn't find a date in this note"), no claims of feelings, no flattery.

Do

  • Use prompt starters drawn from the user's own content.
  • Write one honest line about limits near the input.
  • Teach features at the moment of need.
  • Keep the voice of AI text consistent with the rest of your product.

Don't

  • Promise "anything" or "everything".
  • Give a utility feature a human name and face.
  • Fake typing delays or feelings to seem friendly.
  • Rely on a first-run tour to explain scope.

In the wild

  • ChatGPT, ClaudeOpenAI, Anthropic
    Both show a short note near the input that the assistant can make mistakes and that important information should be checked. A permanent, quiet limit statement beats a one-time disclaimer dialog.
  • PerplexityPerplexity AI
    The empty home screen offers example questions and the answer layout itself (sources first) teaches that this is a search tool, not an oracle. Layout sets expectations as strongly as copy.
  • ClippyMicrosoft Office, 1997
    The animated paperclip assistant interrupted with guesses about what users were doing and became a cautionary tale about intrusive, over-personified help. Personality multiplies the annoyance of being wrong.

Common mistakes

Generic starters

"Write a poem about the ocean" is easy to write and demos well.

Write starters that solve real jobs in your product, ideally filled with the user's own data.

Hiding limits for marketing

Limits feel like admitting weakness.

Stated limits turn failures into "as expected" instead of "broken". Users forgive what they were warned about.

Check yourself

Your journaling app's AI reflects back themes from entries. In tests, users start confiding in it and asking it for life advice. What should you change first?

The behavior shows the mental model has drifted from "tool that finds themes" to "confidant". Fix the cues that caused the drift, clarify scope in place, and route sensitive topics responsibly. A one-time dialog does not change ongoing perception.

Sources, citations and explanations

Taught by Perplexity and NotebookLM, with Google PAIR (explainability)

When an AI states something, show where it came from. A citation turns a claim into something the user can verify in one click, and grounding answers in the user's own data reduces hallucination at the source.

Language models can produce fluent text that is not supported by anything. The most effective design defence is grounding: give the model specific source material (the user's notes, a document, search results) and ask it to answer from that, then show the link between each claim and its source. This changes the user's task from "do I believe this?" to "does this source say that?", which is a question people can actually answer.

Anatomy of a good citation

Explanations: why did it do that?

For recommendations, rankings and classifications, the equivalent of a citation is a reason. Good reasons are short, specific and checkable: "Suggested because you saved 3 articles from this author." Bad reasons are vague and unfalsifiable: "Based on your interests." PAIR's advice is to explain enough for the user to act: to decide whether to trust, correct or ignore. You rarely need to explain how the model works internally; you need to explain which of the user's inputs mattered.

Watch out

Citations can be wrong too: a model can attach a real source to a claim the source does not make. If your feature cites, test citation accuracy separately in your evals (Chapter 16), and prefer showing quoted passages so mismatches are visible to the user.

Do

  • Ground answers in the user's own data or specified sources when possible.
  • Place citation markers at the claim level.
  • Show the quoted passage on hover or tap, with location and date.
  • Label claims that have no source.
  • Write reasons that name the user's inputs.

Don't

  • Dump a list of links at the end and call it citations.
  • Mix grounded and ungrounded statements without distinction.
  • Explain with "based on your preferences".
  • Assume citations are accurate without testing.

In the wild

  • PerplexityPerplexity AI, 2022 onward
    Numbered inline citations with source cards shown above or beside the answer, making sources part of the layout rather than a footnote. Citations as primary UI, not fine print.
  • NotebookLMGoogle, 2023 onward
    Answers questions only from documents the user uploads, with citations that jump to the passage in the source. Limiting the model to the user's sources is both a trust feature and a scope statement.
  • Readwise Reader GhostreaderReadwise, 2023
    AI actions in a reading app work on the document or passage the user is reading, keeping the source text right beside the output. When the source is on screen, verification costs a glance.

Common mistakes

Source-washing

Adding links makes answers look trustworthy, so you add them even when the model did not use them.

Only cite what the answer was actually generated from, and test that each citation supports its claim.

Citations that open the top of a 40-page PDF

Deep linking is extra work.

Store the passage location when you retrieve it, and open the source scrolled and highlighted at that spot.

Check yourself

A user asks your notes app "When is Rohan's visa interview?" The AI finds two notes with different dates. What is the best answer design?

Conflicting sources are information. Showing both with dates and links lets the user resolve it in seconds. Picking one silently hides the conflict; refusing wastes the work the system already did.

Showing confidence and uncertainty

Taught by Apple HIG (confidence), with Microsoft G2 and Edward Tufte

Uncertainty is useful information, but raw percentages rarely help. Show confidence in a form that changes what the user does: highlight the parts that need checking, ask for confirmation below a threshold, or offer alternatives.

Many models and pipelines can give you some signal of how sure they are: probability scores from a classifier, agreement between several samples, whether a retrieved source was found, or whether a parsed field passed validation. The temptation is to print the number ("87% confident"). But most people cannot use "87%": is that good? Should they check? Research on numeric probability displays shows people interpret them inconsistently. The useful question is not "how confident is the model?" but "what should the user do differently because of it?"

Five ways to show it

  1. Nothing. Fine when errors are cheap and obvious (ghost text).
  2. A number. Use for experts who understand calibration (radiologists, analysts), and only if the number is actually calibrated: an "80%" must be right about 80% of the time.
  3. Words or levels. High, medium, low, with a defined behaviour for each. Easier to act on than numbers.
  4. Highlight the uncertain parts. Mark only the fields or phrases below a threshold. Directs attention exactly where checking is needed.
  5. Alternatives. Show the top two or three candidates when the model is torn ("Starbucks or Starbucks Reserve?"). Turns uncertainty into a quick choice.
Lab: one receipt, five confidence displays

Notice that "Percent" gives you four numbers and no instruction, while "Highlight" and "Alternatives" tell you exactly where to look. Raise the threshold and more fields ask for a check: that is the dial between speed and safety.

Do

  • Decide what the user should do at each confidence level, then design that behavior.
  • Highlight only what is below the threshold.
  • Offer alternatives when the model is torn between a few options.
  • Check calibration before showing any number.

Don't

  • Print raw percentages for general audiences.
  • Mark everything as uncertain to be safe; it teaches users to ignore the marks.
  • Use colour alone for uncertainty (see Accessible & Inclusive).
  • Show high confidence on outputs you have never measured.

In the wild

    For some words and phrases it shows alternative translations you can pick, especially where a term is ambiguous or gendered. Alternatives make uncertainty actionable in one tap.
  • Apple Photos search and Live TextApple

  • Bank and expense appsvarious

Common mistakes

Asking the model how sure it is

It is the easiest signal to get: just ask for a score in the prompt.

Treat self-reported scores as weak. Prefer signals you can verify: validation (does the date parse?), agreement across two runs, whether a source was found.

Threshold set once and forgotten

You pick 70% by feel during development.

Tune the threshold on your eval set: find the point where flagged fields catch most errors without flagging most correct values.

Check yourself

The underline is quiet enough for skimming and tells the user exactly where to look; tap-to-hear gives a way to verify. An overall score does not tell you which part to check.

Preview, diffs, editing and undo

Taught by Cursor and GitHub (diffs), with Ben Shneiderman (direct manipulation) and Microsoft G9

Never let AI change the user's work invisibly. Show what will change before it happens, let the user accept or reject each change, make the result editable like anything else, and keep one-step undo for whatever was applied.

Shneiderman's principles of direct manipulation from the 1980s still hold: visible objects, rapid reversible actions, and immediate feedback. AI edits threaten all three. A rewrite can change forty things at once, invisibly, in a way that feels hard to reverse. The diff is the antidote. Programmers have reviewed changes as diffs for decades; the same idea works for prose, data and settings. A diff turns "trust the whole output" into "review a few specific changes", which is a task people are good at.

The review ladder

Match the review tool to the size and risk of the change.

Make output editable, not just acceptable

If it writes a paragraph, the paragraph is normal text. If it builds a filter, the filter shows up in the filter UI. Every applied AI change should be one undo step (not twenty), labeled in the undo history ("Undo AI rewrite"). A simple "1 of 3 ‹ ›" switcher is enough. See Structure & Interaction for undo fundamentals.

Do

  • Show AI edits as diffs with per-change keep and undo.
  • Render AI output in your normal, editable components.
  • Make each applied AI action a single, labeled undo step.
  • Keep previous generations reachable after regenerate.

Don't

  • Replace user content silently.
  • Force correction through re-prompting.
  • Throw away the previous output on regenerate.
  • Split one AI change into many undo steps.

Designing for latency

Taught by Jakob Nielsen (response times), with Chris Harrison (progress perception) and every streaming chat UI

AI is slow, so design the wait. Show something useful fast (stream text, show steps), let people stop or leave, and move long tasks to the background with a notification when they finish.

Three numbers from decades of response-time research still guide the design: around 0.1 seconds feels instant, around 1 second keeps the user's flow of thought, and around 10 seconds is the limit of attention before people switch tasks. Most AI calls land between 1 and 30 seconds, right in the zone where waiting hurts. You usually cannot make the model faster, but you can change what the wait feels like and what the user can do during it. See Motion & Feel for the broader topic of perceived speed.

Time to first useful thing

The single most effective technique is streaming: show output token by token as it is generated. The total time is the same, but the user sees words within a second, starts reading, and judges early whether the answer is going well. The metric to design around is not total time but time to first useful content.

Progress for multi-step work

When a task has phases (search, read, analyze, write), name them as they happen. Do

  • Stream text output.
  • Name real steps for multi-step tasks.
  • Provide Stop for any wait over a couple of seconds, keeping partial output.
  • Move long tasks to the background with a notification.
  • Prefetch or precompute cheaply when you can predict the request.

Don't

  • Block the whole interface with a spinner.
  • Fake progress with a bar that is not tied to real work.
  • Use slow models for patterns that need instant feedback.
  • Lose the work if the user navigates away.

Errors, failure and feedback

Taught by Google PAIR (errors and graceful failure), with Microsoft G9, G10 and G15

AI fails in more ways than ordinary software, and some failures look like success. Name the failure types, design a recovery path for each, and make feedback specific enough that you can actually learn from it.

A taxonomy of AI failure

Graceful degradation

When the AI part fails, the product should still work. If smart search times out, fall back to keyword search and say so in a small note. If the model provider is down, the editor still edits; only the AI actions grey out with a short reason. Never lose user input because a model call failed: keep the prompt, the selection and any partial output so retry is one click. This is Microsoft's G10 (scope services when in doubt) in practice: do less, honestly, rather than fail completely.

Refusals that respect the user

Models sometimes decline requests, occasionally for good reasons and sometimes by mistake. A bare "I can't help with that" is a dead end and feels like an accusation. A good refusal says briefly what it cannot do, why if it can, and what it can do instead. If your product's own policy limits a feature (for example, no generating images of real people), state the policy in plain words and link to it.

Feedback that teaches you something

The thumbs up and down under AI answers is everywhere, and on its own it is nearly useless to a small team: you learn that something was bad, not what or why. Microsoft's G15 asks for granular feedback. Follow a thumbs-down with one tap of structured reasons (wrong facts, too long, wrong tone, did not follow instructions, unsafe), an optional text box, and a clear note about what happens to the feedback. Make giving feedback also fix the user's problem: "Too long" should offer a shorter version right away. Feedback that helps the user now gets given far more often than feedback that only helps you later.

Regenerate and report

Make it obvious, keep earlier versions (Chapter 11), and consider "regenerate with…" options (shorter, more formal, with sources) so the second try is directed rather than random. Do

  • Fall back to non-AI behavior when the model fails.
  • Keep the user's input on every error.
  • Follow thumbs-down with one tap of reasons that also offer a fix.
  • Say what feedback is stored and how it is used.

Don't

  • Show "Something went wrong" for AI failures.
  • Refuse without saying what is possible instead.
  • Collect feedback you never read.
  • Silently use feedback text containing user content for training without consent.

Personalization, memory and privacy

Taught by Microsoft G12, G13, G17, with Apple HIG (implicit feedback) and Helen Nissenbaum (contextual integrity)

An AI that remembers can be far more useful and far more unsettling. Make memory visible, editable and optional; tell people what data goes where in plain words; and never surprise them with what the system knows.

Personalization ranges from small (remembering your preferred summary length) to deep (a long-term memory of facts about your life used across conversations). The usefulness grows with depth, and so does the risk of feeling watched. The philosopher Helen Nissenbaum's idea of contextual integrity is a good test: information flows feel acceptable when they match the norms of the context where the information was shared. A user telling your notes app about a medical appointment expects it to help schedule the appointment, not to tailor ads or bring it up in an unrelated chat with a work colleague's document open.

Four principles for memory

  1. Visible. When the system uses something it remembered, it says so ("Using your preference: short summaries"). There is a page listing what it remembers.
  2. Editable. Users can correct, delete or add memories individually, not only "clear all".
  3. Optional. Memory can be turned off globally (G17) and paused for a session (an incognito-style mode).
  4. Scoped. Memories stay in the context they came from unless the user says otherwise. Work stays in work, personal stays personal.

Data use disclosure

Users increasingly ask three questions about any AI feature: Where does my data go? Is it used to train models? How long is it kept? Answer all three in plain words near the feature, not only in a privacy policy. If you use a third-party model provider, say so, and say what their terms are for your API usage. If content is sent off-device, consider a quiet indicator. Where possible, process sensitive data on device or offer a mode that does. See Honest & Calm for privacy as a design value.

Do

  • Show when memory or personal data was used.
  • Provide a list of memories with edit, delete and add.
  • Ask before saving inferred facts that are sensitive.
  • Answer "where, training, how long" next to the feature.
  • Keep memories within their context by default.

Don't

  • Infer sensitive traits (health, religion, finances) silently.
  • Offer only "clear all" as control.
  • Bury data use in a policy page.
  • Use user content to train models without clear, opt-in consent.

In the wild

  • ChatGPT memoryOpenAI, 2024 onward
    Memory can be viewed and individual items deleted in settings, turned off, and avoided with a temporary chat mode; the interface notes when a memory is updated. Visible, editable, optional, with a pause mode.
  • Apple IntelligenceApple, 2024
    Apple emphasizes on-device processing and describes a Private Cloud Compute system for larger requests, and asks before sending a request to an external model. Where data goes is part of the product design and the product story.
  • Netflix and Spotify profileslong-running
    Separate profiles and ways to remove items from viewing or listening history let people keep one person's or one mood's behavior from shaping another's recommendations. Scoping personalization is an old problem with known solutions.

Common mistakes

Creepy accuracy

The system infers something true that the user never said, and shows off.

Use inferences quietly for ranking if at all, and ask before turning them into stated memories.

Memory that cannot be found

Memory is built as a backend feature without UI.

Ship the memory list on the same day as memory. If you cannot show it, you are not ready to store it.

Check yourself

Your writing app could remember each user's style from their past documents. What is the best first version?

A visible, editable profile from chosen documents makes personalization a feature the user controls. Silent learning risks surprises; a shared model uses other people's writing without consent.

Prompting as interface design

Taught by ChatGPT Canvas and Raycast, with Jakob Nielsen (the articulation barrier)

The prompt is part of your interface, whether you show it or not. Write the hidden instructions as carefully as UI copy, and give users controls (buttons, sliders, presets) for the things they would otherwise have to write prompts to ask for.

Every AI feature has two prompts: the one the user writes (if any) and the one you write, usually called the system prompt or instructions, which sets role, tone, format and rules. Jakob Nielsen has called the difficulty of writing good prompts an "articulation barrier": many people struggle to express what they want precisely in prose. Your design job is to lower that barrier by moving as much of the prompt as possible into your instructions and into controls.

Hidden versus exposed prompts

Hidden prompts keep the interface clean and let you encode expertise ("write at a grade 8 reading level, keep the user's terminology, never invent numbers"). Exposed prompts let power users customize and learn. A good middle path: hide the prompt by default, offer presets as buttons, and let advanced users view or edit the instruction behind a preset, as Raycast does with its editable AI commands.

Taught by Hamel Husain (evals), with Google PAIR (defining success)

  
You cannot design an AI feature you have not seen fail. Build a small set of real test inputs, judge the outputs against criteria you write down, look at them with your own eyes regularly, and price every feature before you ship it.

  
In ordinary software, a test either passes or fails. AI output is graded, varied and context-dependent, so you need a different habit: evaluation, or "evals". Big teams build elaborate pipelines; as a designer you need only the core. Ethics: bias, disclosure, dependence and consent

  
Taught by Kathy Sierra, with Joseph Weizenbaum, Lisanne Bainbridge and Microsoft G6

  
An AI feature is a set of choices about what the system says, to whom, and in whose name. Design so people know when they are dealing with AI, are treated fairly by it, keep control of their own data and voice, and come away more capable rather than more dependent.


  
Bias

  
Models learn from human text and images, so they carry human patterns, including unfair ones: defaulting to one gender for "doctor", rating some names or dialects as less professional, generating images that reflect narrow beauty or cultural norms. Microsoft's G6 asks you to mitigate social biases. You cannot fix the model, but you can test for bias in your own feature: add eval cases that vary names, genders, dialects (including Indian English and Hinglish), and regions, and check that outputs do not change in ways they should not. Avoid features that judge people (hiring screens, "professionalism" scores) unless you can measure fairness seriously.


  
Disclosure

  
People have a right to know when content was generated and when they are talking to a machine. Label AI-generated text, images and voices where they appear, especially when they might be shared beyond your product. For images, support provenance standards such as C2PA Content Credentials where your tools allow. Never let an AI pose as a human in support, sales or social contexts. Regulation is moving in this direction too; the EU AI Act, for example, includes transparency obligations for AI systems that interact with people and for some generated content. Treat disclosure as a design default rather than a legal checkbox.


  

    
    
  


  
Dependence and deskilling

  
Bainbridge's ironies apply to everyday software: when a tool does a task for you, your own skill at it can fade, and you become worse at catching the tool's mistakes. For some tasks that is fine (few people miss doing long division). For others, such as writing, reasoning, learning a language or coding while you are still learning, the skill is the point. Kathy Sierra's Badass: Making Users Awesome (2015) argues that great products are judged by what users become able to do, not by how much users like the product itself. Applied to AI: design features that coach as well as do.


  

  
Consent

  
Ask before using someone's content to train or improve models, and make "no" easy and free of penalty. Ask before cloning a voice, using a person's likeness, or processing someone else's data that the user uploads (a colleague's messages, a client's documents). Do not use AI to manufacture urgency, fake reviews, or personalized manipulation; those are dark patterns with a model behind them (see Honest & Calm).


  

    
Do
  • Add bias checks (varied names, dialects, regions) to your eval set.
  • Label AI-generated content and AI agents clearly.
  • Offer a human path where stakes are high.
  • Design learning-oriented features to explain, not only to do.
  • Ask clear, opt-in consent for training on user data.

    
Don't
  • Give bots human names, photos or claimed feelings.
  • Generate content meant to pass as human-written in public contexts.
  • Score or rank people with a model you cannot audit.
  • Make opting out of training cost features.

    An open standard for attaching provenance information to media, including whether AI tools were used; Adobe attaches it to Firefly-generated images. Disclosure can travel with the file, not just sit in your UI.
    • 
    
  • DuolingoDuolingo
    Added AI features such as explanations of why an answer was wrong, in a product whose whole purpose is building the user's own skill. In learning products, AI should explain, not answer for you.
    •

    • 
  


  
Common mistakes

    
"The model did it"
It feels like the AI's output is not your speech.
Treat every AI output as words your product says. If you would not write it in your UI, constrain the AI from saying it.

    
Bias testing only in English
Your test inputs reflect your own language habits.
Include the languages, dialects and names your real users have, and check for differences in quality and tone.

  


  

    
Check yourself

    
You are adding AI to a writing app for students. Which design best follows the "make users more capable" principle?

    

    
For students, the skill is the goal. Explaining and prompting an attempt builds capability; generating or silently fixing replaces it. Removing AI entirely gives up a real chance to coach.

Designing with AI as a solo creator

  
Taught by your own practice, with Ethan Mollick and the double diamond

  
AI is a superb assistant for the wide parts of design work: gathering, drafting, prototyping, generating options, critiquing. Keep the narrow parts human: choosing, judging, verifying, and deciding what the product stands for. AI widens; you narrow.

  
For a solo creator, AI is like hiring a fast, tireless junior who has read everything and experienced nothing. It can draft forty headlines, sketch three prototypes in an afternoon, summarize twenty interview transcripts, and critique your layout against heuristics. It cannot know what your users felt, which of the forty headlines is true to your product, or when something is technically fine but soulless. Ethan Mollick's Co-Intelligence (2024) suggests treating it as a collaborator you always check, which is the same calibrated trust you design for your users.

The sameness problem

  
Generative tools produce the most likely output given their training. Ask for "a modern SaaS landing page" and you get the average of every modern SaaS landing page: a gradient hero, three feature cards, a pricing table and testimonials. The products Rohit admires (iA Writer, Things, Kindle) are memorable because they made unlikely choices with conviction. Use AI to get past the obvious quickly, then push away from it: give it your constraints and references, ask for options that break a convention, and treat the first output as the thing to avoid.


  

    
    
  


  
For the solo creator
A weekly rhythm that works: Monday, use AI to synthesize last week's feedback and support emails (verify quotes). Tuesday to Thursday, prototype in code with AI help, but design states and copy yourself. Friday, ask an AI for the harshest critique of what you built, then decide, alone, which criticisms matter. Keep a "taste log" of choices you made against the AI's suggestion and why; over months it becomes your design voice written down.


  

    
Do
  • Use AI to generate breadth: options, drafts, prototypes.
  • Give it your constraints, references and voice guide.
  • Verify every fact, quote and spec you publish.
  • Ask for objections and the strongest critique, not reassurance.
  • Keep final choices, and the reasons, yours.

    
Don't
  • Ship the first output.
  • Treat simulated users as research.
  • Let AI pick your aesthetic.
  • Publish AI-written claims you have not checked.

Common mistakes

    
Outsourcing taste
Asking "which design is better?" is easy and the answer sounds confident.
Ask for the trade-offs of each option, then decide yourself against your principles. Write the decision down.

    
Fluency mistaken for truth
Well-written text feels authoritative, especially about guidelines and specs.
For any fact you will rely on (a WCAG criterion, a platform size, an API limit), open the primary source.

    
Polished prototypes, unexamined problems
AI makes prototypes so quickly you skip the problem definition.
Write the problem and success criteria first (see Problems Worth Solving); then prototype.

  


  

    
Check yourself

    
You used AI to cluster 15 user interviews into themes. One theme, "users want dark mode", has 4 supporting quotes. What do you do before acting on it?

    

    
Synthesis tools can invent or distort quotes and over-generalize. Checking the originals takes minutes and protects you from building on fiction. Asking the model or re-running does not verify anything.

  


  

    
Exercise 18.1 90 minutes · on your own product

    
Widen with AI, narrow by hand

    
  1. Pick one screen of your product that you are unhappy with.
  2. Give an AI your constraints (users, principles, references, voice) and ask for 10 genuinely different directions, including two that break a convention.
  3. Discard the 5 most generic. Combine or refine the rest into 2 directions by hand.
  4. Ask the AI for the strongest critique of each; decide which criticisms are valid.
  5. Write a 5-line taste log: what you chose, against what, and why.
